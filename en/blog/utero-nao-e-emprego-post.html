A Uterus Is Not a Job: Feline Reproductive Biology and Ethical Breeding

There is a slogan that circulates frequently among critics of purebred cat breeding: "a uterus is not a job." The phrase sounds like a defense of animal welfare — and, in fact, it contains an important truth. The problem arises when it is used indiscriminately to attack all breeding, without distinguishing between an ethical cattery and predatory commerce. To engage with this debate seriously, we must start with the biology.

The Feline Estrous Cycle: A Reproductive Machine on Permanent Alert

The domestic cat (Felis catus) is a seasonally polyestrous animal — meaning that during certain periods of the year, she enters heat repeatedly and almost continuously if not fertilized. Unlike many species, the cat is an induced ovulator: ovulation does not occur spontaneously, but is triggered by the mechanical stimulus of copulation. This mechanism has a direct and rarely discussed consequence: in the absence of fertilization, the body restarts the cycle within days.

In free-roaming or abandoned conditions, a queen can enter heat every 14 to 21 days during the breeding season, which in Brazil — a low-latitude country — can extend throughout practically the entire year due to daylight duration. Each cycle lasts between 4 and 10 days. Feline gestation spans approximately 63 to 67 days, and return to heat can occur as early as 4 to 6 weeks after birth, even during lactation.

From a physiological standpoint, this means an uncontrolled queen can theoretically produce between 3 and 5 litters per year. Each pregnancy represents an intense metabolic demand: caloric needs increase by up to 50% during lactation, calcium reserves are mobilized, oxidative stress increases, and progressive joint wear accumulates. Continuous repetition of this cycle without adequate recovery seriously compromises the female's health in the medium and long term.

The Real Risks of Unrestricted Reproduction

Veterinary literature clearly documents the effects of reproductive overexploitation in felines. Key risks include:

Puerperal hypocalcemia (eclampsia): a critical drop in serum calcium levels during lactation, especially with large litters. It can cause seizures and be fatal if not treated urgently.

Metritis and pyometra: uterine infections common in females with a history of multiple pregnancies. Pyometra in particular is a surgical emergency with high mortality rates if not diagnosed in time.

Maternal malnutrition: queens nursing large litters without adequate nutritional support lose muscle mass and immune reserves, becoming more susceptible to infections and parasites.

Neonatal compromise: litters from exhausted females tend to show lower birth weights, higher neonatal mortality, and deficient immune development.

These data make it clear that unrestricted reproduction is not merely an ethical issue — it is a documented and measurable animal health issue.

What Makes an Ethical Cattery

The phrase "a uterus is not a job" carries its most important truth precisely here: it accurately describes what happens in so-called backyard breeders (BYB) and low-standard commercial catteries, where females are bred at every available heat cycle with no respect for the body's recovery time.

An ethical cattery operates in a radically different way. The major international feline registration organizations, such as TICA (The International Cat Association) and FIFé (Fédération Internationale Féline), establish guidelines that limit the number of litters per female per period. In practice, serious breeders follow a protocol of no more than 3 litters in a 2-year period per breeding female — and are often even more conservative.

During heat cycles when the female is not bred, responsible management includes strict isolation from males, behavioral monitoring, and in some cases veterinary protocols to manage the heat cycle without compromising future reproductive capacity. The biology is controlled — not exploited.

Beyond reproductive control, an ethical cattery also involves:

Genetic screening: testing for hereditary diseases prevalent in the breed (such as PKD in Persians, HCM in Maine Coons and Ragdolls, PK-def in Abyssinians) before any mating.

High-standard nutrition: specific diets for pregnant and lactating queens, with supplementation when necessary, based on veterinary assessment.

Continuous veterinary follow-up: pre-breeding exams, ultrasound gestational monitoring, and post-birth support for both mother and kittens.

Proper socialization: kittens raised in a domestic environment with early sensory stimulation and human contact from the first weeks show significantly more stable behavioral profiles in adulthood.

The Real Cost of Excellence

Ethical breeding of pedigreed cats is, by no reasonable measure, an easy or highly profitable activity. Importing international bloodlines involves significant costs in logistics, quarantine, and import taxes. Genetic and clinical exams are recurring expenses. High-standard nutrition is a constant cost. The time devoted to socializing kittens has no market price — it is artisanal, vocational work.

When someone pays a fair price for a kitten from an ethical cattery, they are not paying for the "product" itself. They are funding a system that respects the mother's health, guarantees the breed's genetic integrity, and delivers a healthy, well-socialized animal with a documented medical history.

Adoption and Breeding: A False Dichotomy

Ethical breeding and responsible adoption are not opposing positions — they are complementary responses to different demands. Adopting a cat from a shelter or rescue organization is a generous act that saves a life. Seeking a pedigreed animal with health guarantees and predictable temperament is a legitimate choice, especially when life circumstances — young children, allergies, limited living space — make certain breed characteristics relevant to the well-being of the entire family.

The real problem lies not with those who breed responsibly, nor with those who purchase conscientiously. The problem lies with predatory breeders, pet shops that sell kittens of obscure origin, and breeders who genuinely do treat their females' uteruses as production lines. Those are the targets the phrase was made for.

Understanding the difference is the first step toward defending animal welfare with precision — and not merely with good intentions.
