Feline Claws: Much More Than Nails

Anyone who lives with cats is familiar with those small, curved, sharp structures that appear when a cat stretches, climbs, or decides to scratch the sofa. But feline claws are much more than defensive tools. They belong to a sophisticated anatomical system involved in movement, environmental exploration, communication, and natural feline behavior.

Although commonly called nails, cat claws have distinctive features. Their shape, retraction mechanism, and continuous renewal reflect millions of years of evolutionary adaptation.

Understanding how they work helps us recognize normal behavior, prevent injuries, and identify changes that deserve veterinary attention.

1. A precision structure

Claws are specialized structures composed primarily of keratin, a strong protein also found in hair and the nails of other mammals.

Each claw is associated with the distal phalanx, the final bone of the digit. Its outer keratinized covering protects living internal tissues, including blood vessels and nerves that support growth and sensation.

This sensitive internal region, commonly called the quick, must be avoided during trimming. Cutting into it causes pain and bleeding.

Another remarkable feature is the ability to keep the claws retracted during many everyday activities. Coordinated action involving muscles, tendons, and ligaments allows cats to extend their claws when climbing, gripping objects, or capturing prey.

This mechanism helps preserve sharp claw tips by limiting constant contact with the ground.

2. Why do cats scratch?

Scratching is a natural behavior with several functions. It does not necessarily indicate aggression, disobedience, or an intention to damage furniture.

One function is claw maintenance. As new keratin develops, older outer claw sheaths gradually separate. Friction against suitable surfaces helps remove these layers, exposing a newer surface underneath.

Therefore, the small translucent structures occasionally found near scratching posts may simply be discarded claw sheaths rather than entire claws that have been pulled out.

Scratching also stretches muscles and mobilizes joints in the forelimbs, particularly after periods of rest.

There is also a communication function. Scratching leaves visible marks and may deposit chemical signals from glands located in the paws. These signals contribute to territorial and social communication.

Preventing scratching altogether would conflict with normal feline behavioral needs. The appropriate approach is to provide suitable surfaces where cats can express this behavior.

3. Should you trim your cat's claws?

This is one of the most common questions among cat caregivers. The answer depends on the individual cat, its age, mobility, environment, and claw condition.

Young, active cats that regularly use scratching posts may shed their outer claw sheaths efficiently. However, this does not necessarily mean their claw tips will be short. Scratching helps keep claws functional and sharp; it is not equivalent to filing them down continuously.

For indoor-only cats, periodic trimming may help prevent claws from catching on fabrics, causing accidental scratches, or becoming excessively long.

A practical approach is to inspect the claws every 10 to 14 days, particularly in apartment-dwelling cats. This is a suggested monitoring interval, not a scientifically established universal trimming schedule.

Some cats need more frequent trimming, while others require it less often. Decisions should be based on claw length, curvature, and the risk of the claw growing toward the paw pad.

4. How can claws be trimmed safely?

Safe trimming requires gentle handling, adequate lighting, and clippers designed for cats.

When the cat is relaxed, gentle pressure on the digit can expose the claw. In pale claws, the vascularized region is often visible as a pink area near the base.

Only the free tip should be trimmed, leaving a safe margin from the sensitive quick. Dark claws require particular caution because the internal structures are more difficult to see.

There is no need to trim every claw in a single session. Short sessions with positive reinforcement and respect for the cat's tolerance are generally preferable to forceful restraint.

If the cat becomes highly distressed or the safe cutting area cannot be identified, a veterinary professional should demonstrate or perform the procedure.

Proper trimming should not cause pain. Bleeding or a strong pain response may indicate that sensitive tissue has been injured.

5. Do claws change with age?

Yes. Older cats may develop thicker, longer, or more layered claws.

Some of these changes are associated with reduced physical activity and less frequent behaviors that help remove older keratin sheaths.

Osteoarthritis is particularly important. This condition is common in older cats and may cause chronic pain, reducing jumping, climbing, playing, and scratching.

Obesity can also limit mobility and make certain movements more difficult, although it does not necessarily cause claw abnormalities in every affected cat.

A cat that stops using scratching posts, struggles to jump, or avoids particular postures may be showing signs of musculoskeletal discomfort.

In such cases, trimming addresses only part of the problem. The underlying reason for reduced mobility should also be investigated and treated.

6. What can claws reveal about health?

Claws may show signs of local disease and, occasionally, broader medical conditions.

Persistent changes in shape, thickness, color, or strength deserve attention, particularly when accompanied by pain, inflammation, or behavioral changes.

Potential problems include:

  • Traumatic injuries and claw fractures: may occur when claws become trapped in fabric or suffer an impact, causing pain and bleeding.
  • Paronychia: inflammation of the tissues surrounding the claw, potentially associated with trauma, infection, or other disease processes.
  • Bacterial or fungal infections: may affect tissues around the claw and, in certain circumstances, the claw structure itself.
  • Immune-mediated diseases: conditions such as pemphigus foliaceus can cause crusting, inflammation, and lesions around the claws and paw pads.
  • Abnormal claw growth: thickening and deformities may be associated with aging, reduced mobility, or pathological processes.

Appearance alone is not enough for a diagnosis. Brittle claws, for example, do not automatically indicate nutritional deficiency or fungal infection.

A veterinary examination, sometimes supported by laboratory testing, cytology, culture, or biopsy, may be necessary depending on the suspected condition.

7. When does a claw become a problem?

One important complication is excessive claw growth, especially in older cats and those with limited mobility.

Because claws are curved, continued growth may bring their tips progressively closer to the paw pads, the soft structures on the underside of the digits.

In advanced cases, a claw may penetrate a pad, creating a painful wound and increasing the risk of secondary infection.

The dewclaws on the front limbs deserve particular attention because they do not normally contact the ground during walking.

Limping, persistent paw licking, swelling, discharge, sensitivity to touch, and changes in gait are warning signs.

If a claw has penetrated a paw pad, do not simply pull it out. Veterinary treatment allows safe removal of the problematic portion, wound care, and management of possible complications.

8. Scratching posts are a necessity, not a luxury

Environmental enrichment is essential for prevention and feline welfare.

Vertical and horizontal scratching surfaces allow cats to express individual preferences. Materials such as sisal, cardboard, and wood may be suitable depending on texture, stability, and the cat's preferences.

Vertical scratching posts should be tall enough to allow a full-body stretch and stable enough not to tip over during use.

Placement matters as well. Cats often scratch in passageways, near resting areas, and in socially important parts of their environment.

Providing suitable options in these locations is more effective than punishing a cat for scratching furniture.

However, scratching posts do not replace regular claw inspection, particularly for senior cats or those with mobility problems.

9. Is permanent claw removal a solution?

No. Surgical claw removal, known as onychectomy or declawing, is not simply a permanent nail trim.

The procedure involves amputating the distal phalanx of each affected digit, removing bone and associated tissues.

Because of its potential to cause pain and other complications, elective declawing for human convenience is strongly opposed by many animal welfare and veterinary organizations.

Appropriate scratching surfaces, environmental management, temporary furniture protection, and careful claw trimming should be prioritized instead.

10. Small details, significant consequences

Claws provide a direct connection between feline anatomy, behavior, and health.

They allow cats to climb, grip, stretch, mark surfaces, and explore their surroundings. At the same time, subtle abnormalities may reveal mobility difficulties, injuries, or diseases requiring medical attention.

Regularly examining the paws, providing suitable scratching surfaces, and seeking veterinary advice when abnormalities arise are simple actions that support comfort and quality of life.

The next time your cat extends its claws, take a closer look. What appears to be just a nail is part of an extraordinary system of feline adaptation.
