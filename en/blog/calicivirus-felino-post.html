Feline Calicivirus: When a Mouth Sore Reveals More Than a Cold

A small sore on a cat’s tongue can easily go unnoticed. When it is accompanied by drooling, sneezing, fever or refusal to eat, however, it may indicate an infection that deserves attention: feline calicivirus.

Commonly known as FCV, feline calicivirus is a highly contagious virus and one of the major infectious agents associated with respiratory disease in cats. It can cause anything from mild, self-limiting illness to conditions requiring hospitalization. A rare presentation, known as FCV-associated virulent systemic disease, can affect several organs.

What is feline calicivirus?

FCV is a virus that infects felids and has considerable genetic diversity. This means that many different variants circulate among cat populations. Some are mainly associated with respiratory signs and mouth lesions, while others may cause lameness or more severe disease.

It is part of the feline respiratory disease complex, sometimes informally described as “cat flu.” This expression, however, encompasses different infectious agents. Feline herpesvirus type 1 and certain bacteria can also be involved.

FCV does not infect people and is not considered a threat to dogs. Transmission occurs between felids.

Oral ulcers are an important sign

One of the most characteristic manifestations of FCV infection is the development of painful ulcers on the tongue. Lesions may also occur on the gums, lips, palate and other areas of the oral cavity.

Pain can cause a cat to drool, approach food and then back away, have difficulty chewing or show discomfort while swallowing. A reduced sense of smell caused by nasal congestion may further decrease interest in food.

Although oral ulcers are strongly associated with FCV, they cannot confirm the infection on their own. Trauma, dental disease, immune-mediated disorders, kidney disease, irritating substances and other infections may also cause sores in a cat’s mouth. Diagnosis depends on the complete clinical picture and the animal’s history.

What are the other symptoms?

Clinical presentation varies according to the viral variant, the cat’s age, immunity, vaccination status and general health. Possible signs include:

  • sneezing and nasal congestion;
  • eye and nasal discharge;
  • conjunctivitis;
  • fever and lethargy;
  • loss of appetite;
  • ulcers on the tongue and elsewhere in the mouth;
  • excessive drooling;
  • lameness or temporary difficulty walking;
  • pneumonia, particularly in young or vulnerable cats.

Not every cat develops every sign. Some have mild symptoms, while others require intensive veterinary support.

Why do some cats start limping?

Certain FCV variants are associated with a lameness syndrome, most frequently reported in young cats. Affected cats may develop fever, joint pain and difficulty bearing weight on one or more limbs.

The lameness is often temporary, but a cat that suddenly starts limping should be examined. Trauma, falls, orthopedic conditions, neurological disease and other sources of pain must also be considered.

How is FCV transmitted?

The virus is found primarily in saliva and in secretions from the eyes and nose. Transmission may occur through direct contact between cats or indirectly through contaminated objects and surfaces, including:

  • food and water bowls;
  • carriers;
  • toys and equipment;
  • bedding and fabrics;
  • the hands and clothing of people handling different cats.

Control can therefore be particularly challenging in shelters, veterinary hospitals, breeding facilities and multicat households. FCV may also remain infectious in the environment for some time and is not inactivated by every commonly used disinfectant.

When infection is suspected or confirmed, isolation and cleaning procedures should be directed by a veterinarian. Mixing cleaning products or improvising concentrations is unsafe because some combinations release toxic gases and may poison both people and animals.

The cat has recovered. Has transmission stopped?

Not necessarily. Following clinical recovery, some cats continue shedding the virus in their secretions for weeks, months or, in certain cases, even longer. They may appear completely healthy while still contributing to transmission.

Viral shedding may be intermittent. A single negative result does not therefore guarantee that the cat has permanently stopped shedding FCV.

Is there a more severe form?

Yes, although it is rare. FCV-associated virulent systemic disease may cause high fever, swelling of the face and limbs, skin lesions, breathing difficulties, jaundice and damage to internal organs.

Outbreaks of this presentation have been documented mainly in settings with a high concentration of cats, including veterinary hospitals and shelters. Its severity is considerably greater than that of the usual respiratory presentation.

This uncommon disease should not be confused with the more frequent course of FCV infection. Most infected cats do not develop virulent systemic disease.

How is FCV diagnosed?

The veterinarian begins with the cat’s clinical history, an examination of the mouth, respiratory assessment and evaluation of the cat’s general condition. The combination of tongue ulcers and respiratory signs may increase suspicion, but it does not replace a complete diagnostic assessment.

Molecular tests such as RT-PCR can detect FCV genetic material in samples collected from the mouth, conjunctiva or nasal region. Results must, however, be interpreted alongside the clinical findings.

A healthy cat may test positive because it is shedding the virus without showing disease. Depending on the type of vaccine and the timing of sampling, recent vaccination may also complicate interpretation. Likewise, a negative result does not completely rule out infection, particularly when samples are collected late or contain only a small amount of virus.

Is there a specific treatment?

For most infections, there is no medication that simply eliminates calicivirus from the body. Treatment is mainly supportive and must be tailored to the individual patient.

Veterinary care may include:

  • fluid therapy;
  • safe control of pain and fever;
  • nutritional support;
  • careful cleaning of discharges;
  • nebulization or other prescribed measures to relieve congestion;
  • treatment of secondary bacterial infections;
  • hospitalization in severe cases.

Antibiotics do not eliminate FCV because antibiotics do not act against viruses. A veterinarian may prescribe them when a secondary bacterial infection is suspected or confirmed.

Giving human painkillers, anti-inflammatory drugs or decongestants is also dangerous. Several medications commonly used by people are toxic to cats.

When does appetite loss become an emergency?

A cat with a painful mouth or congested nose may stop eating very quickly. This should never be dismissed as fussiness. Inadequate food intake can cause dehydration and weakness and, particularly in overweight cats, increase the risk of hepatic lipidosis.

Refusal to eat, intense drooling, difficulty swallowing, oral pain, weakness or a marked reduction in food intake justify prompt contact with a veterinarian. Kittens, senior cats and animals with other medical conditions may deteriorate even faster.

Does vaccination prevent infection?

Vaccination against FCV is considered a core vaccine for all cats. It is generally administered as part of a combination vaccine that also protects against feline herpesvirus type 1 and feline panleukopenia virus. Additional components vary among products and countries.

Because FCV is genetically diverse, vaccination does not prevent every infection and cannot guarantee that a cat will never shed the virus. Its main benefit is reducing the likelihood and severity of clinical disease, particularly severe manifestations.

The vaccination protocol should be individualized according to the cat’s age, health, vaccination history, lifestyle and exposure risk. Vaccination should also be discussed with a veterinarian for cats that live exclusively indoors.

How can cats be protected in a multicat household?

In addition to appropriate vaccination, several measures can help reduce viral circulation:

  • temporarily separate the sick cat under veterinary guidance;
  • avoid sharing bowls, toys, carriers and equipment during isolation;
  • wash hands before and after handling each group of cats;
  • clean the healthy cats’ areas first and the isolation area last;
  • follow a veterinary cleaning and disinfection protocol appropriate for FCV;
  • reduce overcrowding and sources of stress;
  • keep every cat’s vaccination up to date.

Introducing a new cat into a multicat household should also be planned carefully. Veterinary assessment, gradual introduction and initial separation help control infectious diseases while reducing stress.

Which signs require prompt veterinary care?

Seek veterinary assistance promptly if the cat develops:

  • refusal or difficulty eating and drinking;
  • difficult, rapid or open-mouth breathing;
  • intense drooling or obvious mouth pain;
  • marked lethargy;
  • persistent fever;
  • dehydration;
  • swelling of the face or limbs;
  • yellow discoloration or skin lesions;
  • rapid deterioration in general condition.

A mouth sore alone cannot establish that a cat has FCV infection. It should not, however, be ignored. Recognizing the signs, seeking veterinary assessment and maintaining appropriate vaccination are ways of turning information into protection.
