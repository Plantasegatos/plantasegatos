FIV and FeLV: what every cat guardian should know 🐾

By Plantas & Gatos — Your trusted guide to feline care and behavior

Living with cats is a privilege, but it also calls for special attention to their health. Among the most discussed feline diseases are FIV (Feline Immunodeficiency Virus) and FeLV (Feline Leukemia Virus). Although different, both can significantly impact our furry friends’ well-being and require evidence-based care using up-to-date science.

Epidemiological Data in Brazil

Recent scientific research highlights the importance of these diseases nationally. Studies from Brazil’s Central-West show FIV prevalence (12.5%) higher than FeLV (4.5%) in domestic cats. In the Lisbon metropolitan area, similar data report frequencies of 10.5% for FIV and 11.2% for FeLV, with higher incidence in intact adult males.

What is FIV?

FIV, known as “Feline AIDS,” is caused by a retrovirus that progressively weakens the cat’s immune system, making them more vulnerable to opportunistic infections. Transmission usually occurs through deep bite wounds during fights, especially among unneutered males disputing territory or during mating. Vertical transmission (from mother to kittens) is possible but less common.

  • Not transmissible to humans or other species — it is a feline-specific virus
  • The incubation period can be long; some cats remain asymptomatic for years
  • Disease progression varies individually; good quality of life is possible with proper management

Stages of FIV Infection

  1. Acute phase: May cause mild fever and lymphadenopathy, often unnoticed
  2. Latency phase: Asymptomatic period that can last years, with the virus inactive
  3. Terminal phase: Marked immunodeficiency and secondary diseases

What is FeLV?

FeLV is an oncogenic retrovirus that causes immunosuppression, anemia, lymphomas, leukemias, and secondary infections. It transmits more efficiently than FIV, via close contact: mutual grooming, shared food and water bowls, fighting, nasal secretions, saliva, urine, and from mother to kittens during pregnancy or nursing.

  • More contagious than FIV due to multiple transmission routes
  • Can cause different syndromes: neoplastic, degenerative, immunosuppressive, and reproductive
  • Effective prevention exists through vaccination

Categories of FeLV Infection

  • Progressive infection: Continuous viral replication leading to clinical disease
  • Regressive infection: The immune system controls infection, but the virus remains latent
  • Localized infection: Limited to certain tissues, such as mammary glands

Diagnosis: Methods and Interpretation

Available Tests

ELISA/Snap (Rapid Test):

  • Detects FIV antibodies and FeLV antigens
  • Results in 10–15 minutes
  • High sensitivity and specificity for screening
  • May show false positives in kittens with maternal antibodies (FIV) or in very recent infections

PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction):

  • Directly detects viral genetic material
  • Greater accuracy to confirm doubtful cases
  • Ideal for evaluating kittens born to positive mothers
  • Allows differentiation between active infection and prior exposure

Recommended Diagnostic Protocol

  1. Initial test for every new cat before introduction into the home
  2. Retest after 60 days of quarantine to detect window-period infections
  3. Confirmatory testing with PCR in doubtful positives
  4. Periodic assessment yearly for cats with outdoor access

Vaccination: Prevention Strategies

WSAVA 2024 Guidelines

The World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA) updated its 2024 guidance, recommending FeLV vaccines be considered essential for cats under 1 year of age and for adult cats with outdoor access or living with cats that go outdoors.

  • First vaccination: Between 8–9 weeks of age
  • Second dose: 3–4 weeks after the first
  • Booster: Yearly for high-risk cats; every 3 years for low-risk indoor cats
  • Important prerequisite: Test for FeLV before vaccinating

Efficacy and Limitations

FeLV vaccination shows roughly 80–85% efficacy when done correctly. Note there is no FIV vaccine approved in Brazil, making environmental control crucial for prevention.

Living with Positive Cats

Clinical Management

  • Indoor housing to prevent transmission and pathogen exposure
  • Regular veterinary visits every 6 months, including complete blood counts
  • High-quality nutrition for optimal support
  • Strict parasite control (internal and external)
  • Careful vaccination with inactivated vaccines only (never live attenuated)

Health Monitoring

  • Early signs of secondary infections (changes in appetite, lethargy, fever)
  • Dental and gingival issues (more common in immunosuppressed cats)
  • Behavioral changes that may indicate pain or discomfort
  • Unexplained weight loss or changes in water intake

Nutritional Aspects and Supplementation

Specialized Nutritional Support

  • High-quality protein (at least 35% on a dry-matter basis)
  • Omega-3 fatty acids with anti-inflammatory properties
  • Antioxidants such as vitamins E and C, and selenium
  • Probiotics for gut health
  • Caloric control to avoid obesity

Therapeutic Supplementation

  • L-lysine: May help manage secondary herpesvirus infections
  • Recombinant feline interferon: Used in some countries for immune support
  • Lactoferrin: A protein with immunomodulatory properties

Prevention: Integrated Strategies

New Cat Introduction Protocol

  1. Initial isolation for 2–4 weeks
  2. ELISA/Snap test upon arrival
  3. Retest after 60–90 days
  4. Full clinical exam including ectoparasite check
  5. Vaccination only after confirmed FeLV-negative status

Environmental Strategies

  • Early neutering to reduce risk behaviors
  • Environmental enrichment to reduce stress
  • Population management in shelters via TNR
  • Tutor education on early clinical signs and the importance of veterinary follow-up

Future Perspectives and Research

Therapeutic Advances

  • Antiretroviral therapy adapted from human medicine
  • Immunotherapy to stimulate the feline immune system
  • Therapeutic vaccines under development
  • Regenerative medicine with stem cells

New Diagnostic Methods

  • More sensitive point-of-care tests
  • Genetic sequencing to characterize strains
  • Biomarkers to monitor disease progression

Socioeconomic Impact and Animal Welfare

Associated Costs

  • More frequent veterinary consultations
  • Regular lab testing
  • Medications for secondary infections
  • Specialized diets with higher cost

Benefits of Prevention

  • Annual vaccination (R$ 80–120)
  • Rapid FIV/FeLV test (R$ 60–100)
  • Neutering reduces risk behaviors

Management

Managing FIV and FeLV is one of the major challenges in contemporary feline medicine. Evidence-based approaches show it is possible to provide a satisfactory quality of life for cats affected by these retroviruses. Prevention through vaccination (for FeLV), regular testing, proper environmental management, and tutor education remains the most effective strategy.

For already diagnosed cats, specialized veterinary follow-up, appropriate nutrition, and specific home care can provide additional years of comfortable life.

Investing in prevention and early diagnosis benefits individual feline health and also helps control the population burden of these diseases — an ethical and scientific commitment to animal welfare.

💡 Professional tip: Keep an open dialogue with your veterinarian about testing, vaccination, and monitoring, considering age, lifestyle, and pre-existing conditions. Preventive medicine is the best investment in feline well-being.
