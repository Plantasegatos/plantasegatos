Where You Vaccinate Matters: FISS and Injection Sites in Cats

When we take a cat to be vaccinated, we naturally tend to focus on which vaccine will be given and when the next dose will be due. There is, however, another important and much less familiar question: where will that vaccine be administered?

In feline medicine, the injection site is not simply a matter of convenience or preference. It is part of a strategy designed to address a rare but potentially serious complication: Feline Injection-Site Sarcoma, or FISS.

What is FISS?

FISS is a malignant neoplasm that may develop in an area where a cat previously received an injection. For many years, the condition was commonly called a “vaccine-associated sarcoma.” We now know that this description is too restrictive.

Although vaccines are among the injections historically associated with these tumors, sarcomas have also been reported following other injectable products. This is why modern terminology focuses on the injection site rather than vaccination alone.

The leading hypothesis involves a persistent inflammatory response at the injection site. In susceptible cats, this inflammatory environment may contribute to cellular changes that eventually result in neoplastic transformation, particularly involving fibroblasts and myofibroblasts.

This does not mean that every inflammatory reaction will become a tumor, nor that vaccinating a cat will cause FISS. On the contrary, FISS is considered an infrequent and idiosyncratic event, and several aspects of its pathogenesis remain incompletely understood.

Why did a rare tumor change where cats are vaccinated?

The answer has less to do with how likely FISS is to occur and more to do with what may be necessary if it does occur.

These sarcomas can be highly locally invasive. Tumor cells may extend beyond what can be seen or felt externally and infiltrate surrounding tissues. Consequently, treatment may require very wide surgical margins and removal of deep fascial planes.

This is where anatomy becomes extremely important.

Consider a tumor located between the shoulder blades or in the middle of the back. There is a limited amount of tissue that can be removed while still allowing a viable surgical reconstruction. Obtaining adequate oncologic margins in this area can therefore be extremely difficult.

Now consider the same problem in the distal portion of a limb. In an extreme situation, limb amputation may provide surgical margins that would be considerably more difficult to achieve on the trunk.

This is why AAHA/AAFP guidelines recommend avoiding vaccination in the interscapular region and favor distal limb sites.

The more distal, the better

In anatomy, “distal” essentially means farther away from the center of the body.

Therefore, saying that a vaccine was administered “in the leg” is not enough. The recommendation is to administer it sufficiently below the major joints: distal to the elbow in the forelimbs and distal to the stifle in the hindlimbs.

The standardized scheme originally proposed by the AAFP and supported by the AAHA/AAFP guidelines uses:

  • Right forelimb, distal to the elbow: vaccines against feline panleukopenia virus, feline herpesvirus-1 and feline calicivirus — commonly grouped as FVRCP.
  • Left hindlimb, distal to the stifle: feline leukemia virus (FeLV) vaccine.
  • Right hindlimb, distal to the stifle: rabies vaccine.

This standardization provides two advantages. In addition to preserving surgical options, it can help identify which antigen had previously been administered if a sarcoma later develops — provided that the product, route and exact administration site were properly documented in the medical record.

What about the tail?

The distal tail has also been studied as an alternative vaccination site. When used, vaccination should be administered in the distal third — close to the tip — so that adequate surgical margins could still potentially be obtained if necessary.

Studies have reported acceptable tolerance and immune responses, although tail vaccination may not be practical for every clinician or patient. Its recognition as an alternative does not mean it should routinely replace distal limb vaccination.

What about the abdomen?

Subcutaneous injections in the ventral abdomen have also been described. The rationale is that a tumor in this location might potentially be removed without amputating a limb.

There is an important limitation, however. If wide oncologic margins and deep fascial planes must be removed, surgery may still require aggressive resection of the abdominal wall and potentially tissues within the abdominal cavity.

The ventral abdomen should therefore not be interpreted simply as a universally preferable alternative to distal limb sites.

The 3-2-1 rule: a lump appeared. What now?

Small local reactions can occur following vaccination, and finding a lump does not automatically mean cancer. What matters is knowing when a mass should no longer simply be observed and requires further investigation.

AAHA/AAFP guidelines provide a simple tool known as the 3-2-1 rule.

  • 3 months: is the mass still present three months after vaccination?
  • 2 centimeters: is it larger than 2 cm in diameter?
  • 1 month: is it still increasing in size one month after the injection?

If any one of these criteria is met, the guidelines recommend further investigation with an incisional biopsy.

This distinction matters. Fine-needle aspiration may fail to provide sufficient diagnostic cellular material in some of these tumors. At the same time, simply removing a small mass through an excisional biopsy may compromise subsequent oncologic surgery because the first surgical approach is particularly important in FISS management.

Should we be afraid of vaccines?

No.

This is perhaps the most important message of the entire discussion. FISS is a serious but infrequent complication. Vaccination, meanwhile, protects cats against infectious diseases capable of causing severe illness, permanent complications and death.

The same guidelines that discuss FISS extensively continue to recommend feline vaccination. Modern feline medicine uses an individualized risk-benefit assessment to determine which vaccines a cat needs according to age, history, lifestyle, environment and potential exposure.

Understanding FISS is therefore not an argument against vaccination. Quite the opposite: it is an argument for vaccination that is careful, traceable and appropriately performed.

Vaccinate, but do it right

Good feline vaccination practice involves several decisions: choosing the antigens that are actually needed, following appropriate intervals, selecting a suitable anatomical site and recording which product was used, by which route and exactly where it was administered.

Cat owners also have an important role: monitor the injection site afterwards and contact the veterinarian if a persistent or growing mass is detected.

So, at your cat's next appointment, asking “Where will this vaccine be administered?” is not a sign of distrust. It is an opportunity to discuss preventive medicine and better understand the care being provided to that patient.

Because in feline medicine, even a few centimeters can make an enormous difference.
