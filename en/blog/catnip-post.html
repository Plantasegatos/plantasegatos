Catnip: The Herb That Drives Cats Wild and Once Healed Humans

One Herb, Two Worlds

For a cat, catnip is an intense sensory experience — a temporary state of euphoria involving rolling, face-rubbing, vocalizing, and heightened playful behavior. For the humans who cultivated it over millennia, it was medicine, seasoning, sedative, and even an object of superstition. Nepeta cataria is one of the plants with the richest history at the intersection of felines and human civilization — and its biochemistry still surprises modern researchers.

Botany: Who Is Catnip?

Catnip belongs to the Lamiaceae family — the same family as mints, basils, and lavenders. It is a short-lived perennial herb with square stems typical of the family, oval leaves with serrated margins and a slightly velvety texture. It grows between 20 and 100 cm tall and produces, during summer, small tubular flowers ranging from white to pale lilac that attract pollinators. Native to Europe, western Asia, and North Africa, it was introduced to the Americas by European settlers in the 18th century and quickly naturalized across the continent.

The scientific name is revealing: Nepeta derives from the Etruscan city of Nepete (or Nepti), in west-central Italy, and cataria comes from the Latin catus, meaning cat. The plant was named after the two references that defined its historical identity: the human territory that cultivated it and the animal that transforms it into ritual.

History: From Greeks and Romans to the Middle Ages

Documented use of catnip dates back to Classical Antiquity. The Greek physician Dioscorides, in the 1st century AD, recorded the plant in his seminal work De Materia Medica, describing applications for insomnia, anxiety, and digestive disturbances. Dioscorides' work was the primary pharmacological compendium of the Western world for over fifteen centuries — and catnip appeared among its ingredients.

The Romans not only used the plant medicinally but also included it in culinary recipes: leaves and flowers added to salads and aromatic preparations were common in Roman domestic herb gardens. The city of Nepeta, in present-day Tuscany, became especially renowned for its extensive cultivation and use of the plant — so much so that it lent its name to the botanical genus. For the Romans, who had coexisted with domestic cats for centuries (heirs of Egyptian domestication), the interaction of felines with the herb in their gardens was likely not overlooked.

In the Middle Ages, catnip maintained its presence in European herbals and medical treatises. It was used as a tea to relieve fevers, cramps, coughs, and nervousness, especially in children. It also appeared in folk medicine practices related to fertility and protection, with records of use in "love potions" in some European regions — likely due to the symbolic association with cats, animals linked to medieval mysticism. It reached the Americas with British colonizers in the 18th century, where it quickly naturalized and was cultivated for both medicinal purposes and use with animals.

The Chemistry of the Feline Trance: Nepetalactone

The compound responsible for the cat's response to catnip is nepetalactone, a volatile iridoid produced in the glandular trichomes of the plant's leaves and stems. When a cat rubs, bites, or simply approaches catnip, nepetalactone is released and comes into contact with the animal's nasal mucosa.

The neurological cascade that follows is well mapped: nepetalactone binds to specific olfactory receptors in the nasal mucosa, activating sensory neurons that transmit the signal to the olfactory bulb — the frontal brain region responsible for processing smells. From there, the signal propagates to the amygdala (emotional responses) and the hypothalamus (instinctive behaviors). The result is an elevation in plasma levels of β-endorphins, which stimulate μ-opioid receptors in the central nervous system — the same system involved in states of pleasure and euphoria.

Studies using the opioid antagonist naloxone confirmed this mechanism: when administered prior to catnip exposure, naloxone completely blocks the behavioral response, demonstrating that the endogenous opioid system is indispensable for catnip's "high." The response does not pass through the vomeronasal organ (Jacobson's organ), as was once believed — it is entirely processed by the main olfactory system.

The duration of the effect is typically 5 to 15 minutes, followed by a refractory period of 30 minutes to 2 hours, during which the animal does not respond even if re-exposed. The compound is not addictive and causes no damage to the nervous system — the cat naturally "comes down" from the state without aftereffects.

Why Don't All Cats Respond?

Approximately 30% of adult domestic cats show no response to catnip — and the reason is genetic. Sensitivity to nepetalactone is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait: a single allele of the responsible gene is enough for the animal to respond. Those who inherit two copies of the recessive allele simply do not have the appropriate olfactory receptors to detect the compound effectively.

Kittens under 6 to 8 weeks of age and most tigers (Panthera tigris) also do not respond to catnip, for different reasons — in kittens, the olfactory system is still developing; in tigers, studies suggest that the response pattern differs among Felidae subfamilies. Adult lions, leopards, jaguars, and ocelots, on the other hand, typically respond to nepetalactone similarly to domestic cats.

Researchers from the Cat Health Network conducted genome-wide studies searching for the chromosomal region responsible for catnip sensitivity — to date, no significant genetic region has been precisely isolated, suggesting the inheritance may involve multiple loci or rare variants with large effects.

Catnip as an Insect Repellent — For the Plant and For the Cat

One of the most fascinating aspects of catnip is that nepetalactone did not evolve to fascinate cats. It evolved as the plant's chemical defense against herbivorous insects. Comparative studies show that nepetalactone repels mosquitoes with efficiency comparable to DEET (N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide), the benchmark synthetic repellent. The compound activates the TRPA1 receptor of insects — an irritant ion channel — causing them to move away from the plant.

Herein lies one of the most elegant discoveries in recent ethology: the behavior of rolling and rubbing the face in catnip, which appears to be pure pleasure, also has an adaptive function. By impregnating their fur with nepetalactone, cats effectively cover themselves with a natural mosquito repellent. Studies published in 2021 confirmed that cats exposed to catnip showed reduced attraction of mosquitoes (Aedes albopictus) following the anointing behavior. The plant rewards the cat with euphoria; the cat protects itself from bites. An elegant chemical symbiosis written over millions of years of co-evolution.

Therapeutic Properties for Humans

Although nepetalactone produces no psychoactive effects in humans — our neurochemistry simply lacks the appropriate receptors to process the compound in any meaningful way — catnip does possess relevant pharmacological properties for our species, primarily through oral administration.

Mild sedative: Catnip teas are used in herbal medicine to reduce anxiety and induce relaxation. The effect is attributed primarily to nepetalactol and other secondary compounds that act gently on the central nervous system.

Digestive and antispasmodic: Historically used to relieve cramps, gas, nausea, and intestinal spasms. Modern studies point to antispasmodic properties in gastrointestinal smooth muscle.

Antipyretic and diaphoretic: In European and North American folk medicine, catnip tea was used to reduce fever by inducing perspiration. Preliminary studies suggest modest anti-inflammatory activity.

Topical repellent: Catnip extracts have been studied as natural mosquito and fly repellents for human use, with promising laboratory results — although the volatility of nepetalactone requires specific formulations for effective topical application.

It is important to note that the medicinal human use of catnip lacks robust clinical trials, and pregnant women should avoid consumption, as the plant may stimulate uterine contractions. Recreational use through inhalation or herbal cigarettes, mistakenly popularized in the 1960s (when catnip was confused with cannabis in some samples), produces no euphoria and may cause discomfort.

Catnip Alternatives: When Your Cat Doesn't Respond

For the 30% of cats that do not react to catnip, science has identified effective alternatives. A study published in 2017 in BMC Veterinary Research (Bol et al.) tested four plants on 100 domestic cats and reached illuminating results:

Silvervine (Actinidia polygama): The most potent alternative. Native to the mountainous regions of China, Japan, and Korea, silvervine contains multiple active compounds — actinidine, dihydroactinidiolide, and nepetalactol — which explains its broader effectiveness. In the study, 79% of cats responded to silvervine versus 68% to catnip. Many cats that do not respond to catnip respond to silvervine. It has been widely used in Asia as feline enrichment for centuries and has entered the Western pet market in recent years.

Valerian (Valeriana officinalis): The active compound in valerian for cats is not valerian acid (responsible for sedative effects in humans), but rather iridoid compounds related to those found in catnip. About 47% of cats responded to valerian root in the study. The intense odor — which humans often describe as unpleasant — is particularly attractive to felines. It is a good option for catnip non-responders, especially in the form of toys stuffed with dried root.

Tatarian Honeysuckle (Lonicera tatarica): A shrub native to eastern Asia whose wood contains cat-attracting compounds. About 53% of cats responded. It is marketed in the form of sticks and wood shavings for biting and rubbing. Important: only the species L. tatarica has this property — other honeysuckle species lack the same effect and some may be toxic.

The study also concluded that combinations work: cats that do not respond to any single plant may respond to combinations. And silvervine in fruit gall form (fruit deformed by the wasp Pseudasphondylia matatabi) showed the highest concentration of active compounds and the highest response rate among all forms tested.

Catnip and Animal Welfare

From an environmental enrichment perspective, catnip and its substitutes are valuable and underutilized tools. Studies in behavioral veterinary medicine indicate that olfactory stimulation — frequently neglected in comparison with physical and cognitive enrichment — has great potential for reducing stress in captive cats, during transport, hospitalizations, and in shelters.

Cats periodically exposed to catnip or silvervine show greater play engagement, lower frequency of stereotypical behaviors, and better sleep quality. Exposure should not be daily — the refractory period and maintenance of novelty are important to prevent the effect from losing intensity. Two to three times per week is the frequency generally recommended by feline behavior specialists.

Growing Catnip at Home

Catnip is a relatively easy plant to grow, but requires some precautions in homes with cats. Young seedlings are vulnerable — adult cats may destroy them before they grow enough to recover. The ideal approach is to grow them in elevated or protected pots until the plant is well established. It prefers full sun to partial shade, well-drained soil, and moderate watering. It flowers twice if pruned after the first cycle. Harvesting should be done preferably during flowering, when nepetalactone concentration is at its peak. For drying, hang the branches upside down in a dark, well-ventilated location.
