Feline Hepatic Lipidosis: The Danger of Fasting in Cats

What is feline hepatic lipidosis?

Feline hepatic lipidosis — also known as fatty liver syndrome — is one of the most common liver diseases in cats. It occurs when there is a massive accumulation of fat (triglycerides) in more than 80% of the liver cells, the hepatocytes, severely compromising the organ's function.

The nearly universal trigger is anorexia: the cat goes 24, 48 hours or more without eating, or ingests far less than its daily caloric needs. What appears to be a simple refusal to eat can quickly escalate into a medical emergency.

How does the disease develop?

When a cat enters a negative energy balance, the body activates an emergency mechanism: it begins mobilizing large amounts of peripheral fat toward the liver in an attempt to obtain energy.

The problem is that the feline liver has a low capacity to oxidize fat and deficiencies in transport proteins, unlike what occurs in dogs and humans. Unable to process and export all that lipid efficiently, the liver becomes literally clogged with fat and begins to fail.

This is an evolutionary design flaw: cats are obligate carnivores, metabolically adapted to ingest animal protein every day. Prolonged fasting, therefore, is not merely undesirable — it is physiologically dangerous for the species.

Which cats are at risk?

Obese cats are the most vulnerable, as the amount of fat mobilized during fasting is proportionally greater. However, cats of any size can develop the disease, especially when there is an underlying primary condition causing loss of appetite.

The main risk factors include:

  • Obesity
  • Severe stress or sudden change of environment
  • Abrupt diet change without gradual transition
  • Hidden diseases such as pancreatitis, cholangitis, or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

Clinical signs: when to raise the alarm

Signs appear relatively quickly and tend to worsen as days go by. The most common are:

  • Jaundice — yellowing of the skin, mucous membranes, gums, and whites of the eyes
  • Rapid weight loss with visible reduction in muscle mass
  • Vomiting and nausea — often accompanied by excessive drooling
  • Extreme lethargy — the cat stops interacting, hides, and loses interest in its surroundings

Jaundice is the most alarming sign. Its presence indicates that the liver is already under significant stress and requires immediate veterinary care.

How is the diagnosis made?

Diagnosing hepatic lipidosis combines clinical history, physical examination, and complementary tests. The owner's account of the anorexia is a key piece: reporting how many days the cat has not been eating directly influences case management.

The most commonly used tests are:

  • Abdominal ultrasound: reveals an enlarged and hyperechoic liver with a characteristic fatty appearance
  • Serum biochemistry: marked elevation of ALP with normal or low GGT — the classic pattern of lipidosis, distinguishing it from other hepatopathies (Kam et al., 2024)
  • Hepatic aspiration (FNA or biopsy): confirms lipid accumulation in the cells

Treatment: assisted nutrition as the central pillar

The good news is that hepatic lipidosis is reversible — provided treatment is started early. The central pillar is assisted nutrition: since the cat experiences intense nausea and food aversion, waiting for it to eat spontaneously is not a viable option.

The veterinarian will place an esophageal or gastric feeding tube to provide caloric and protein support gradually and in a controlled manner. This process may take weeks, but it is essential for the liver to recover.

Complementary medical support includes:

  • Fluid therapy to correct electrolytes — potassium and phosphorus deserve special attention (Center, 2005)
  • Antiemetics to control vomiting and nausea
  • Vitamin supplementation, especially vitamins B and K

How to prevent it?

Prevention is straightforward, but requires daily attention:

  • Monitor food intake every day
  • Never change the diet abruptly — the transition should take 7 to 10 days
  • In weight loss programs, reduce weight gradually — severe caloric restriction is dangerous
  • During stressful situations (moving house, a new pet arriving, travel), pay extra attention

Golden rule: if your cat goes more than 24 to 48 hours without eating, seek veterinary care immediately. Do not wait to see what happens.
