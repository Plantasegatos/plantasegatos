Not Every Cat on the Street Was Abandoned

When we find cats living in vacant lots, parking areas, industrial sites or other urban spaces, it is easy to assume that all of them were once companion animals that were later abandoned.

Abandonment is real, serious and must be prevented. But abandonment alone does not explain the entire ecology of free-roaming cat populations.

A relationship thousands of years old

The history of the domestic cat is closely connected to human settlements. Genetic evidence links the domestication of Felis catus to Near Eastern wildcat populations and their association with early agricultural communities.

Stored grain attracted rodents. Rodents attracted cats. This relationship became a classic example of commensalism: cats benefited from resources associated with human settlements long before becoming dependent household companions.

As humans expanded across continents and maritime routes, cats traveled with them and eventually reached the Americas. On ships, their ability to hunt rodents also helped protect stored provisions.

Feral does not simply mean abandoned

There is an important behavioral distinction between a socialized companion cat that becomes lost or abandoned and a feral cat.

Cats born and raised with little or no positive human contact during the sensitive socialization period may develop strong avoidance behavior. Many avoid physical proximity, handling and confinement and can organize much of their lives independently of direct human interaction.

A cat seen on the street, therefore, may never have lived inside a home. It may have been born in that environment, just like its parents and even previous generations.

A colony is a population

A useful way to understand a feline colony is to consider it a dynamic population.

Births, deaths, immigration, abandonment, disappearance and adoption continuously change the number of individuals. The reproductive capacity of fertile cats allows new generations to emerge rapidly under favorable conditions.

The presence of many cats in one location therefore does not, by itself, demonstrate a recent episode of mass abandonment.

What happens when the city changes?

Urban environments are constantly changing.

Vacant lots are fenced, buildings are demolished, new developments appear, and sources of food and shelter disappear or move. A feline population that remained relatively unnoticed for years may suddenly begin circulating through highly visible areas.

To someone seeing them for the first time, the cats may appear to have suddenly arrived. The population itself, however, may have a much longer history.

Why can simply removing cats fail?

Removing individuals immediately reduces the number of cats present, but it does not necessarily remove the conditions that supported the population.

If food, shelter and access to the territory remain available, other cats may occupy the space. Fertile individuals that remain may also continue reproducing.

This helps explain what is commonly called the vacuum effect: reducing a population without addressing the factors supporting it may allow the territory to be occupied again.

TNR: Trap, Neuter and Return

One strategy used to manage free-roaming cat populations is TNR — Trap, Neuter and Return, known in Portuguese as CED.

Cats are humanely trapped, surgically sterilized and, after appropriate recovery, returned to their original territory. Socialized cats may be placed for adoption when appropriate and feasible.

The approach targets one of the fundamental drivers of population growth: the birth of new kittens.

However, neutering a few cats is not the same as population management. Effective programs require scale, continuity, monitoring and attention to new individuals entering the population.

Evidence from Brazil

A 2024 study followed a free-roaming cat colony in Brazil's Federal District for 18 months. The study initially involved 157 cats and combined mass sterilization, monitoring, adoption and educational actions.

By the end of the study, 98.8% of the animals had been sterilized and the colony population had decreased by 47.8%.

The researchers also documented adoption, death, disappearance, abandonment and immigration — demonstrating that a colony is an open and dynamic population.

The authors emphasized that planning, mass sterilization, active management, continuous monitoring, adoption, abandonment prevention and education are important components of sustainable population management.

Abandonment remains part of the problem

Recognizing the existence of feral populations does not minimize the seriousness of abandonment.

Abandoning another cat in an established colony introduces another individual into a population that already requires management. If the animal is fertile, it may also contribute to future generations.

Preventing abandonment, identification, sterilization, responsible adoption and management of free-roaming populations are therefore complementary measures.

A different way of looking at the problem

Perhaps the first question when encountering a colony should not only be:

“Who abandoned these cats?”

We should also ask:

“How long has this population been here — and what can we do to prevent it from continuing to grow?”

Understanding the history and biology of Felis catus does not mean romanticizing life on the streets. It means recognizing that a population problem requires a population-level response.

Individual neutering helps. Population management requires scale, continuity and monitoring.
