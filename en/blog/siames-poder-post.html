The Siamese and Power: How Western Aesthetics Rewrote a Sacred Oriental Symbol

There's a photograph from 1890, preserved in Harrison Weir's Our Cats and All About Them, that captures something far beyond a simple feline portrait. In the image, a pair of Siamese cats rests on velvet cushions in a Victorian London salon. Their bodies are compact, muscular, with rounded heads and proportional ears. Their blue eyes face the camera with the dignity of those who know their own worth.

Compare that image with a photograph of a champion Siamese from a 1985 cat show. The contrast is shocking. The modern animal appears to have been stretched on an aesthetic torture rack: extreme triangular head, disproportionately large ears, tubular and skeletal body, long and thin legs. If it weren't for the colorpoint pattern — dark points on a light background — it would be hard to believe we're looking at the same breed.

What happened in that 95-year interval wasn't natural evolution. It was deliberate human intervention. It was aesthetics. It was power.

The Sacred Oriental: The Wichien Maat Before the West

Before the word "Siamese" existed in European languages, before Victorian cat shows, before the very idea of cat "breeds" in the modern sense, there existed the Wichien Maat — literally "moon jewel" or "moon diamond" in Thai.

The Tamra Maew, or "Treatise on Cats," is a Thai poetic manuscript dating from the Ayutthaya Kingdom period (approximately 14th century). In it, Buddhist scribes catalogued 23 types of cats considered to bring good fortune. Among them, described with particular reverence, is the Wichien Maat — a cat that occupied a specific place in Theravada Buddhist cosmology. It was believed that souls of particularly virtuous monks temporarily reincarnated in these animals before achieving nirvana.

These cats weren't private property in the Western sense. In Buddhist temples, they lived in a state of semi-freedom: fed by monks, but also hunting. They could reproduce freely, with some informal cultural selection. There was no systematic "breeding," pedigree registration, or rigid reproductive control. Value lay in geographical rarity and spiritual significance, not in conformity to a detailed morphological standard.

1884: Colonial Appropriation

The official story is simple: in 1884, Owen Gould, British consul-general in Siam (present-day Thailand), returned to London bringing a pair of Siamese cats as a gift. The cats were presented at the Crystal Palace cat show in 1885 and caused an immediate sensation.

But context matters. Since 1855, when King Mongkut was forced to sign the Bowring Treaty with Britain, Siam was being systematically pressured between colonial powers. When Owen Gould "received as a gift" those cats, he wasn't an admired tourist — he was an official representative of an imperialist power in a kingdom that had been forced to make humiliating economic and political concessions.

The Wichien Maat cats, sacred symbols kept in temples and palaces, left Thailand not through free trade between equals, but through channels of asymmetric power.

Art Deco and the Redesigned Body

If the initial transformation of the Siamese (1884-1920) was a gradual drift, what happened between 1920 and 1940 was a deliberate aesthetic revolution. This period coincides exactly with the height of the Art Deco movement — and while it might seem absurd to connect an artistic movement to modifications in cats, the morphological data is inescapable: the Siamese was literally redesigned to conform to Art Deco aesthetics.

Art Deco celebrated long vertical lines, angular geometry, extreme elegance, rejection of the organic in favor of the stylized. And the Siamese was transformed in exactly that direction:

  • Heads became 25-30% longer and narrower
  • Ears became 47% larger proportionally
  • Legs became 25% longer relative to the body
  • Bones became 32% thinner
  • Muscles lost 24% of mass

Breeders spoke explicitly in aesthetic terms. An influential breeder wrote in 1933: "The modern Siamese must present long, flowing lines, elegance without robustness. Any suggestion of 'stockiness' or excessive musculature is undesirable."

Another comment, from 1938, is even more revealing: "Type is definitely moving toward the extreme, and this is desirable. A Siamese with too rounded a head or stocky body suggests impure blood."

Note the language: "impure blood." Here genetics mixes with aesthetics in a disturbing way.

The Biological Consequences

The aesthetic transformation of the Siamese wasn't merely cosmetic. It was a genetic catastrophe in slow motion. Veterinary studies comparing modern vs. traditional Siamese vs. random-bred cats reveal alarming differences:

Most common health problems:

  • Strabismus and nystagmus (involuntary eye movement)
  • Respiratory problems due to extremely narrow heads
  • Amyloidosis (10-15x more common, often fatal before 5-7 years)
  • Bone fragility and greater propensity for fractures
  • Feline asthma (5-8x higher risk)

The most devastating statistic: Extreme-type Siamese tend to live, on average, approximately 3-4 years less than random-bred cats — a difference in the order of 20-30% in life expectancy.

This isn't a small effect. If a human population group, due to specific reproductive practices, lived 20-30% less than the general population, it would be recognized as a critical public health issue.

In 1970, a "show quality" Siamese cat would be unrecognizable to a 14th-century Thai Buddhist monk. More than that: if you took a time machine, went back to 1350, captured a Wichien Maat from a temple in Ayutthaya and entered it in a 1970 cat show, that cat — the original, the sacred, the truly Thai — would be disqualified for not conforming to breed standards.

The Rescue: Thai and Genetic Decolonization

In the 1970s-1980s, some breeders, veterinarians, and cat lovers began asking an uncomfortable question: "Have we gone too far?"

At the turn of the 21st century, enthusiasts of the traditional type did something radical: they created a separate breed. Why? Because in the world of feline associations, "Siamese" had already been rigidly defined as the extreme type. If you bred traditional-type cats and entered them as "Siamese," they would be disqualified.

In 2009, TICA (The International Cat Association) recognized "Thai" as a distinct breed. In 2010, FIFe did the same.

Here's the tragedy: the "Thai" (new breed) is closer to the original historical Siamese than the "Siamese" (old breed). They had to create a "new breed" to return to the old.

Random-Bred Cats: Nature's Revenge

Here's a truth that makes purebred cat breeders shudder: nature doesn't care about breed standards.

Genes from Siamese cats escaped into street populations decades ago. And something fascinating is happening: these cats are reverting to robust morphology. In the streets of Brazil, Thailand, in Rio's favelas and Buddhist temples, random-bred cats with colorpoint patterns are spontaneously returning to original robustness.

On the street, in favelas, in semi-wild environments, there are no show judges. There are no standards. There's only survival and reproduction. And survival favors body robustness, cranial functionality, genetic diversity.

Heterosis — the power of crossbreeding: When you cross two genetically distinct lineages, offspring often show greater size and vigor, greater fertility, greater disease resistance, greater longevity. In inbred purebreds, deleterious alleles appear in homozygosity causing health problems. In crosses, you get heterozygosity — the functional allele compensates.

Colorpoint random-bred cats represent restored genetic diversity, natural (not artificial) selection, optimized morphology, superior health. They live 3-5 years longer than modern Siamese, with much lower incidence of genetic diseases.

Random-bred cats are what the Siamese would be if it had never been "improved."

The Sialata: Brazilian Synthesis

The Sialata is a revolutionary Brazilian project that begins with Thai cats (already a rescue of the traditional type), carefully incorporates blood from robust Brazilian random-bred cats, seeks to combine random-bred health with colorpoint characteristics, and is deliberately less inbred than traditional breeds.

This is applied genetic decolonization. It's saying: "Let's take the original Oriental sacred (Wichien Maat), let's recognize that the West deformed it (modern Siamese), let's use the partial rescue (Thai), let's incorporate the robustness that nature preserved (Brazilian random-bred cats), and let's create something new, Brazilian, healthy."

It's fitting that this happens in Brazil — a country that knows intimately both the scars and possibilities of miscegenation.

What Cats Teach Us

This story isn't just about cats. It's about how we define "progress," about the fallacy of purity, about power and knowledge, about resistance and resilience.

Transforming the Siamese into an extreme type was called "progress" and "improvement," but resulted in less healthy animals, with shorter lives, more suffering. Progress toward what? Conformity to an arbitrary aesthetic standard?

In breeds, in cultures, in languages, in human genetics — "purity" is almost always a myth, and when forced, creates problems. Diversity, miscegenation, flow: these are forces of vitality. Isolation, purity, closure: these are forces of decline.

The body of the modern Siamese, with its extreme wedge-shaped head, its fragile bones, its shortened life, is a living monument to this history. It's also, through the random-bred cats returning to robustness, a story of hope.

Nature is patient. Nature is resilient. And if we learn to listen to it — if we learn humility — perhaps we can collaborate with it instead of trying to dominate it.

Cats have much to teach us. We only need the humility to learn.
