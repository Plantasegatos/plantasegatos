Can Your Cat Sense Pregnancy? What Science Says About This Change

The same story is told in many homes: even before the pregnancy becomes visible, the cat begins following the woman from room to room, sleeping closer to her, repeatedly sniffing her clothes or choosing her belly as a favorite resting place.

For those experiencing it, one conclusion seems almost inevitable: the cat knows.

Science, however, calls for a more careful answer. There is currently no evidence showing that cats understand the concept of human pregnancy or consciously recognize that a baby is developing. What we do have is a growing body of knowledge about feline smell, cognition, attachment, environmental sensitivity and behavior that helps explain why many cats may genuinely notice that something has changed.

They may not know that a baby is coming. Yet they may perceive, with an accuracy that often exceeds our own senses, that a familiar person no longer smells, moves or follows a routine in exactly the same way.

It is not a sixth sense

When a cat appears to anticipate a human change, people often attribute the behavior to some supernatural ability. In most cases, the most plausible explanation is less mysterious and equally fascinating: cats experience a sensory world that is different from ours.

Feline behavior is strongly influenced by odors, quiet sounds, vibrations, posture, movement patterns and changes in environmental predictability. Information that goes unnoticed by humans may be important enough to alter the way a cat approaches, rests, observes or keeps its distance.

Therefore, when a cat behaves differently during pregnancy, it is probably not interpreting pregnancy as a diagnosis. It is responding to the physical and environmental signals produced by it.

The body begins telling a different story

Pregnancy involves profound physiological changes. Hormonal concentrations, blood circulation, body temperature, perspiration, metabolism and bodily secretions all change. These transformations may also subtly alter the natural odor of the skin, sweat, breath and clothing.

There is no specific evidence showing that cats identify estrogen, progesterone or human chorionic gonadotropin as signs of pregnancy. It would be inaccurate to claim that they “smell the pregnancy hormone” as though they were performing a biological test.

However, we know that smell plays a central role in feline life. In 2025, researchers found that domestic cats investigated the odor of unfamiliar people for longer than the odor of their caregivers, indicating that cats use olfactory information to distinguish familiar from unfamiliar humans.

This makes it plausible that a persistent alteration in the body odor of a familiar person could be detected. The cat may not understand its cause, but it notices that the person’s sensory profile has changed.

Warmth may also change proximity

Cats consistently seek warm places. Sunny windows, blankets, electronic devices and the human lap often become preferred resting areas.

Pregnancy can involve changes in circulation, thermal sensation and skin temperature. This does not mean that every cat will begin lying on a pregnant belly, but it may help explain why some seek greater physical contact or choose particular areas of the body for rest.

There is also a less physiological and equally relevant explanation: as pregnancy progresses, a woman may spend more time sitting or lying down. For the cat, this means greater access to a stable, warm and familiar place.

What appears to be an exclusive attraction to the belly may partly result from a combination of warmth, body position and opportunity for contact.

Can the cat feel the baby moving?

During the later stages of pregnancy, fetal movement can become externally perceptible. A cat lying on the abdomen may feel small contractions, shifts or vibrations.

It is reasonable to assume that an animal attentive to subtle movement could notice these stimuli. Once again, however, perception is not the same as understanding.

The cat may detect movement beneath the surface of the body. There is no evidence that it interprets that movement as a baby, a human infant or a future member of the household.

This distinction is essential. It allows us to appreciate the relationship without attributing cognitive abilities that have not been scientifically demonstrated.

Cats also perceive emotional and social changes

Domestic cats are often described as independent, but independence does not mean indifference. Research shows that they form complex social relationships with humans, recognize familiar signals and may develop attachment patterns toward the people with whom they live.

During pregnancy, the emotional dynamics of the household may change. There may be new conversations, medical appointments, visitors, worries, disrupted sleep and changes in attention. The pregnant woman may speak differently, walk more slowly, rest more often or seek the cat’s company more frequently.

The cat does not need to understand pregnancy in order to respond. It may simply notice that its familiar person is behaving differently.

In some cases, this increases proximity. The cat follows the pregnant woman, seeks contact, sleeps beside her or vocalizes more often. In others, it may choose to observe from a distance.

Is the cat protecting the pregnant woman?

Some caregivers describe their cat as a sentinel: following the pregnant woman around the home, remaining nearby when strangers visit or sleeping close to the bed or doorway.

It is tempting to interpret this behavior as conscious protection. A more cautious explanation is that the cat is monitoring an important change in its social environment.

Following, watching and staying close can indicate affiliation, curiosity, a search for security or an attempt to maintain predictability. In some cats, greater proximity may also reflect an already strong bond with the pregnant woman.

This does not make the behavior any less meaningful. Instead, it shows that the cat is responding to change through the sensory and emotional resources of its own species.

Not every cat becomes more affectionate

One of the greatest mistakes in discussions of feline behavior is turning common reports into universal rules.

Not every cat moves closer during pregnancy. Some may avoid the lap, sleep in different rooms, hide or appear more irritable. These responses do not mean that the cat rejects the baby or no longer cares about the pregnant woman.

Cats rely heavily on predictability. Changes in smell, furniture, schedules, sounds and attention can be difficult to interpret. For a more insecure, sensitive or change-averse individual, withdrawal may be an adaptive response.

Temperament, previous experiences, age, health, socialization and environmental quality strongly influence each cat’s reaction.

For this reason, comparing one cat with another is rarely useful. Behavior must be understood within the history of the individual animal.

The home begins changing before the baby arrives

A crib, stroller, clothes, toys, furniture and new products gradually enter the home. To humans, these are meaningful preparations. To the cat, they are unfamiliar objects occupying previously predictable spaces.

Change does not have to be negative. Problems are more likely when everything happens at once or when the cat abruptly loses access to important spaces, people and routines.

Gradual introduction allows the cat to investigate new objects safely. Letting the cat smell a baby blanket, observe the crib from a comfortable distance and become familiar with new sounds may reduce the impact of the transition.

There is no need to force contact. The goal is not to make the cat “love” every baby item, but to allow the unfamiliar to become less threatening.

How to prepare your cat during pregnancy

The best preparation begins before birth and is based on predictability, respect and positive associations.

Whenever possible, maintain regular times for feeding, play, litter box care and social contact. When a routine will need to change after the baby arrives, introduce that change gradually during pregnancy.

Present baby furniture and objects one at a time. Allow the cat to investigate without punishment while establishing consistent boundaries when necessary. Provide comfortable alternatives such as beds, shelves, hiding places and elevated resting areas near the family.

It is also important not to reduce contact abruptly. Some families, fearing that the cat may become “jealous,” begin pushing it away before the baby is born. To the animal, this can turn the baby’s arrival into a sequence of losses.

It is better to preserve predictable moments of attention and gradually teach new patterns of coexistence.

When behavioral changes require veterinary attention

Not every behavioral change occurring during a caregiver’s pregnancy is caused by the pregnancy itself. Two events happening at the same time do not prove that one caused the other.

If the cat stops eating, urinates outside the litter box, overgrooms, becomes suddenly aggressive, vocalizes unusually or remains hidden for long periods, veterinary evaluation is important.

Pain, urinary disease, metabolic disorders, dental problems and other medical conditions may initially appear as behavioral change.

Before describing any reaction as “jealousy,” “protection” or “premonition,” clinical causes should be excluded and the environmental context should be assessed.

The bond begins before birth

The cat may not know that a baby is coming. It may not understand the word pregnancy, the meaning of an ultrasound or the reason the home is being reorganized.

But it knows that person’s scent. It knows their footsteps, schedules, voice and the small rituals that shape everyday life.

When the body and routine change, the cat perceives that its familiar reference has changed as well. It approaches, watches, investigates or withdraws according to its personality and the security provided by the environment.

It is not magic. It is not a diagnosis. And it does not need to be either in order to remain extraordinary.

It is a sensitive animal responding, in its own way, to one of the greatest transformations in human life.

Pregnancy does not occur only inside a woman’s body. It changes the entire household. For the cat sharing that territory and relationship, the transition may begin long before the baby’s first cry.
