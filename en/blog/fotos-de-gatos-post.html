Why Do We Take So Many Photos of Our Cats?

You open your phone’s gallery looking for a document and find a sleeping cat. Then the same cat yawning. Next, your cat is staring out of the window, curled up on the sofa, sitting inside a box, and lying in exactly the same position as in the previous photo.

To anyone looking from the outside, many of these pictures seem identical. To us, none of them are. In one, the cat tilted its head ever so slightly. In another, the eyes narrowed. In the next, one paw curled in a way that lasted only a few seconds. Naturally, we had to keep them all.

We Do Not Photograph Only What Happens

We photograph what matters. For a brief moment, the shutter interrupts the quiet passage of days and turns an ordinary scene into something we can return to. A photograph cannot preserve body warmth, the sound of purring, or the texture of fur, but it can become a doorway back to those memories.

That is why an apparently ordinary image can carry so much. It does not show only a sleeping cat. It holds a particular morning, a favorite blanket, sunlight entering through the window, and the peaceful knowledge that, at that moment, your cat was there.

What Does Science Say About Our Desire to Take Photos?

Across a series of nine experiments, researchers found that taking photos of positive experiences could increase people’s engagement with what they were doing and, under certain conditions, enhance their enjoyment. When we search for something worth photographing, we direct our attention, examine details, and consciously choose the moment we want to preserve.

This helps explain why we notice tiny differences among so many pictures of our cats. We are not merely recording a body position: we are following expressions, movements, and small events that our emotional bond makes meaningful.

But a Camera Does Not Replace Memory

The relationship between photography and memory is not simple. Another study, conducted during a museum tour, identified what became known as the photo-taking-impairment effect: when participants automatically photographed entire objects, they later remembered fewer details. When they zoomed in and examined one specific part, however, this effect was reduced.

In other words, the number of photographs does not determine the value of an experience. Attention appears to be what matters. Photographing with presence can bring us closer to the moment; clicking mechanically and never revisiting the image can turn the phone into little more than a storage space.

Why Do Cats Capture Our Attention So Easily?

Some facial characteristics associated with the so-called baby schema — such as relatively large eyes, a short muzzle, and rounded contours — may support perceptions of cuteness and draw attention. Research using human, canine, and feline faces shows that these visual cues influence gaze and cuteness ratings, although they cannot explain the depth of the bond between a person and their cat on their own.

After appearance comes life together. We learn to recognize the look that asks for play, the meow that seeks company, a favorite sleeping position, and even the expression that appears just before mischief. The cat is no longer simply a beautiful animal, but an individual whose story we know in detail.

An Attempt to Make the Moment Last

Perhaps our phones run out of storage because some things matter too much to be left to memory alone. We take photos knowing, even if we do not consciously think about it, that cats grow, change, and continually invent new ways to inhabit both our homes and our lives.

Among thousands of images, there is almost always one special photograph. It does not need to be the sharpest, the prettiest, or the best lit. It is the one that seems to say: “This is my cat. This is how I know them. This is how I want to preserve this moment.”

Now open your phone’s gallery and choose just one: what is the best photo you have of your little love?
