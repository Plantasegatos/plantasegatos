Cat Vaccination: Complete Guide

By Plantas & Gatos — your trusted guide to feline health and care

Vaccination is one of the most effective ways to protect your cat’s health. Even strictly indoor cats face risks — whether through indirect contact with viruses and bacteria carried on clothes and shoes, or in emergency situations such as an escape or the need for hospitalization.

💉 Essential Vaccines

V4 or V5 – Protect against rhinotracheitis, calicivirus, panleukopenia and, in the case of V5, also chlamydiosis. These are killed or modified vaccines designed to stimulate immunity without causing the disease.

Rabies – Mandatory in several states of Brazil, protects against a fatal disease that also poses a risk to humans. Rabies is a zoonosis with no cure once symptoms appear.

📅 When to Vaccinate?

The basic protocol begins at around 8 weeks of age, with boosters every 3–4 weeks until the initial series is complete (usually 3 doses). Afterwards, boosters are given annually or every three years, depending on the veterinarian’s evaluation and the type of vaccine used.

Adult cats with no vaccination history can also begin the protocol at any time, usually with two initial doses and a subsequent annual booster.

🐾 Why is it so important?

Diseases like panleukopenia (also called “feline parvovirus”) and feline rhinotracheitis are highly contagious and can be fatal, especially in kittens and immunosuppressed cats. Vaccination not only protects your cat but also contributes to controlling diseases within the feline community.

🦠 Main Diseases Covered by Vaccines

  • Feline Panleukopenia (V4/V5) – Causes high fever, severe vomiting, intense diarrhea, and a drastic drop in white blood cells. Highly fatal in unvaccinated kittens.
  • Feline Rhinotracheitis (V4/V5) – Respiratory disease caused by herpesvirus, leading to sneezing, nasal and ocular discharge, fever, and corneal ulcers. Can become chronic.
  • Feline Calicivirus (V4/V5) – Causes respiratory symptoms, mouth and tongue ulcers, fever, and in some cases, pneumonia. More aggressive strains can be fatal.
  • Feline Chlamydiosis (V5) – Bacterial infection mainly affecting the eyes, causing persistent conjunctivitis and sometimes respiratory symptoms.
  • Rabies – Virus attacking the nervous system, 100% fatal once symptoms appear. Transmissible to humans.

⚠️ Important: Limitations of Vaccination

Even fully vaccinated cats may still contract these diseases, usually in a much milder form. This happens because:

  • No vaccine provides 100% protection;
  • There are different viral strains, some not covered by vaccines;
  • Immune responses vary among individuals;
  • Immunosuppressed cats may not develop adequate protection.

🩺 Special Care: FIV and FeLV

Cats with FIV (Feline Immunodeficiency Virus) or FeLV (Feline Leukemia Virus) require special attention regarding vaccination:

For V4/V5 vaccines: Most veterinarians consider killed or recombinant vaccines safe for FIV+ and FeLV+ cats. Modified live vaccines should be avoided as they may cause the very diseases they are meant to prevent.

For rabies vaccine: Generally considered safe and recommended, since rabies is fatal and a public health risk.

Recommendation: Always inform your veterinarian if your cat is FIV+ or FeLV+. They will assess the safest vaccine type and whether the benefits outweigh the risks for your cat.

🔬 About the FeLV Vaccine

The vaccine against Feline Leukemia Virus (FeLV) is especially recommended for:

  • Cats with outdoor access;
  • Multi-cat environments;
  • Cats that may come into contact with felines of unknown FeLV status.

Important: This vaccine should not be given to cats already positive for FeLV, as it has no therapeutic effect. Testing before vaccination is recommended.

⚠️ Watch for Post-Vaccination Signs

After vaccination, it is common to see:

  • Mild sleepiness for 24–48 hours;
  • Soreness or a small lump at the injection site;
  • Low-grade fever.

Contact your veterinarian immediately if there is:

  • Persistent vomiting;
  • Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath;
  • Swelling of the face or paws;
  • Extreme lethargy;
  • Collapse or seizures.

These rare reactions may indicate anaphylaxis or another severe adverse response.

✅ Final Tip

Always keep your cat’s vaccination record up to date and ask your veterinarian for personalized guidance on the ideal protocol, considering age, lifestyle, health conditions, and local disease prevalence. Vaccination is an act of care and responsibility for your cat’s health and for the entire community.
