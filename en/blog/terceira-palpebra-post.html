The Cat's Third Eyelid: Protection, Tears, and Warning Signs

Sometimes, while a cat is deeply asleep or has just awakened, a pale film appears at the inner corner of the eyes. It may look unusual to someone who has never noticed it, but it is neither loose skin nor an abnormal growth. It is the third eyelid, also known as the nictitating membrane.

Humans rely on the upper and lower eyelids. Cats, like many other animals, have this additional membrane. It normally remains retracted near the nose and moves across part of the ocular surface when needed. It is a discreet, rapid, and highly efficient anatomical solution.

A small shield in the corner of the eye

The third eyelid helps protect the cornea from dust, particles, vegetation, and minor trauma. For a predator that historically moved through shrubs, chased prey, and explored narrow spaces, protecting the eyes without keeping them fully closed offered an important advantage.

It does not work like a curtain that the cat raises voluntarily. Its position depends on orbital anatomy, movement of the eyeball, muscle tone, and regional innervation. When the eye retracts slightly, as can happen during sleep, the membrane tends to move forward.

It also helps lubricate the eye

The third eyelid does more than provide mechanical protection. As it moves, it helps spread the tear film across the cornea, keeping the ocular surface moist and assisting in the removal of small particles.

A gland associated with the membrane contributes to the watery portion of tears. The area also contains lymphoid tissue involved in local immune defense. This structure therefore acts as a shield, cleaner, tear distributor, and component of ocular immune surveillance.

When can it be normal to see it?

A small part of the third eyelid may become visible during deep sleep, intense relaxation, immediately after waking, or while an animal is still recovering from sedation or anesthesia. In these situations, it should retract when the cat becomes fully awake and alert.

Context is essential. A membrane that is briefly visible in a comfortable, active cat with otherwise normal eyes is very different from a third eyelid that remains elevated for hours.

When does it become a clinical sign?

The third eyelid is usually not the disease itself. When it stays visible, it often indicates that an ocular, neurological, or systemic problem is affecting the normal position of the eye and membrane.

Painful eye diseases such as corneal ulcers, uveitis, glaucoma, severe conjunctivitis, foreign bodies, and trauma can cause it to rise. Tearing, redness, discharge, sensitivity to light, or keeping the eye partly closed may also be present.

Dehydration, weight loss, fever, weakness, and other systemic illnesses may also make the third eyelid more prominent. Looking at the eyes alone is therefore not enough: appetite, behavior, hydration, stool quality, and activity level help complete the clinical picture.

One eye or both?

The distribution of the change provides useful clues. When only one eye is affected, the veterinarian carefully considers local disease, ocular pain, trauma, and neurological abnormalities on that side.

In Horner syndrome, for example, disruption of sympathetic innervation can produce a constricted pupil, slight drooping of the upper eyelid, a sunken appearance of the eye, and elevation of the third eyelid, usually on one side. Middle-ear disease is among the possibilities investigated in cats, although different points along the nerve pathway may be involved.

When both third eyelids appear at the same time, particularly without severe ocular inflammation, one possibility is Haws syndrome. It is primarily described in cats and may occur in association with gastrointestinal disturbances, including diarrhea. That association does not justify diagnosing it from appearance alone: the cat still needs examination so that ocular, neurological, and systemic causes can be excluded.

Not every pink structure is merely the membrane

The gland associated with the third eyelid can prolapse, creating a rounded pink mass at the inner corner of the eye, popularly called cherry eye. It is far more common in dogs than in cats, but it can occur. Because this gland contributes to tear production, modern treatment aims to preserve and reposition it whenever possible rather than simply removing it.

How does a veterinarian investigate it?

Evaluation may include inspection of the eyelids and conjunctiva, fluorescein staining of the cornea, measurement of intraocular pressure, assessment of tear production, and examination of the pupils and neurological reflexes. Depending on the signs, ear examination, bloodwork, fecal testing, or diagnostic imaging may also be required.

This investigation matters because apparently harmless eye drops may worsen certain diseases. Products containing corticosteroids, for example, are contraindicated in some corneal injuries and should never be used without veterinary examination.

When should veterinary care be sought?

Seek veterinary evaluation when the third eyelid remains visible while the cat is awake, appears suddenly, or is accompanied by pain, discharge, redness, unequal pupils, loss of appetite, persistent diarrhea, lethargy, or altered vision.

Eye conditions can deteriorate rapidly. Waiting several days to see whether a painful change resolves on its own can turn a treatable problem into a more serious injury.

A window into a cat's health

The third eyelid is a remarkable example of how nearly invisible structures support a cat's daily life. It protects, lubricates, and assists ocular defense without drawing attention. When it appears, however, it invites the caregiver to observe the animal more carefully.

Knowing an individual cat's normal behavior provides the best reference. A brief appearance during sleep may be physiological. A persistent, painful change or one accompanied by other signs should be understood for what it truly is: a reason to investigate, not a diagnosis by itself.
