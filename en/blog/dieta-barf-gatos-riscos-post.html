BARF Diet for Cats: What Science Says About the Risks of Amateur Prescription

What the BARF diet is — and why it divides opinion

BARF is the acronym for two overlapping concepts: Biologically Appropriate Raw Food or Bones and Raw Food. In essence, it is a feeding approach based on raw meat, bones, organs, and depending on the variant, raw vegetables and eggs — with the stated goal of reproducing the diet that the ancestral cat would have had in the wild.

The evolutionary argument has intuitive appeal: cats are obligate carnivores, hunters that for millions of years consumed whole, unprocessed prey. Why not feed them as close as possible to what their biology "expects"? This logic sustains a significant community of enthusiastic owners, active forums, and a growing market for raw pet products.

The problem — and it is a technical problem, not a philosophical one — is that reproducing the complete nutrition of a wild prey in a domestic kitchen is extraordinarily difficult. The difference between BARF done correctly by a veterinary nutritionist and BARF assembled from forum recipes is the difference between medicine and self-medication. And the consequences of error can take months or years to manifest — enough time for the damage to become severe and irreversible.

The calcium:phosphorus imbalance — the most silent and most serious risk

Of all the nutritional risks of homemade BARF, the imbalance between calcium and phosphorus is the most documented, the most insidious, and the most dangerous. To understand why, it is necessary to understand the basic biochemistry of these two minerals.

Phosphorus is abundant in muscle meat — it is structurally part of cells, a component of cell membranes and energy molecules like ATP. Any diet based on muscle meat will naturally have high phosphorus content. Calcium, on the other hand, is concentrated in bones — not in soft meat. The ideal Ca:P ratio in the feline diet is approximately 1.1:1 to 1.5:1 (calcium equal to or slightly higher than phosphorus). Below this, the body enters compensation mode.

The compensation mechanism is nutritional secondary hyperparathyroidism: when calcium intake is chronically low relative to phosphorus, the parathyroid glands respond by elevating PTH (parathyroid hormone) production. PTH signals the body to mobilize calcium from where it is stored — and the largest reservoir is the skeleton. Bone begins to be demineralized to maintain serum calcium levels within the normal range. During this process, blood tests may show apparently normal calcium — the animal is being depleted from the inside while the tests reassure the owner.

The clinical progression includes muscle weakness, bone pain on palpation, pathological fractures from minimal trauma, seizures, and in the most severe cases, collapse. In kittens and young animals whose bones are still forming, the consequences are even more serious: nutritional osteodystrophy, permanent skeletal deformities, and growth compromise.

An analysis published in 2001 in the Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association (Freeman & Michel) found among homemade BARF diets tested a Ca:P ratio of only 0.20 — meaning phosphorus was five times higher than calcium. Vitamins A and E were below the detectable limit. Vitamin D was nearly twice the AAFCO maximum. A diet that its defenders would describe as "natural" and "balanced."

A subsequent European study evaluated 95 homemade BARF diets reported by owners and found serious nutritional imbalances in 60% of them. Royal Canin Academy notes in its review that calcium and phosphorus imbalances are consistently found in BARF recipes — both homemade and commercial — and that clinical signs may be absent for months or years until the body's reserves are exhausted.

Other documented nutritional imbalances

The Ca:P imbalance is the most serious risk, but it is far from the only one. The scientific literature records a consistent set of nutritional problems associated with homemade BARF:

  • Hypervitaminosis A: animal liver — especially pork liver — is extremely rich in vitamin A. Cats fed liver-based diets can develop hypervitaminosis A, manifested as depression, poor coat quality, cervical stiffness, and lameness. A case published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery (Polizopoulou et al., 2005) documented recovery only after return to commercial food.
  • Vitamin D deficiency: vitamin D is essential for intestinal calcium absorption. BARF diets frequently present vitamin D deficiency, compounding the problem of calcium availability even when it appears present in sufficient quantity.
  • Taurine deficiency: cats are unable to synthesize sufficient taurine and depend on diet to supply it. Studies have found reduced plasma taurine levels in cats and dogs fed BARF. Chronic taurine deficiency in cats leads to dilated cardiomyopathy and central retinal degeneration — both irreversible.
  • Deficiency in potassium, zinc, and magnesium: analysis of five BARF diets published by the British Veterinary Nursing Association found deficiencies in potassium, magnesium, and zinc in two of the five tested diets.
  • Pansteatitis: diets rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids without adequate vitamin E supplementation can cause pansteatitis — painful inflammation of adipose tissue — documented in cats fed raw fish without supplementation.
  • Hyperthyroidism induced by thyroid tissue: meat from the larynx and neck of animals may contain thyroid tissue. Regular ingestion of this tissue can elevate thyroid hormone levels and simulate or induce hyperthyroidism in cats.

Royal Canin Academy summarizes the structural problem precisely: "An animal with specific deficiencies in vitamins and trace elements can remain free of clinical signs for months or years until the body's reserves are exhausted. Because the animal appears healthy, the owner frequently argues that the diet must be correct. Blood tests can also be misleading: serum calcium and phosphorus levels are maintained within tight limits by the body — changes indicate a pathological process that rarely has a direct relationship with diet in a single test."

Microbiological contamination: risk for the cat and for the family

The second axis of BARF risk is contamination by pathogens — and here the problem goes beyond the cat, reaching all humans who live with it.

Joffe & Schlesinger (2002) tested BARF diets and found Salmonella in 80% of samples. Of animals fed these diets, 30% shed Salmonella in their feces — even without showing signs of disease. A cat that appears perfectly healthy can be an active Salmonella reservoir, contaminating surfaces, hands, other animals, and vulnerable humans in the household.

A Cornell University study (2025, cited in a Tufts University review) found viable, culturable bacteria in 42% of raw cat foods tested — and in none of the cooked foods. The bacteria identified included Salmonella enterica, E. coli, and Klebsiella. One Salmonella enterica strain was genetically identical to a strain associated with a reported human infection — direct evidence of zoonotic transmission.

Listeria monocytogenes was isolated in 54% of frozen raw pet food products in a Dutch study (van Bree et al., 2018) and in 16% of American samples (Nemser et al., 2014). Listeriosis in humans has disproportionate severity: over 90% of cases result in hospitalization, and mortality is significant. In pregnant women, it can cause premature birth or fetal death.

Beyond Salmonella and Listeria, the AVMA — which formally discourages raw feeding for pets — lists among pathogens documented in raw diets: Campylobacter spp., Clostridium difficile, Clostridium perfringens, Clostridium botulinum, E. coli (including shiga toxin-producing strains), Yersinia enterocolitica, enterotoxigenic Staphylococcus aureus, and the parasite Toxoplasma gondii. A European study (Nüesch-Inderbinen, 2019) found that 72.5% of BARF samples tested did not meet applicable microbiological standards — and that 62.7% contained antimicrobial-resistant bacteria.

That last point deserves special attention: the spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria through raw diets is a growing public health concern. Pets fed BARF present a higher risk of shedding bacteria with resistance genes to extended-spectrum cephalosporins and other critically important antibiotics — generating resistance pressure within the household.

The AVMA is direct in its official position: "The AVMA discourages the feeding to cats and dogs of any animal-source protein that has not first been subjected to a process to eliminate pathogens — because of the risk of illness to cats and dogs as well as humans."

Physical risks of raw bones

Raw bones are frequently recommended in BARF as the primary calcium source — and they are indeed the most bioavailable source of the mineral. But their inclusion introduces physical risks that cannot be ignored.

Whole bones or large pieces can cause oral and dental trauma (tooth fractures), esophageal or intestinal obstruction from large fragments, and — most seriously — perforation of the esophagus, stomach, or intestine by sharp bone splinters. Gastrointestinal perforation from bone is a surgical emergency with significant mortality even with rapid intervention. Cooked bones present an even higher risk of fracturing into splinters, which is why no responsible BARF protocol recommends them.

The paradox is real: the most naturally efficient source of calcium in the feline diet is also a vector of physical risk. Alternatives such as bone meal, quality eggshell powder, or calibrated mineral supplements eliminate the physical risk — but their dosage requires calculation and supervision to avoid the opposite problem (calcium excess, which is also harmful).

When BARF can be considered — and under what conditions

The scientific position is not that BARF is intrinsically impossible or always harmful. It is that, in practice, it is rarely executed with the level of rigor necessary to be safe. There is a significant difference between:

  • A raw diet formulated by a veterinarian specialized in nutrition, with laboratory analysis of ingredients, proportions calculated for the specific species, age, weight, and health condition of the animal, and periodic clinical monitoring.
  • A raw diet assembled based on Facebook group recipes, breeder blogs, or digital influencer videos — however well-intentioned their authors may be.

Only 8% of owners who adopt BARF reported consulting a veterinarian to formulate the diet (Morelli survey, 2019). The other 92% build their animals' diets from sources without nutritional validation. This number explains why studies consistently find imbalances in 60 to 90% of analyzed homemade diets.

BARF is absolutely contraindicated in: animals with chronic kidney disease (high-protein, high-phosphorus diets accelerate progression), immunosuppressed animals (risk of infection by pathogens that a healthy immune system might control), growing kittens without rigorous supervision (risk of osteodystrophy), pregnant or lactating animals (intensified nutritional demands rarely adequately covered), and households with pregnant women, young children, elderly individuals, or immunosuppressed persons (zoonotic risk).
