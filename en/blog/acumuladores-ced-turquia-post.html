Cat Hoarders, TNR and the Turkish Lesson: When Love Becomes Neglect

The problem that begins with good intentions

The scene is familiar: someone starts feeding a street cat. Soon there are two, then five. At some point, the house has 30, 50, 80 cats — and the person swears they are all fine, all loved, that it's better than the street. This narrative has a technical name in scientific literature: animal hoarding. And it represents one of the most widespread and underestimated forms of feline mistreatment.

Understanding why hoarding is harmful, how the TNR method offers an ethically sound alternative for managing urban colonies, and what happened in Turkey when decades of good practice were discarded in favor of a controversial law — that is the thread connecting this article.

Cat hoarders: what science says

The Hoarding of Animals Research Consortium (HARC) defines animal hoarding by three criteria that must occur simultaneously: accumulation of animals that has overwhelmed the person's ability to provide even minimal standards of nutrition, sanitation, and veterinary care; failure to acknowledge the deteriorating condition of the animals — including disease, malnutrition, and even death —; and denial of the impact of these conditions.

The most disturbing element of this definition is the third: denial. Most hoarders do not act out of deliberate cruelty. They act out of a genuine cognitive distortion — sincerely believing they are rescuing, protecting, loving. This psychological profile, sometimes called Noah Syndrome, causes external interventions to be experienced as persecution, not help.

From the cats' perspective, however, the consequences are documented and serious. A retrospective study published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery analyzed 371 cats rescued from 14 different hoarding situations and found a consistent clinical picture: upper respiratory infection in 13 of the 14 groups evaluated; skin disease in 12; ear mites in 9; gingivitis in 9. Nearly 90% of the animals were unneutered. 18% of females of breeding age were pregnant at the time of rescue.

What hoarding does to a cat's body and mind

Overcrowding is not simply a lack of physical space — it is a state of chronic physiological stress. Research published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery by Amat et al. (2016) documents that cats with high stress levels are almost five times more likely to develop respiratory infection than cats in adequate environments. The mechanism is direct: stress suppresses immune function and facilitates reactivation of feline herpesvirus, the primary agent of feline rhinotracheitis.

Beyond the respiratory system, hoarding systematically compromises:

  • Digestive system: stress alters intestinal permeability, causing chronic diarrhea and facilitating infections by Tritrichomonas and other enteropathogens.
  • Urinary tract: feline interstitial cystitis (FIC) has a direct correlation with chronic stress. Cats in hoarding situations develop lower urinary tract signs at significantly higher rates.
  • Skin and coat: malnutrition and parasitism by fleas, ticks, and mites cause anemia, dermatosis, and alopecia. In severe hoarding cases, dermatophytosis from Microsporum canis — ringworm — affects nearly all animals in the group.
  • Retroviruses: FeLV and FIV show significantly higher prevalence in hoarded cats than in conventional feral or domestic populations, a direct result of intense physical contact, agonistic interactions, and stress-induced immunosuppression.
  • Behavior: aggression, urinary marking outside the litter box, compulsive behaviors, and chronic fear are documented behavioral consequences of hoarding. Many cats rescued from hoarding situations present psychological trauma that complicates subsequent socialization.

And there is the most brutal finding of all: in more than half of investigated hoarding cases, at least one dead animal is found on the premises — and the caretaker had frequently not noticed.

TNR: Trap, Neuter, Return

The ethically grounded alternative for managing urban cat colonies is called TNR — Trap, Neuter, Return — known in Portuguese-speaking countries as CED (Capturar, Esterilizar, Devolver). The method emerged in Europe in the 1980s, with the pioneering work of British veterinarian Dr. Jenny Remfry and the studies of veterinarian Roger Tabor on neutered colonies, and consolidated as public policy in several countries throughout the 1990s.

The TNR protocol works in stages:

  • Colony assessment: mapping of all individuals present, their territories and behaviors.
  • Trapping: use of humane traps with attractive bait (sardines, deli meat, wet food). The process respects the animal and minimizes trapping trauma.
  • Veterinary procedures: neutering or spaying, internal and external deworming, rabies vaccination when indicated, full clinical examination. Sick animals receive treatment.
  • Ear tip: a small standardized cut at the tip of the left ear, performed under anesthesia during the neuter surgery, with no additional suffering for the animal. It is the international identification mark for TNR-neutered cats, recognized in dozens of countries. It prevents unnecessary recaptures and the stress that would come with them.
  • Return to territory: the cat is returned to where it lived. Its presence keeps the territory stable — preventing other unneutered cats from occupying it — and preserves natural rodent control.
  • Colony caretaker: a volunteer responsible for regular feeding, animal monitoring, and identification of new individuals who need to be included in the program.

The ear tip is not mutilation — it is identification

One of the strongest points of resistance among people who encounter cats with a clipped ear tip is the immediate impression that the animal has been hurt or mutilated. This reaction, while understandable, stems from a misunderstanding. The cut is performed by a veterinarian, under anesthesia during the neuter surgery, in quickly healing cartilaginous tissue. It causes no pain beyond what is already present in the main surgical procedure.

The function of the ear tip is strictly practical and humanitarian: a cat with a marked ear can be identified from a distance as already neutered and belonging to a managed colony. Without this identification, the same cat could be retrapped, subjected again to transport and anesthesia — unnecessary stress — only for the veterinarian to confirm what the ear tip already communicated. It is an international convention that spares suffering.

Confusing it with cruelty is the opposite of what it represents: it is the mark of an animal that received care.

Why TNR works: the logic of the vacuum effect

Critics of TNR often argue that if the problem is cats, the solution should be to remove them. The logic seems intuitive. But it ignores a well-documented phenomenon in animal population ecology: the vacuum effect.

Cats establish themselves in a territory because resources exist there — food, shelter, protection. When a colony is removed — whether by capture, relocation, or culling — the resources remain. And other unneutered cats, from adjacent colonies, occupy the empty space within a relatively short time, restarting the reproductive cycle from zero.

TNR breaks this cycle differently: it keeps neutered animals in the territory, stabilizing the local population while it naturally ages and declines. A colony well managed through TNR tends to reduce progressively over years, without generating the vacuum that would attract new individuals. Concrete benefits include fewer litters, less noise from mating and fights, reduced territorial marking by unneutered males, and overall improvement in the health of remaining animals.

Turkey as a case study: 20 years of TNR and the 2024 reversal

Turkey represented, until recently, one of the most cited examples of successful coexistence between humans and street animals in an urban environment. The 2004 animal protection law — Article 6/4 — required municipalities to capture, neuter, vaccinate, and return animals to their place of origin. In 20 years of this policy, estimates indicate that more than 2.5 million dogs were neutered through the program. Cities like Istanbul became global references for coexistence — the documentary Kedi (2017) and the worldwide fame of cats like Gli and Tombili are expressions of this culture.

In July 2024, this model was abandoned. The Turkish parliament approved a new animal protection law proposed by the ruling AKP party, which mandated the removal of all stray animals from the streets and their confinement in municipal shelters. The word "euthanasia" was removed from the final text after massive protests — but remained implicit through reference to veterinary legislation that allows the procedure in cases of incurable disease, aggressive behavior, or public health risk.

The structural problem was immediate and obvious: the 322 existing shelters had a total capacity for approximately 105,000 animals. The estimated stray dog population was 4 million. Municipalities were legally required to build new shelters by 2028, with a minimum investment of 0.3% of annual budget — but in the short term, there was nowhere to place the captured animals.

The results were rapid and disturbing. Reports from cities like Niğde and the Altındağ district of Ankara documented animals dying in shelters — some in large numbers, buried or left in the open. Dog carcasses were found in bags at garbage dumps. Syringes were identified beside bodies found along roads. Shelter veterinarians admitted, in some cases anonymously, that animals were being put down under pretexts — such as "negative behavior" or "disease" — due to the impossibility of housing them.

Turkey's Veterinary Association and animal rights organizations denounced the situation. The opposition brought the case to the Constitutional Court. In May 2025, the court rejected the annulment request, ruling the law constitutionally valid — increasing pressure on municipalities to enforce it. Opposition municipalities, including Istanbul, announced resistance to enforcement while awaiting judicial outcomes.

Brigitte Bardot personally wrote to President Erdoğan asking that Turkey — which she described as "seen until now as a model of compassion for stray cats and dogs, living harmoniously with humans" — not become synonymous with institutionalized cruelty.

What the Turkish experience teaches

The Turkish case is an empirical demonstration of something science had already documented in theory: removing animals without replacing them with an effective population control program does not solve the problem — and can create conditions of suffering at an unprecedented scale.

The comparison with Bhutan is illustrative. The country initially tried to contain its stray dog population through shelters — and was "horrified by the results," according to its own government reports. It switched to the CNVR model (Capture, Neuter, Vaccinate, Return — equivalent to TNR), neutered more than 70% of its stray dogs with NGO support, and reported sustained positive outcomes.

The TNR method, with the ear tip as universal identifier, the colony caretaker as field agent, and mass neutering as population control tool, is not a perfect solution. It is slow, requires long-term commitment, and demands coordination between public authorities, veterinary clinics, and the community. But it is the only method that effectively stabilizes populations without generating the cycle of suffering that more drastic alternatives produce.

And for the cats living in urban colonies managed through TNR — vaccinated, neutered, fed, with the ear tip that tells the world someone saw them — it is simply the difference between a dignified life and a disposable existence.
