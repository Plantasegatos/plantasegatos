Louis Wain: The Cats, the Illness and the Myth

Perhaps few people have ever looked at cats quite the way Louis Wain did.

Where someone else saw an animal sleeping on a cushion, he seemed to see a personality. Where there was a cat sitting at a table, an entire little society emerged. His cats played, talked, argued, celebrated, performed music, drank tea and, above all, seemed to conduct their feline lives with delightfully human seriousness.

But before there were the thousands of cats that would make Louis Wain famous, there was one cat.

His name was Peter.

The cat beside the bed

Louis Wain was born in London in 1860. He studied art and worked as an illustrator, drawing a wide range of subjects for newspapers and magazines. Cats were not yet the center of his universe.

That began to change after his marriage to Emily Richardson.

Not long after they married, Emily became seriously ill. During her illness, the couple's black-and-white cat, Peter, became an important presence in their home. Wain began drawing him.

He drew Peter in different poses, situations and little scenes that could amuse Emily. There is something deeply moving about that: before Wain's cats delighted the public, one cat — and the drawings inspired by him — helped bring some comfort to a woman who was ill.

Emily died in 1887, only a few years after their marriage.

Peter, however, would continue to live through Wain's work.

When cats began behaving like people

Over the following decades, Louis Wain turned the cat into a protagonist.

His animals became increasingly anthropomorphic. They walked on two legs, wore clothes, attended parties, played sports, performed music and recreated, with enormous humor, the rituals and absurdities of British society.

They were not merely drawings of cats.

They were little human comedies performed by cats.

From the late nineteenth century into the early twentieth, his images appeared in books, magazines, newspapers, postcards and annuals. The “Louis Wain cat” became instantly recognizable.

And perhaps this is an important part of his legacy: Wain helped a generation see cats not merely as useful animals, but as companions, characters and individuals.

Then the story becomes more difficult

Wain's own life was not as cheerful as many of his illustrations.

He experienced financial hardship and later developed severe behavioral and mental-health problems. In 1924 he was admitted to Springfield Hospital. The following year, after admirers discovered his circumstances and campaigned on his behalf, he was transferred to Bethlem Royal Hospital.

In 1930, Wain was transferred again, this time to Napsbury Hospital, where he remained until his death in 1939.

He was diagnosed with schizophrenia in his own time. Retrospectively assessing a psychiatric diagnosis made around a century ago requires considerable caution, and the nature of his condition has subsequently remained a subject of discussion.

One thing, however, is clear:

Louis Wain did not stop drawing.

The cats continued

Even during his years in hospitals, Wain continued making art until close to the end of his life.

And some of that work is extraordinary.

Some cats remain recognizable, figurative and familiar. Others seem to explode into geometric patterns, intense colors and almost kaleidoscopic structures. The face of the cat remains, but lines, symmetry and pattern begin to dominate the image around it.

They are fascinating works.

And from them emerged one of the most famous — and problematic — stories about Louis Wain.

The famous sequence of cats

You have probably seen the image.

Several cats are placed side by side. The first is relatively naturalistic. Then patterns appear. The forms become increasingly complex. Finally, the cat almost disappears inside a geometric explosion.

For decades, this sequence was presented as though it were a visual record of the deterioration of Louis Wain's mental state.

The more abstract the cat, the worse the artist was presumed to have become.

It is a powerful story.

The problem is that there is no evidence supporting that chronology.

The eight cats and Walter Maclay

In 1939, psychiatrist Walter Maclay came across eight Louis Wain cat paintings and arranged them according to a chronology that he himself assumed.

Maclay was interested in the relationship between mental illness and art, and interpreted the sequence as demonstrating progressive disorganization associated with schizophrenia.

But there was a fundamental problem:

the works did not carry a chronology supporting that sequence.

The order had not been established by Louis Wain.

It was imposed later by someone else.

Yet the idea was so visually compelling that it survived for decades. The so-called Kaleidoscope Cats were repeatedly reproduced as though they were a psychiatric case history painted by Wain himself.

But art is not a laboratory test

Perhaps this is the most important part of the story.

We can acknowledge that Louis Wain experienced serious mental illness without reducing his entire artistic production to that illness.

We can recognize that his style changed without assuming that every stylistic change necessarily corresponds to a clinical stage.

We can look at his geometric cats and ask questions about color, composition, experimentation, perception and imagination — rather than pathology alone.

The fact that Wain continued producing different kinds of work itself makes problematic the simple narrative in which a naturalistic cat inevitably transforms, step by step, into an abstract one as the artist's mind supposedly deteriorates.

The illness was real.

The kaleidoscopic cats were real too.

What we cannot do is automatically use one as a measuring instrument for the other.

Perhaps we should return to Peter

After all this discussion of diagnosis, hospitals, abstraction and psychiatry, there is something comforting about returning to the beginning.

To a room.

To a sick woman.

To a man drawing.

And to a black-and-white cat named Peter.

Perhaps the most important thing about Louis Wain's story is not discovering the exact moment when his cats began acquiring impossible colors.

Perhaps it is realizing that throughout much of his life, while the world around him changed — sometimes brutally — the cats remained.

First as companionship.

Then as work.

Then as a language.

And finally, as an entire universe.

Some drank tea.

Some played the piano.

Some seemed to be made of electricity, stained glass and kaleidoscopes.

But perhaps we have spent too much time trying to diagnose those cats.

Sometimes, when standing before a work by Louis Wain, it may be better simply to do what we do when we encounter a real cat:

look.

And accept that not everything needs to be explained in order to be extraordinary.
