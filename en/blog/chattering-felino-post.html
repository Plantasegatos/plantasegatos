Feline Chattering: Why Do Cats Make the "Kekeke" Sound?

What is chattering

Anyone who lives with cats has seen it: the cat is at the window, spots a bird or an insect outside, and suddenly starts producing a rapid jaw tremor accompanied by a staccato sound — a repeated clicking or chittering, almost as if stuttering. In Brazil, the popular onomatopoeia became "kekeke". In English, the behavior is called chattering or chittering.

The movement involves the lower jaw vibrating at high frequency, usually accompanied by vocalization — a sound between a click and a chirp that varies from cat to cat. The behavior almost always occurs in response to a visually identified but inaccessible prey: a bird in a tree, a gecko on the wall, an insect flying behind the glass.

What science says

Despite being a widely observed behavior, chattering still lacks a definitive explanation consolidated in the ethological literature. The most accepted hypotheses coexist — and each reveals something different about the workings of the feline nervous system.

Predatory frustration

The most cited theory is behavioral in nature: chattering would be a frustration response. The cat identifies the prey, activates the predatory circuit, but encounters an insurmountable obstacle — the glass, the height, the distance. The hunting system is "cocked" with nowhere to fire, and chattering would be a discharge of that accumulated tension. Comparative ethology studies show that captive felids exhibit the behavior more frequently than free-living felids, which reinforces this interpretation.

Rehearsing the killing bite

Another well-documented hypothesis involves the biomechanics of hunting. Felids — including big cats — use a specific bite to kill small prey: the nape bite (also called killing bite), in which the canines are inserted between the cervical vertebrae of the prey to sever the spinal cord. Chattering would be an involuntary motor rehearsal of this movement, triggered by the mere sight of prey. It would be, in other words, the nervous system "practicing" the strike before the attack even occurs.

Vocal mimicry — the most intriguing hypothesis

In 2010, researchers from Instituto Pró-Carnívoros and the Wildlife Conservation Society recorded, in the Brazilian Amazon, a margay (Leopardus wiedii) imitating the call of a baby pied tamarin to attract the group. The sound was a precise replica of the monkeys' contact call. This finding led ethologists to revisit domestic chattering under a new light: could it be an attempt to mimic the sounds of prey — birdsong, insect chirping — to lure or distract the animal? The hypothesis is fascinating, but still lacks systematic studies in Felis catus.

Autonomic nervous system arousal

A more physiological line of thought interprets chattering as the result of intense sympathetic activation. At the peak of predatory excitement, the autonomic nervous system releases adrenaline and prepares the body for attack — heart rate increases, muscles tense, reflexes sharpen. The jaw tremor would be a side effect of this activation, similar to the trembling humans feel in moments of extreme adrenaline.

The role of sound

Chattering is not silent. The vocal component varies considerably: some cats produce a dry click, others a longer chirp, and others something close to a rhythmic ca-ca-ca. The vocalization appears to be involuntary — cats do not seem to "choose" to produce it — and there is anecdotal evidence that cats deaf from birth also exhibit the jaw movement without the sound component, suggesting that the sound is a byproduct, not the primary function of the behavior.

Chattering in big cats

The behavior is not exclusive to the domestic cat. Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus), pumas (Puma concolor), leopards (Panthera pardus), and other wild felids exhibit variants of chattering in predatory contexts. This indicates that the behavior has ancient evolutionary origins, predating domestication, and has been conserved across millions of years of feline evolution — which in itself suggests it serves some relevant adaptive function.

Is it a sign of stress?

A common concern among cat owners: does chattering mean the cat is suffering? The answer is generally no. The behavior is part of the normal feline ethogram and is not considered pathological. However, if a cat exhibits the behavior at very high frequency, combined with other signs such as lethargy, loss of appetite, or repetitive behaviors, a veterinary evaluation may be warranted to rule out chronic anxiety or insufficient stimulation.

The best way to address frequent chattering is to ensure adequate environmental enrichment: regular play sessions with prey-simulating toys, windows with safe visual access to the outdoors, and when possible, catios — outdoor enclosures that allow the cat to observe the environment safely.

Why this behavior survived domestication

The domestic cat has lived in association with humans for about 10,000 years, but its predatory brain remains essentially intact. Unlike dogs, which underwent profound cognitive changes through artificial selection, cats were domesticated in a much gentler way — and largely preserved the behavioral repertoire of their wild ancestor, Felis silvestris lybica. Chattering is one of these behavioral fossils: an echo of the savanna inside the apartment.
