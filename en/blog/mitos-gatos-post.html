3 Cat Myths You Need to Forget 🐾

By Plantas & Gatos — practical content for cat guardians and plant lovers

Domestic cats have lived alongside humans for more than 9,000 years, yet they still carry an aura of mystery that feeds countless popular beliefs. These ideas, passed down from generation to generation, often not only distort our understanding of these fascinating animals but can also harm their well-being.

Based on scientific evidence and the experience of veterinary specialists, let’s debunk three of the most persistent cat myths — and reveal what science really teaches us about our feline companions.

🐱 Myth 1: "Cats always land on their feet and never get hurt"

What science says

Cats possess an extraordinary ability called the “vestibular righting reflex,” first described by French physiologist Étienne-Jules Marey in 1894. This mechanism allows cats to reorient themselves midair in just 0.125 seconds, using:

  • Vestibular system: balance organ located in the inner ear
  • Flexible spine: 53 vertebrae (13 more than humans)
  • Absence of clavicle: enables greater shoulder mobility
  • Tail as a stabilizer: works like an aerodynamic rudder

The dangerous reality

Veterinary studies reveal alarming data about the so-called “High-Rise Syndrome” in cats:

Most dangerous falls:

  • 2nd to 6th floor: not high enough for the cat to fully position itself, yet high enough to cause serious trauma
  • Most common injuries: jaw fractures (20%), pneumothorax (15%), limb fractures (35%)
  • Survival rate: although high (90%), 37% of cats require surgery

Falls from higher floors:
Paradoxically, cats falling from above 9 floors often suffer fewer severe injuries. The explanation: once reaching terminal velocity (about 60 mph / 100 km/h), cats relax their bodies, distributing the impact more evenly.

Essential prevention

  • Install protective screens: a must in apartments above ground level
  • Beware of tilt windows: can cause strangulation
  • Hunting instinct: birds and insects can distract cats and lead to falls
  • Supervise balconies: even with protection, always monitor

🥛 Myth 2: "Cats love milk and need it to stay healthy"

The origin of the myth

The link between cats and milk dates back to the 18th century, when cats lived on farms and had access to fresh milk. This romantic image stuck in popular culture, but ignores a key biological fact.

The truth about lactose in cats

Development of intolerance:

  • Kittens: produce lactase (enzyme that digests lactose) until 8–12 weeks of age
  • Adult cats: 85% gradually lose the ability to produce lactase
  • Consequence: undigested lactose ferments in the intestine

Symptoms of intolerance:

  • Diarrhea (which may cause dehydration)
  • Excess gas
  • Abdominal cramps
  • Vomiting
  • In severe cases: imbalance of gut flora

Real nutritional needs

What cats truly need:

  • Water: 50–70 ml per kg of body weight daily
  • Animal protein: 26% of dry diet (compared to 18% for dogs)
  • Taurine: essential amino acid found only in animal tissue
  • Arachidonic acid: vital fatty acid found in animal fats

Safe alternatives:

  • Lactose-free milk made for cats: available in pet shops
  • Unsalted meat broth: occasionally
  • Fresh water: always the best choice for hydration

Signs of good hydration

Check hydration with the “skin tent test”: gently lift the skin on the back of the neck; it should return to normal within 2 seconds.

🌙 Myth 3: "Cats are cold, independent animals that don’t form bonds"

The science of feline bonding

Recent animal behavior research has revolutionized our understanding of feline attachment:

Oregon State University study (2019):

  • Secure attachment test: 65% of cats showed secure attachment to their guardians
  • Comparison: similar rates found in dogs (58%) and human children (65%)
  • Method: cats were separated and later reunited with guardians, while behavior was observed

Unique ways of showing affection

Cats express love in subtle, often misunderstood ways:

  • Purring: frequency of 20–50 Hz, with proven therapeutic properties
  • Slow blinking: the “cat kiss”
  • Kneading: a behavior carried over from kittenhood
  • Showing the belly: a sign of extreme trust
  • Following the guardian: positive territorial behavior
  • Bringing “gifts”: instinct to provide for the family group

Individual personalities

Studies identify five main feline personality types:

  1. Neurotic: anxious, insecure
  2. Extroverted: active, alert
  3. Dominant: territorial, aggressive
  4. Impulsive: erratic, reckless
  5. Agreeable: friendly, confident

Building healthy bonds

Evidence-based strategies:

  • Respect personal space: let the cat initiate contact
  • Establish routines: cats are creatures of habit
  • Provide environmental enrichment: scratchers, toys, vertical spaces
  • Use positive reinforcement: treats and affection for desired behavior
  • Create safe zones: places where cats can retreat undisturbed

Bonding timelines:

  • Kittens: 2–4 weeks for adaptation
  • Adult cats: 3–6 months for full trust
  • Rescue cats: up to 1 year depending on history

✨ The Impact of Cat Myths on Feline Health

Busting these myths goes beyond curiosity — it directly impacts feline health and well-being.

Consequences of persistent myths:

  • Preventable accidents: thousands of falls could be avoided yearly
  • Digestive problems: unnecessary treatments caused by lactose intolerance
  • Abandonment: unrealistic expectations about behavior lead to surrender

Benefits of scientific knowledge:

  • Effective prevention: evidence-based safety measures
  • Proper nutrition: diets tailored to real needs
  • Harmonious relationships: understanding feline communication signals

Modern science offers a fascinating and precise view of cats. The more we understand their real needs — based on evidence, not folklore — the better we can provide them with a full, healthy, and truly happy life.

Remember: every cat is a unique individual with its own personality and needs. Careful observation and respect for their natural traits are the keys to extraordinary coexistence between humans and felines.
