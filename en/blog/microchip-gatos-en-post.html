Microchip, GPS and AirTag: the definitive guide to cat identification

Introduction

Every week, desperate posts appear in pet owner groups asking the same question: "my cat escaped, I have an AirTag but the location isn't updating — what do I do?" The answer reveals a technological confusion that can be costly. A microchip is not GPS. An AirTag does not track in real time. And a dedicated GPS tracker with a dead battery is useless. Understanding exactly what each technology does — and what it does not do — is the first step in truly protecting your cat.

The most dangerous myth: "a microchip is GPS"

This is the most common and potentially most costly misconception a pet owner can have. A microchip does not emit signals, does not transmit location, and has no battery. It tracks nothing, anywhere, at any time.

A feline microchip is a passive transponder — a radiofrequency identification (RFID) device encapsulated in biocompatible glass, roughly the size of a grain of rice. It operates according to international standards ISO 11784 and ISO 11785, which define both the structure of the identification code and the communication protocol between the chip and the reader. The standard operating frequency is 134.2 kHz, and each chip carries a unique 15-digit numeric code — the first three digits identify the manufacturer.

The mechanism is elegantly simple: when a reader is brought near the animal, it emits an electromagnetic field that energizes the chip by induction. The chip then transmits its identification code. Without a reader, without an electromagnetic field, the chip remains completely inert forever. This is simultaneously its greatest limitation and its greatest strength: no battery, no maintenance, no failure from discharge — with an estimated lifespan of over 20 years.

What a microchip actually does — and does very well

A microchip is a tool for permanent, inviolable identification. When a lost animal arrives at a veterinary clinic, shelter, or animal control post, the code read by the scanner is checked against a database that returns the owner's name and contact information. Collars fall off, tattoos fade, documents get lost. Microchips don't.

The microchip also serves as legal proof of ownership. In cases of theft, irregular adoption, or custody disputes, the chip number registered in the owner's name is the most robust document available. No Bluetooth tracker offers this level of legal validity.

AirTag, Samsung SmartTag and similar devices: what they are and where they fail

The Apple AirTag and Samsung Galaxy SmartTag are object trackers that operate via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) combined with Ultra-Wideband (UWB) technology. They do not have their own GPS.

The AirTag emits a Bluetooth signal detected by nearby Apple devices — iPhones, iPads, Macs belonging to anyone in the "Find My" network. That anonymous device sends the encrypted location to iCloud, which forwards it to the owner. The Samsung SmartTag operates similarly, within the Android ecosystem and the SmartThings Find network.

The practical consequence is straightforward: if your cat escapes to an area without a density of Apple (or Android) users, the device simply stops updating the location. Parks without foot traffic, low-density neighborhoods, rural areas, or quiet streets in the early morning hours render these tags virtually useless. Apple itself acknowledges the product's limitations and does not recommend it for tracking animals or vehicles. The Bluetooth range for precise UWB tracking is approximately 30 meters — meaning the owner must already be physically close to the animal.

There is also a concrete physical risk: documented reports exist of animals swallowing an AirTag that was hanging from their collar like a pendant. The CR2032 battery contains toxic substances. The device must be securely embedded in the collar, never hanging loosely.

The Samsung SmartTag shares the same structural problem: without nearby Android users, there is no location update. Its potentially broader coverage in markets with higher Android penetration does not eliminate this dependency on third parties.

Real GPS: a different category altogether

Dedicated GPS trackers for pets — such as Tractive, Weenect, Whistle, or Marco Polo — are an entirely different category. They have a satellite receiver (GPS/GNSS), mobile network connectivity (3G/4G), and a rechargeable battery. They offer real-time tracking independent of other users, but require a monthly subscription and periodic recharging. A cat wearing a GPS collar with a dead battery is a cat without tracking — and cats have a talent for getting lost precisely when the battery is low.

SinPatinhas and the Brazilian landscape

In April 2025, the Brazilian federal government launched SinPatinhas — the National Domestic Animal Registry System — developed under the National Program for Ethical Population Management of Dogs and Cats (ProPatinhas). The system is accessed at sinpatinhas.mma.gov.br via a Gov.br account, free of charge.

Brazil has approximately 62.2 million dogs and 30.8 million cats, with around 35% living on the streets or in shelters — figures that justify the initiative. Registration generates an Animal ID with a unique QR Code that can be attached to the collar. Anyone who finds the animal simply points their phone camera at the code and immediately locates the owner, without any special equipment.

Importantly, SinPatinhas accepts microchipped and non-microchipped animals alike. If the animal has a microchip, the number can be added to the registration, strengthening identification. Registration is voluntary for most owners — it is only mandatory for those using federal public funds for microchipping or neutering. The QR Code on the collar can be removed; the microchip cannot.

International landscape

The Brazilian model aligns with similar initiatives around the world. In the United Kingdom, microchipping has been mandatory for dogs since 2016 and for cats since June 2024, with registration in government-accredited databases such as Petlog (the country's largest) and PETtrac. These systems integrate with the European network EuroPetNet, enabling cross-border chip queries across the European Union.

In Ireland, the four approved databases synchronize data daily with EuroPetNet. In Australia, each state maintains its own registry, with growing national interoperability. In the United States, the system is fragmented — there are multiple private registries (HomeAgain, AKC Reunite, Found Animals) with no unified federal database, creating operational challenges for shelters finding animals from other states.

Microchipping and travel: a real requirement, not optional

For owners who plan to travel with their cats — whether for a permanent move or an extended trip — the microchip goes from being a recommendation to a legal requirement in most international destinations.

In Brazil, CFMV Resolution 1.321/2020, Article 2, Section VII, requires microchipping for international travel, along with supporting documentation. The Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock, and Food Supply (MAPA) requires a microchip for issuing the International Veterinary Certificate (CVI). The device must comply with the ISO 11784/11785 standard, be active, and be linked to the owner's updated information. Without this identification, the animal may be denied boarding or entry into the destination country.

There is a critical chronological rule that many owners are unaware of: the microchip must be implanted before or on the same day as the rabies vaccination to be accepted in the European Union and the United States. Microchipping the animal days before the trip does not resolve the issue if the rabies vaccination has already been administered — all health documentation must be linked to the same identification number.

Requirements vary by destination. For Europe, cats need a microchip, rabies vaccination, a 30-day waiting period, and a rabies serology test before departure. For Japan, the process is even more demanding: two doses of rabies vaccine with a 30-day interval after microchipping, serology, and six months of quarantine — a preparation process that can exceed seven months. For Mercosur countries, the microchip must comply with ISO 11784 and/or ISO 11785 standards, with the anatomical location of the chip specified in the CVI.

No Bluetooth tracker substitutes the microchip in this context. AirTags and SmartTags are invisible to health authorities, customs officers, and border veterinarians. The microchip is the only internationally recognized identifier under veterinary and legal protocols.

The ideal strategy: complementary layers

Microchips and trackers are not competitors — they are layers of protection with distinct functions. The microchip is permanent, passive, and legally valid: it does not track, but it identifies definitively. The AirTag or SmartTag is useful in dense urban environments as a supplementary search tool — with a clear awareness of its limitations. Dedicated GPS offers active real-time tracking, but depends on battery and a subscription. The SinPatinhas QR Code adds an accessible layer for any smartphone, without special equipment.

For an indoor cat that occasionally escapes, the combination of microchip + SinPatinhas registration + QR Code on the collar covers the vast majority of scenarios. For cats with outdoor access or an adventurous profile, a rechargeable GPS tracker may be considered — always as a complement, never as a substitute for the microchip.
