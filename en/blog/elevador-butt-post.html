Elevator Butt: What Science Says About This Feline Reflex

You touch the base of your cat's tail and, almost instantly, the chest drops, the hips rise, and the tail lifts or curves to one side. It looks deliberate — but it isn't. This common gesture has a name in animal behavior literature: lordosis, popularly known in English as "elevator butt." Understanding what's behind it dismantles a few of the most repeated myths about cats.

A reflex, not a decision

Lordosis is described as the natural body posture associated with sexual receptivity in females of most mammals, including cats — characterized by lowering of the forelimbs, extension of the hind limbs with elevation of the hips, ventral arching of the spine, and raising or sideward displacement of the tail. The important point is that this arch is part of a reflex circuit: sensory information from the lumbar and sacral region travels up the spinal cord to brain structures that coordinate the response with the animal's postural balance. In other words, the cat isn't "choosing" to make this movement — the body responds to the stimulus almost involuntarily.

Why the base of the tail is so sensitive

The area concentrates dense nerve endings and pheromone-producing glands, including the so-called supracaudal gland. A pioneering 2002 study tested the behavioral response of nine cats to touch in different glandular areas — perioral, temporal, and caudal — and found more positive reactions in the temporal region (between the eyes and ears) and more negative reactions in the caudal region, around the tail. A later replication, with a larger sample and tighter methodological controls, confirmed that the caudal region tends to produce the most negative behavioral scores during handling, regardless of how familiar the cat is with the person touching it.

This doesn't mean every cat dislikes being touched there — it means the region is neurologically "loaded": intensely pleasurable for some cats, quickly uncomfortable for others. It's worth reading your own cat's body language rather than assuming a universal rule.

Myth: "it's always a sign of heat"

Partly false. True estrus-related lordosis requires hormonal priming and only occurs in intact (non-spayed) females, in response to stimulation of the flanks and perineal region — normally provided by a male during mating. Neutered males and spayed females also perform "elevator butt" when petted at the base of the tail, but through a different mechanism: it's a sensory reflex tied to nerve sensitivity in the area, not reproductive hormonal motivation.

True estrus lordosis has its own, more specific signs: the tail shifts to the side (rather than standing straight up), there's insistent, repetitive vocalization, the hind paws continuously "tread," and the pattern repeats cyclically every few weeks. If you recognize this pattern in an unspayed female, it's time to talk to your veterinarian about spaying.

Trust, communication, and a bit of kitten nostalgia

Among cats, exposing the hindquarters is a gesture of vulnerability — part of the same social repertoire as the raised tail used when approaching someone trusted. It also facilitates scent exchange between individuals, something central to feline communication through scent glands. A complementary behavioral hypothesis suggests the reflex has roots in kittenhood: kittens raise their hindquarters so the mother can groom them and stimulate elimination, and this neurological pattern may remain wired in, reactivated in adulthood as a response to pleasant touch.

Even veterinary routine benefits from it

This reflex is put to practical use in clinics: touching the base of the tail makes it easier to access the perianal region during routine exams, since the cat spontaneously lifts and holds the area still. It's also why professionals watch closely when this behavior changes — sudden hypersensitivity or self-trauma in the tail region can signal a condition called feline hyperesthesia syndrome, which requires veterinary evaluation.

In practice

If your cat seeks out and enjoys being touched at the base of the tail, there's no reason for guilt — it's genuine comfort. If it reacts poorly (ears back, tail lashing, attempting to bite), the best move is simply to stop and avoid pushing it: reacting with laughter or scolding can actually reinforce the unwanted behavior rather than reduce it. Every cat has its own threshold, and respecting it is the safest — and most affectionate — way to engage with this curious little gesture.
