Feline Takeover: Why Cats Are Conquering Modern Life

For a long time, dogs almost completely dominated our idea of the traditional companion animal. A house, a yard, a family and a dog seemed to belong naturally together.

But something is changing.

Dogs still outnumber cats in Brazil, yet the feline population has been growing at a faster proportional rate. In 2024, Brazil reached approximately 32.2 million cats, an increase of about 4.5% from the previous year. Dogs reached 63.7 million, remaining the clear majority but growing at a slower proportional rate.

The interesting question, therefore, may not be whether cats are going to “replace” dogs.

It is this:

why does the cat seem to fit so remarkably well into the way we now live?

Our cities have changed

Brazilian life has become increasingly urban and vertical. According to the 2022 Brazilian Census, 12.5% of the population lived in apartments, compared with 8.5% in 2010.

Brazil is still predominantly a country of houses, but the figures reveal an important change in the way people live.

In compact environments, several natural feline characteristics become particularly useful. Cats use space not only horizontally but vertically. Shelves, elevated resting areas, protected windows and climbing structures can dramatically increase the territory available to them.

For a cat, a well-designed small home can provide more environmental opportunities than a larger but poorly structured space.

Our families have changed too

Brazilian households have also undergone significant changes. The 2022 Census shows a growing presence of couples without children and other household arrangements, while traditional households composed of couples and children have proportionally declined.

More people are also living alone or in households formed by only one or two people.

It would be tempting to conclude that fewer children automatically mean more cats. The evidence, however, does not support such a simple causal claim.

What we can say is that smaller households, new family structures and the growth of cat ownership are occurring at the same time.

Our routines are different

Hybrid work, long commutes, variable schedules and limited free time have also changed our relationship with the home.

In this context, some feline characteristics may offer practical advantages.

Cats use litter boxes, do not depend on outdoor walks for elimination, can live entirely indoors when their environment is properly designed and possess a degree of behavioral autonomy.

But one distinction is essential:

autonomy does not mean having no needs.

Cats still require play, opportunities to express hunting behavior, elevated resting areas, hiding places, properly distributed resources, social interaction, veterinary care and meaningful relationships with their humans.

A cat may fit certain modern routines particularly well. That does not mean a cat can simply be left alone and forgotten.

Cats or dogs?

Turning this discussion into a competition between species would miss the point.

Dogs and cats have different evolutionary histories, behavioral repertoires and needs.

For many people, walking a dog every day, sharing outdoor activities and maintaining an active social routine with the animal is precisely part of the relationship they want.

For others, feline autonomy, indoor living and the ability to use vertical space may fit their circumstances better.

There is no winner.

There is compatibility between species, environment and lifestyle.

But convenience cannot explain everything

If the rise of cats were merely the consequence of smaller apartments and busier schedules, we would be leaving out perhaps the most important part of the story.

Cats have also acquired a new emotional status.

Research into human-cat relationships reveals complex emotional bonds. Studies involving thousands of cat owners have identified relationships involving friendship, strong emotional investment, proximity and social support.

Another study involving approximately 1,800 cat owners found that many described their cats as family members, children or best friends. These perceptions were also associated with the way cats participated in everyday household life.

The cat is no longer merely inside the house.

It is on the bed, on the sofa, beside the computer during meetings, in family photographs, during moves and in everyday decisions.

The cat has become part of our personal histories.

And a feline economy emerged

This emotional transformation also moves money.

Brazil's pet market generated approximately R$75.4 billion in revenue in 2024. This figure includes dogs, cats and other companion animals, so it should not be interpreted as the feline market alone. Nevertheless, the growing cat population supports increasingly specialized categories.

Cat-specific food, litter, water fountains, scratching posts, furniture, window protection, environmental enrichment, feline medicine, health plans, monitoring technology, boarding, pet sitting and countless specialized products now form an extensive economic chain.

The animal that once shared barns, ports and early human settlements with us now influences architecture, design, veterinary medicine, technology and consumer behavior.

Maybe cats have not changed

There is something fascinating about this story.

Cats still carry many of the characteristics they have always possessed: relative independence, curiosity, territoriality, a remarkable ability to use three-dimensional space and a very particular way of forming social relationships.

Perhaps the greatest transformation occurred on the other side of the relationship.

Our cities changed.

Our homes changed.

Our families changed.

Our routines changed.

And in this new world, we discovered that the small feline that began living alongside humans thousands of years ago seems remarkably well suited to the twenty-first century.

Maybe cats have not changed. We have.
