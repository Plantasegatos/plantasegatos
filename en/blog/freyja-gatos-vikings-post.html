Freyja and the Viking Cats: Mythology, Archaeology and the Animal the Norse Revered

The goddess who chose cats

In Norse cosmology, every deity has their sacred animal. Odin has his ravens Huginn and Muninn. Thor has his goats Tanngrisnir and Tanngnjóstr. But it is Freyja — goddess of love, fertility, magic, and war — who makes the most improbable and most fascinating choice: two large cats, grey or blue, who pull her chariot across the skies of Ásgarðr.

The reference appears in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, compiled in the 13th century from much older oral traditions: "And when she travels, she drives two cats and sits in a chariot." Simple, direct, unadorned. As if it were the most natural thing in the world for the most powerful goddess of the Vanir pantheon to choose felines as her divine mode of transport.

The cats are often identified in modern sources by the names Bygul and Trjegul — but an important caveat applies here: these names have no ancient Norse origin. They were coined by the American author Diana Paxson in the 20th century and widely adopted by popular culture. The primary Norse sources do not name them. What exists in the Eddas is the image: the goddess and the cats, inseparable.

Who is Freyja — and why cats make sense

Freyja belongs to the Vanir — one of the two groups of Norse deities, distinct from the Æsir of Odin and Thor. She is the goddess of fertility, erotic love, beauty, and wealth — but also of seiðr, the magic of divination and transformation that the Norse associated with the feminine, the hidden, and the dangerous. She leads the Valkyries on the battlefield and holds the unique privilege of choosing half of the fallen warriors before Odin claims his share — taking them to Fólkvangr, her hall in the divine fields.

She is a goddess of paradoxes: sensuality and war, fertility and death, domesticity and wild magic. And the cat — unpredictable, independent, a born hunter that accepts human presence on its own terms — mirrors those paradoxes with near-perfect precision.

The World History Encyclopedia records that the cat was considered as unpredictable as life itself — exactly the quality Freyja embodied as goddess of fate and luck. It was the only animal that combined the domesticity of the hearth with the ferocity of the wild predator; that purred on a woman's lap and hunted rats in the barn on the same afternoon. For the Norse, this was not contradiction — it was power.

The Skogkatt: the cat that could pull a chariot

Norse sources describe Freyja's cats as large and powerful — far larger than a common domestic cat. This points to a specific breed that had existed in Scandinavia for millennia: the skogkatt, the Norwegian Forest Cat.

The Skogkatt emerged from Scandinavian forests approximately 4,000 years ago, most likely from short-haired cats from southern Europe that progressively adapted to the extreme northern climate. The result is a robustly built feline with water-resistant double coat, broad snow-adapted paws, and a frame that can easily exceed 7 or 8 kg — dimensions that, in the mythic imagination of a people who sailed the northern seas and hunted bears, made perfectly plausible the idea of two such animals pulling a goddess's chariot.

There is also the hypothesis that Freyja's cats may be interpreted as lynxes — larger wild felines that inhabited medieval Scandinavia — or as mythical creatures blending traits of cat, panther, and lynx. Some modern Norse jewelers, such as the Swedish jeweler Freyathor with their "Lynx of Freya" collection, adopt this reading, using the lynx as a unifying symbol of all the mythical felines of the Norse pantheon.

Feeding the cat well was good luck for the wedding

The relationship between Freyja and cats was not merely mythical — it had practical consequences in daily Viking life. There was a popular belief recorded by the German mythologist Jakob Grimm: if a bride walked to her wedding in fine weather, it was said she had "fed the cat well" — meaning she had pleased Freyja through her generosity toward the sacred animal.

It was also tradition to give a cat (or more) to newlyweds as a wedding gift — a symbol of Freyja's blessings on the new household. The cat was, in this context, a living intermediary between the human and the divine: treating it well cultivated the goddess's favor; mistreating it meant risking her wrath.

This belief had curiously practical effects. In a society where killing a cat was considered bad luck, feline populations were informally protected by religious superstition — which also made them more effective at controlling rodents in Viking barns and ships.

What archaeology says — and where it contradicts the myth

Here the story becomes more complex and more interesting. Matthias S. Toplak, director of the Viking Museum Hedeby and researcher at the University of Tübingen, published in 2019 in Current Swedish Archaeology a thorough re-evaluation of the role of cats in the Viking Age — and the archaeological findings reveal a reality more ambivalent than the myth suggests.

On one hand, there is clear evidence that cats were slaughtered for their fur. On the bones found at the Viking trading center of Haithabu and other northern European settlements, cut marks consistent with skinning show that cat fur — especially white cat fur — was highly valued during the Norse Middle Ages. The famous sorceress in the Saga of Eirik the Red wears black cat skin gloves and a cat skin-lined hat — the ritual garb of a völva, a practitioner of seiðr.

On the other hand, at Birka — the primary Viking trading center from the 8th to 10th centuries, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site in Sweden — cat bones found there show no cut marks. And something even more revealing: cats appear in Viking burials alongside warriors and high-status women. Sometimes they are the only grave good present. Over time, by the late Viking Age, cat bones begin appearing in more common burials too — indicating that cats had established themselves as a regular element of the domestic fauna of Scandinavia.

At Birka, an amber figurine in the shape of a cat was also found — most likely a children's toy. And the gripping beast motif, ubiquitous in Viking art of the 9th and 10th centuries in the so-called Borre Style, presents triangular features and characteristic claws that researchers like Prehal associate with the domestic cat — especially the white cat, possibly linked to ritual magic.

Cats, seiðr and the magical feminine

The association between cats and magic in Norse culture is documented in multiple sagas. Seiðr — the magical practice of divination, transformation, and destiny manipulation — was primarily a feminine domain, and Freyja was its original and most powerful practitioner. Cats, with their ability to see in the dark, their unpredictable behavior, and their connection to the nocturnal world, were naturally associated with this universe of hidden forces.

Toplak, however, makes an important methodological caveat: most of the sagas and Norse literature were transcribed in the 13th century — when Christianity was already the dominant religion in Scandinavia and pre-Christian beliefs were increasingly demonized. The negative association of cats with witchcraft and evil forces that appears in some medieval texts may therefore not reflect Viking Age beliefs at all — but rather the Christian reinterpretation of Norse traditions.

In other words: the same animal Freyja chose as a divine companion may have been gradually transformed, through the lens of medieval Christianity, into a symbol of witchcraft and danger — the same process that would occur far more dramatically in Western Europe centuries later, during the witch persecutions.

Cats on Viking ships: the domestication that sailed the world

Beyond the home and the temple, Viking cats had a strategic role: they sailed. The presence of cats on Viking longships was not accidental — it was functional. Protecting food supplies from rats and mice during weeks-long voyages was just as critical at sea as on land. And cats, with their low energy requirements and high predatory efficiency, were the perfect pest control for vessels.

There is archaeological and genetic evidence that Vikings transported cats to Greenland. It is possible — though unproven — that cats were aboard Leif Erikson's ships when he reached North America around the year 1000. A genetic study from 2016, presented at the 7th International Symposium on Biomolecular Archaeology in Oxford by researcher Eva-Maria Geigl, analyzed the remains of over 200 cats from 30 archaeological sites across Africa and Eurasia — and confirmed that Vikings played a significant role in the dispersal of domesticated cats along northern European trade routes.

The cat that Freyja chose to pull her divine chariot also traveled with her worshippers across the most feared seas of the medieval world. There was poetry in that — even if unintentional.

The legacy that persists

The Skogkatt — the Norwegian Forest Cat, the likely living model for Freyja's cats — nearly disappeared in the 20th century. Crossbreeding with short-haired domestic cats was bringing the breed to extinction. It was only in the 1970s that Norwegian breeders, led by figures such as Helene Nordane and Edel Runas, launched a preservation program that saved it. In 1977, the Norwegian Forest Cat was officially recognized for European competition. Today it is one of the most popular breeds in Scandinavia — and notably, in 2023, ranked as the 4th most registered breed in the FIFe European registry.

On Malaya Sadovaya Street in St. Petersburg, two bronze cats named Yeliseyev and Vasilisa watch over the city from the building facades — descendants of the Siberian cats that saved the Hermitage from rats during the Siege of Leningrad. They are not Freyja's cats, but they belong to the same mythical lineage: felines that, through their singular nature, became something more than animals — they became symbols.

Perhaps that is what Freyja saw in them. Not servants, not tools. Partners — who accept her company on their own terms, and are therefore worthy of pulling the chariot of a goddess.
