Miyazaki's Cats: When the Everyday Opens a Door to the Fantastic

There is something profoundly feline about the worlds Hayao Miyazaki helped create.

Perhaps it is their independence. Perhaps it is the way a cat can remain beside us while still preserving a part of itself that never completely belongs to us. Or perhaps it is simply that familiar feeling, known to anyone who has lived with a cat, that they sometimes notice things we cannot see.

In Studio Ghibli films, that quality seems to take physical form. Cats cross rooms, cities, forests and stories as though they know passages that remain invisible to us.

Sometimes, all we have to do is follow.

Before the fantasy, there is a cat

Part of the magic is that Ghibli's cats rarely need to stop behaving like cats.

They watch. They wait. They sleep while everyone else is busy. They choose their company. They disappear and return as though nothing remarkable happened at all.

And that is precisely where the fantastic begins.

Secondary accounts mention Ushiko, a cat associated with the Studio Ghibli environment, among the real feline references remembered around the studio's productions. Direct links between Ushiko and specific characters, however, are less firmly documented in official material.

Perhaps the most beautiful way to understand the story is not as the origin of one particular character, but as evidence of something larger: real cats moved through the same everyday spaces where artists were drawing imaginary worlds.

Jiji: the voice that accompanies childhood

In Kiki's Delivery Service, released in 1989, Jiji accompanies a young witch who must leave home and discover who she is when nobody is there to tell her exactly what to do.

Jiji talks, questions, complains and watches. At times, he feels like the intimate voice still accompanying Kiki as she crosses the border between childhood and independence.

And then something changes.

As Kiki grows, her relationship with Jiji changes as well. It is one of the film's great subtleties: growing up does not necessarily mean losing what came before. Sometimes it means learning to hear the world differently.

Jiji remains a cat.

Perhaps Kiki is the one who is no longer quite the same.

Catbus: when nobody needs to explain the impossible

A year earlier, in My Neighbor Totoro, Miyazaki had already taken the feline idea somewhere even more improbable.

Catbus has too many legs, glowing eyes, the body of a cat and the purpose of a bus. It runs along routes that seem not to exist for the rest of the world.

Another kind of film might try to explain such a creature.

Ghibli does not need to.

Catbus simply exists.

Perhaps childhood can still accept a truth adults gradually forget: something does not have to be possible before it can become true inside a story.

Moon: follow the cat

In Whisper of the Heart, released in 1995 and directed by Yoshifumi Kondō from a screenplay and storyboards by Hayao Miyazaki, something much quieter happens.

Shizuku encounters a large cat riding a train by itself.

She could simply have found it curious and continued on her way.

Instead, she follows it.

The cat, known as Moon, leads her through streets she might have walked on any ordinary day. Yet on this particular day, they lead to an antique shop.

Inside that shop there is Baron.

There is Seiji.

There is a story waiting to be written.

Moon does not have to speak. He needs no supernatural powers. He does not even have to know that he is changing Shizuku's life.

He simply does what real cats do every day: chooses a path and keeps going.

We are the ones who decide to follow.

Baron: when a figurine begins to breathe inside the imagination

Baron Humbert von Gikkingen first appears as an elegant feline figurine inside the antique shop.

To someone in a hurry, he would be merely an object.

To Shizuku, he becomes a character.

This is one of the most beautiful ideas in Whisper of the Heart: something we encounter in the real world may continue living inside us until it becomes something entirely new.

A song can become a memory. A shop can become a setting. A cat can become a guide. A small figurine can acquire an entire life.

Imagination does not have to escape everyday life.

It can begin precisely there.

Muta and a kingdom where cats finally rule

In 2002, The Cat Returns, directed by Hiroyuki Morita, took the idea even further.

Baron returns. Muta gains a much larger presence. And what once seemed to pass quietly through our world is finally given a world of its own.

There is an entire feline society.

There is a king, a court, rules, subjects, ceremonies, dangers and ambitions that exist independently of humans.

It is a wonderful reversal.

For a moment, we stop thinking of the cat as a supporting character in our homes and are invited to consider the opposite possibility:

what if we are merely supporting characters in theirs?

What makes these cats so special?

Perhaps it is the fact that Miyazaki and Studio Ghibli's artists rarely need to transform cats completely in order to make them magical.

They begin with qualities we already recognize.

Independence.
Curiosity.
Silence.
The ability to disappear.
The watchful gaze.
Trust offered according to rules of their own.

Anyone who lives with cats knows this paradox.

They may sleep on the same sofa every day and still appear perfectly capable of having a second life about which we know absolutely nothing.

We may know when they ask for food, their favorite window and even the particular meow they use to call us.

Yet something remains unreadable.

And perhaps that small distance is precisely what makes cats such perfect characters for a universe in which the border between reality and fantasy is never fully closed.

The fantastic begins where certainty ends

Ghibli's worlds do not tell us that we must abandon reality to find the extraordinary.

Quite often, they tell us the opposite.

Look more closely.

Perhaps something is happening on that street. Perhaps a creature is hiding in that forest. Perhaps an old shop contains a story. Perhaps the cat that boarded the train knows exactly where it is going.

This may be one reason so many of us continue to find comfort in these films long after childhood.

They preserve a possibility that ordinary life often tries to erase: that there are still things we do not completely understand.

Cats seem perfectly comfortable living in that space.

They live with us, yet they never seem to belong entirely to our world.

Perhaps that is why they fit Miyazaki's universe so beautifully.

A cat does not need to cross a portal in order to seem mysterious.

It can simply sit in front of a window.

Looking at something we cannot see.

And for a few seconds, making us ask the question that seems to echo through so many Studio Ghibli stories:

what if there is something on the other side?

Perhaps that is why following a cat in a Ghibli film is almost never simply following a cat.

It is accepting an invitation.

It is surrendering, for a moment, the certainty that we already know every path.

It is recognizing that an invisible door may still be right beside us.

And that sometimes, all we need to find it...

is the courage to follow the cat.
