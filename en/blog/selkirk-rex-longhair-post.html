Selkirk Rex Longhair: The Cat in Lamb's Clothing

In 1987, at an animal shelter in Montana, a stray kitten with curly fur and twisted whiskers caught the attention of breeder Jeri Newman. The cat, named Miss DePesto, was not just another case of unusual coat — she carried a genetic mutation that had never been documented in the feline world. What Newman discovered when she crossed Miss DePesto with a black Persian on July 4, 1988 would change the history of cat breeding: of the six kittens born, three had curly coats. The curl was not recessive. It was dominant.

Thus was born the Selkirk Rex — the only feline breed to carry a person's name, honoring Jeri Newman's stepfather. But what makes this breed truly singular is not just its cinematic origin. It is the biology behind every strand of fur.

The Genetics of the Curl: Why the Selkirk Differs from All Other Rex Breeds

When we talk about "Rex cats," most people think of the Cornish Rex and the Devon Rex — equally curly breeds, but fundamentally different in their genetics. Both the Cornish and the Devon carry recessive genes for the curl: this means that for a kitten to be born with a wavy coat, both parents must carry the mutation. The practical result is that when crossed with straight-haired cats, the entire litter is born straight-coated.

The Selkirk Rex works in the opposite way. Its curl gene is autosomal dominant: just one parent needs to carry the mutation for some of the litter to be born with curls. It is therefore perfectly normal — and genetically expected — for a Selkirk litter to contain both straight-haired and curly-haired kittens. The straight-coated ones inherit the same gentle temperament and robust physical structure of the breed, though they are not accepted in pedigree competitions.

There is also an important distinction within the breed itself: heterozygous cats (one copy of the mutant gene) tend to have more uniform and structured curls, smaller ears, and a rounder head — the preferred standard for shows. Homozygous cats (two copies) may be born nearly hairless and develop their coat later; their adult coat, when present, is extraordinarily long with extreme curls, making them valuable for breeding programs as they guarantee 100% curly offspring.

Architecture of a Teddy Bear

Looking at a Selkirk Rex for the first time is a disconcerting experience. The animal looks like it came out of a toy factory. The head is round, with full cheeks and a muzzle with a distinct stop — that abrupt transition between forehead and nose, a trait inherited from the Persian. The whisker pads are well-filled, creating an illusion of squareness in the face. The eyes are large, round, and well-separated, giving a perpetually sweet and mildly surprised expression.

The body is anything but delicate: rectangular torso, heavy and muscular, with wide bone structure. Males can weigh between 5 and 7 kg; females are slightly smaller but equally solid. This is a cat that, when you pick it up, genuinely has heft.

And then there is the coat. The whiskers are naturally curly and fragile — prone to breaking, which is considered normal and does not indicate a health problem. The coat follows the same principle of organized disorder: random, unstructured groupings with no uniform wave pattern. There are no regular waves as in the Cornish Rex — there are curls that follow their own logic, as if each strand made an independent decision.

Longhair vs. Shorthair: Two Worlds of Curls

The Selkirk Rex exists in two coat-length variations, recognized separately by most feline associations:

The Shorthair features short, dense, plush curls with the appearance of luxurious velvet. The body coat measures between 2 and 5 cm, and the curls lie compactly around the tail without projecting outward. The ruff matches the length of the rest of the body, without drama.

The Longhair — the focus of this article — is an entirely different aesthetic experience. The loose curls form what resembles dreadlocks or cascading rings. The tail has a plumy effect, with curls projecting outward from the central structure. The ruff frames the face dramatically, transforming the animal into a sort of miniature lion with a permanent wave. It is, objectively, one of the most spectacular coats in the feline world.

A Color Palette Without Restrictions

Unlike breeds such as the Chartreux — restricted to blue — or the Korat — strictly blue-silver —, the Selkirk Rex enjoys a rare chromatic freedom. The Cat Fanciers' Association (CFA) recognizes and accepts all genetically possible colors and patterns for the breed: solids (white, black, blue, red), classic, mackerel, and spotted tabbies, shaded and smoke, bicolors, calico, and colorpoints in mink, sepia, and lynx variations. Any eye color is equally permitted and celebrated.

This chromatic diversity is a direct consequence of the outcrossing policy adopted in the breed's early decades, when Persian, British Shorthair, and Exotic Shorthair were used to expand the genetic base. It is worth noting that, as of January 1, 2025, the CFA ended permission for these external crossings: all registered kittens must now descend from two Selkirk Rex parents.

Personality: The Easygoing Companion

If the Selkirk Rex's appearance is already captivating, its temperament does not disappoint. The breed is known for its teddy bear patience — a phrase breeders use with genuine precision. They are extremely affectionate cats, with very high tolerance for children and other animals. They are neither hyperactive nor particularly demanding, but they are far from indifferent: they prefer the comfort of a human lap over acrobatics and intense hunting games.

Vocalization is low — these are cats that "talk" little, communicating more through physical presence and facial expressions. Intelligence and curiosity are high, meaning they appreciate puzzle toys and scratching posts, but do not require constant stimulation to maintain emotional balance.

A notable characteristic is their sociability with change: Selkirks handle travel and routine changes well, as long as they are accompanied by their family. They are not cats that suffer from new people or environments — as long as their reference humans are nearby.

Curl Maintenance: What to Do (and What to Avoid)

Maintaining a Selkirk Rex coat requires counterintuitive knowledge. The natural temptation is to brush — after all, it is a long, dense coat. But brushing destroys the curl structure and creates excessive frizz, ruining exactly what makes the breed special. The same applies to hot-air dryers, which temporarily straighten the coat and eliminate the characteristic texture.

The correct protocol is minimalist and gentle:

  • Comb only with a wide-toothed comb, 1 to 2 times per month.
  • Detangle with fingers using gentle movements to fluff the curls daily.
  • Bathe with lightweight shampoos that do not weigh down the coat.
  • Dry gently with a towel and allow to air dry.

An oily or shapeless coat is a warning sign: it may indicate lack of self-grooming (common in sick or painful cats) or underlying health problems that warrant veterinary attention.

Health: Robust, but Mindful of the Genetic Legacy

The Selkirk Rex is generally robust, with a life expectancy of 10 to 15 years. However, its crossbred lineage — especially with the Persian and British Shorthair — has introduced predispositions that responsible breeders must actively monitor:

  • PKD (Polycystic Kidney Disease): inherited from the Persian, causes the formation of cysts in the kidneys that can progress to renal failure. DNA testing of both parents is essential.
  • HCM (Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy): inherited from the British Shorthair, involves thickening of the heart muscle. Regular echocardiograms in breeding cats are the minimum required standard.
  • Hip Dysplasia: a severe joint condition, also frequent in Persian lines, which compromises mobility in serious cases.

There is also the blood type risk, little discussed outside breeding circles: the Selkirk Rex has two documented blood types, Type A (dominant) and Type B (recessive). Type B cats produce highly aggressive antibodies against Type A blood. If a Type B female is crossed with a Type A male, the Type A kittens face a lethal risk when ingesting maternal colostrum — a phenomenon called Neonatal Isoerythrolysis, which can result in Fading Kitten Syndrome. The solution is straightforward: mandatory blood typing of both parents before any breeding pairing or medical transfusion.

Responsible breeding eliminates virtually all these risks through systematic genetic and cardiological testing. When acquiring a Selkirk Rex, always request documentation of these exams.

The Perfect Storm: What Makes the Selkirk Unique

The Selkirk Rex is, in essence, a feline synthesis: the affectionate temperament and luxurious density of the long coat from the Persian; the easygoing personality, resilience, and robust physical structure of the British Shorthair; the rounded face and muscular solidity of the Exotic Shorthair — all fused by Miss DePesto's dominant mutation, which turned every strand into a magnificent, unstructured curl.

It is a recent breed — just over three decades of recognized existence — but already firmly established as one of the most appreciated worldwide among those seeking a cat that is simultaneously spectacular in appearance and balanced in temperament. The cat in lamb's clothing, born by pure genetic chance in a Montana shelter, turned out to be anything but ordinary.
