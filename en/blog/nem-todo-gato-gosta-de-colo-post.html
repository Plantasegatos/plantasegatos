Not Every Cat Likes Being Held: The Science Behind Feline Resistance

The scene is familiar: you reach out, lift the cat with genuine affection, and suddenly you are holding a claw-equipped torpedo. It bites, scratches, twists free and sprints away the moment it hits the floor. You wonder what you did wrong. The honest answer? Probably nothing — except assuming it wanted that in the first place.

Not every cat likes being held. And that is not stubbornness, unresolved trauma, or a lack of affection. It is biology.

A predator in the air: the loss of spatial reference

Cats are ambush predators that also appear on the menu of larger animals. Their entire sensory architecture — vestibular balance, proprioception, peripheral vision, escape capability — was built to function with all four paws on the ground or on surfaces they control.

When a cat is lifted, it instantly loses the references its nervous system uses to calculate position, distance, and escape routes. The inner ear signals imbalance. The proprioceptive muscles in the paws find no resistance. Neurobiologically, the animal perceives total vulnerability — and the limbic system responds accordingly.

This is not metaphor. It is activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis: cortisol rises, heart rate increases, musculature tenses. The cat is not being dramatic. It is frightened.

Scruffing and what science actually says

Holding cats by the scruff of the neck is often justified by the argument that mother cats carry their kittens that way. That is true, but the analogy breaks down at critical points.

Kittens up to approximately three weeks of age have an immobilization reflex called CLIP (Clipnosis or Pinch-Induced Behavioral Inhibition), which makes them go limp and passive when held by the scruff. This reflex disappears as the nervous system matures. In adults, scruffing does not trigger comfortable stillness — it triggers stress.

Studies published in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery found that adult cats subjected to scruffing show measurable stress markers, including mydriasis (pupil dilation), tachycardia, and escape attempts. The American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) explicitly recommends avoiding scruffing in clinical and routine handling contexts.

The ones who tolerate it: conditioning, not innate preference

If some cats clearly enjoy being held — purring, settling in, staying put — what explains the difference? The answer lies largely in the socialization window.

Between two and seven weeks of age, the feline brain is in a period of intense neural plasticity. Cats regularly exposed to human contact during this window — including being picked up, held, and handled carefully — develop positive associations with that type of interaction. They do not learn to biologically enjoy being lifted; they learn that this specific context is safe, and the stress response is suppressed by conditioning.

Cats that did not go through this socialization window, or had negative experiences during it, tend to retain the stress response intact. There is nothing to "fix": the system is working exactly as designed.

The primate bias

This is where one of the most common mistakes lives. We are primates. Primates use physical contact — hugging, carrying, bodily closeness — as the primary language of affection and security. Human infants need to be carried. Chimpanzees embrace during conflict. Touch is, for us, fundamentally reassuring.

Cats do not share this system. Feline affection is expressed through bunting, slow blinking, chosen proximity, and scent exchange. Intense physical contact — especially contact that removes control over one's own body — is not part of their comfort repertoire. When we pick a cat up because we need affection, we are projecting our own emotional grammar onto a species with an entirely different one.

The cat is not ungrateful. It simply does not speak the same body language.

How to interact respectfully

The good news is that many cats tolerate or even enjoy forms of physical contact that do not involve being lifted. Petting around the head and neck — where sebaceous scent glands are concentrated — is often welcome. The key is always letting the cat initiate or, at minimum, show clear signs of acceptance, and stopping the moment those signals change.

If you need to pick up the cat out of necessity — veterinary visit, transport, emergency — technique matters: firm support under the hindquarters, body held close to your torso, no squeezing. Minimize the time and end with something positive, such as a treat or petting in a preferred spot. With consistent repetition and no forcing, many cats develop increasing tolerance. But tolerance is not the same as enjoyment, and recognizing that difference is what respect looks like.
