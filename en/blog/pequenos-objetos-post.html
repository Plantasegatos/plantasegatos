Small Objects, Big Risks: Household Hazards for Cats

A hair tie forgotten on the floor. A bobby pin that fell behind the furniture. A piece of string, a cotton swab, a tiny orthodontic elastic or the twist tie used to close a bread bag.

To us, these are ordinary objects. To a cat, however, many of them roll, bounce, swing or move in ways that may resemble small prey.

Problems begin when play no longer involves only the paws and the object reaches the mouth. Depending on what was swallowed, its shape and where it becomes lodged, an item only a few centimeters long may cause gastrointestinal obstruction, internal injury, perforation or require veterinary intervention.

Why are cats so interested in small objects?

Feline play behavior is closely connected with the species' predatory repertoire. Chasing, grabbing, pawing, biting and manipulating objects are all components of this behavioral sequence.

The AAFP and ISFM environmental guidelines emphasize the importance of allowing cats to express hunting and play-related behaviors in the home. This helps explain why a moving string, a sliding hair tie or a bouncing elastic can be particularly attractive.

Curiosity, however, does not mean that a cat can recognize danger. An object may be entertaining while still being inappropriate to leave available without supervision.

Strings and threads: the special danger of linear foreign bodies

String, thread, ribbon and similar materials deserve particular attention because they can behave as linear foreign bodies.

Part of the material may become anchored at one point, including underneath the tongue or within the gastrointestinal tract, while the remaining length continues to move through the intestine.

The bowel may then bunch or plicate around the material. Tension produced by the string can damage the intestinal wall and, in severe cases, contribute to perforation and contamination of the abdominal cavity.

Surgical studies in cats confirm that linear foreign bodies are an important cause of gastrointestinal procedures and can require more complex surgical management than some discrete foreign objects.

Hair ties, elastic bands and small accessories can also obstruct

An object does not need to be sharp or rigid to cause a problem.

Hair ties, rubber bands, small rubber objects and similar accessories can be swallowed whole and behave as discrete foreign bodies.

If they cannot pass normally through the gastrointestinal tract, they may remain in the stomach or cause intestinal obstruction.

Not every ingested foreign object requires surgery. Treatment depends on the material, location, time since ingestion and the cat's clinical condition. In some cases, however, endoscopic or surgical removal may be necessary.

Bobby pins, clips and twist ties: small but potentially traumatic

Bobby pins, metal clips and packaging twist ties introduce another important factor: shape.

In addition to causing obstruction, rigid or pointed objects may injure the mouth, esophagus, stomach or intestines.

Those small twist ties that are sometimes kept after opening a package because they “might be useful someday” are a good example. Their size and ability to move across the floor may make them more interesting to a cat than we expect.

Needle and thread: a particularly dangerous combination

Sewing needles attached to thread are a well-recognized veterinary hazard.

A retrospective study of 65 dogs and cats with sewing needle foreign bodies included 38 cats. In cats, the esophagus and stomach were among the most common locations, and gastrointestinal perforation was documented in some animals.

The danger is increased because a cat may initially interact only with the thread and subsequently swallow the attached needle.

Our own self-care routines can create hazards

Many potentially dangerous objects come directly from our daily hygiene and grooming routines.

Cotton swabs, dental floss, bobby pins, hair clips, hair ties and tiny orthodontic elastics are often left on bathroom counters, bedside tables or sinks, or simply dropped onto the floor unnoticed.

This does not mean that every one of these objects will cause obstruction whenever a cat plays with it. The concern increases when they are chewed, fragmented or swallowed.

Tablets and capsules present a different kind of risk

Medications belong to a separate category because their danger is not only physical but also chemical.

Human tablets and capsules may fall onto the floor or remain on countertops or bedside tables. A curious cat may paw at, chew or swallow them.

Some medications commonly used by people can be extremely dangerous to cats. Acetaminophen, for example, can cause severe toxicosis because cats have limited ability to metabolize some of the compounds involved in its elimination.

Human anti-inflammatory drugs and several other medications can also cause serious poisoning. Medications should therefore never be given to a cat without veterinary guidance and should always be stored where animals cannot reach them.

What signs may occur after ingestion?

Clinical signs vary according to the object, its location and the time elapsed since ingestion.

Possible warning signs include:

  • vomiting or repeated attempts to vomit;
  • excessive salivation;
  • reduced or absent appetite;
  • lethargy;
  • abdominal pain or discomfort;
  • changes in defecation;
  • unusual behavior or hiding.

Some cats may show only subtle signs initially. For this reason, when ingestion is known or strongly suspected, waiting until severe symptoms develop is not a good strategy.

Can you see string coming from the mouth or anus? Do not pull it

This is one of the most important safety rules.

If string, thread or similar material is visible emerging from the mouth or anus, do not pull it.

The other end may still be anchored inside the gastrointestinal tract. Pulling may increase tension on the intestine and worsen internal injury.

Prevent the cat from continuing to manipulate the material and seek veterinary evaluation.

Not every ingestion should be managed in the same way

Do not attempt to induce vomiting at home unless specifically instructed by a veterinarian.

Management depends on what was swallowed. A needle, linear foreign body, medication, hair tie or sharp object presents very different clinical considerations.

Veterinary assessment may include physical examination and diagnostic imaging such as radiography or ultrasonography. Depending on the circumstances, the object may be monitored, removed endoscopically or require surgery.

Prevention is usually simple

The goal is not to create a sterile home or prevent cats from playing.

Cats need appropriate opportunities to stalk, chase, catch and manipulate safe toys. The key is to provide this enrichment while removing objects that were never designed to function as toys.

Simple precautions include:

  • store string, thread, ribbon and sewing materials after use;
  • pick up hair ties, elastic bands and clips that fall onto the floor;
  • discard packaging twist ties safely;
  • keep cotton swabs and dental floss inside closed trash containers;
  • store medications in cabinets or containers inaccessible to cats;
  • never leave needles attached to thread on tables, beds or sofas;
  • supervise play involving toys with strings or detachable parts.

They are small precautions against risks that, under the wrong circumstances, can become very large.

To us, a hair tie may be nothing more than a hair tie. To a curious cat, it may look like the best prey in the house.

And that is why the best emergency remains the one we manage to prevent.
