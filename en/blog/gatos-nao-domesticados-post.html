The Great Illusion: Cats Are Not Truly Domesticated

There is a widespread belief that cats are domesticated animals just like dogs, cows, or horses. The reality, however, is far more nuanced and fascinating. Scientifically and behaviorally, domestic cats (Felis catus) represent a unique case in the animal kingdom: they chose to live alongside humans, but they never allowed themselves to be truly domesticated.

Genuine domestication involves a long process of artificial selection in which humans deliberately breed individuals with desirable traits over many generations. This results in deep genetic, morphological, and behavioral transformations. Dogs, for example, were domesticated around 15,000–40,000 years ago and underwent dramatic changes: they developed the ability to read human facial expressions, adapted their social structure to coexist with human groups, and show classic domestication traits such as floppy ears, spotted coats, and hormonal changes.

Cats, on the other hand, began living near humans around 10,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent, when we first started storing grain. Rodents were drawn to the abundant food supply, and wildcats saw an opportunity. This process is known as self-domestication or commensalism: cats approached human settlements of their own free will, attracted by easy prey — not because humans tried to capture or breed them intentionally.

Genetic studies by Driscoll and colleagues (2007) revealed that the genome of the domestic cat is astonishingly similar to that of its wild ancestors. While dogs differ from wolves in thousands of genetic markers, cats have preserved roughly 95% of their original genetic makeup. Genetically, your house cat is nearly identical to the African wildcat (Felis lybica).

Behaviorally, the evidence is even more compelling. Unlike truly domesticated animals, cats retain full independence. They hunt even when well-fed — an instinct that thousands of years of coexistence with humans have not erased. Research by Bradshaw (2013) demonstrates that cats do not view humans as hierarchical authority figures, but as resource providers and, at best, as tolerated social companions. They do not show the submissive behaviors typical of fully domesticated species.

Feline territoriality also remains intact. Cats do not form cooperative packs like dogs; their social structure remains largely solitary, similar to wild felids. When they do interact socially, it is by choice and mutual tolerance — not out of reliance or group structure. A domestic cat can survive perfectly well without humans, as feral populations around the world clearly demonstrate.

Neuroscientist John Bradshaw, in his book Cat Sense, argues that cats essentially treat us as oversized, clumsy cats. They have not significantly adapted their communication to interact with humans. Adult meowing — often cited as a communication strategy toward humans — is actually a retained juvenile behavior that some individuals learn is effective, not an evolutionary adaptation.

Neurologically, cats differ as well. Brain-imaging studies show that dogs developed expanded brain regions specialized for processing human faces and emotions. Cats do not display these neurological adaptations. Their brains remain optimized for solitary hunting, complex territorial navigation, and highly tuned sensory processing for detecting prey.

Archaeozoology supports this perspective. Burials of dogs alongside humans date back thousands of years, demonstrating deep emotional bonds and social integration. Cat burials, however, are rare and appear mainly in specific cultural contexts such as ancient Egypt — where their presence was symbolic or religious rather than evidence of emotional attachment.

So what are cats? They are semi-domesticated commensals that developed a tolerance for human presence while retaining their core identity as solitary, independent predators. They accepted a mutually beneficial arrangement: humans provide shelter, food, and protection from larger predators; cats provide pest control and, occasionally, companionship. But this is a partnership — not true domestication.

This understanding doesn’t diminish the human-cat relationship; it makes it even more fascinating. Unlike animals we shaped to fit our needs, cats negotiated the terms of coexistence. They allow us to believe we domesticated them, when in reality, they simply decided that living with us had enough advantages to be worthwhile.

The illusion of domestication persists because some cats form genuine emotional bonds with humans and display behaviors we interpret as dependency or submission. But make no mistake: the cat purring in your lap chose to be there. Tomorrow, they may choose differently. And that is precisely the beauty of the relationship: it is not imposed by millennia of artificial selection, but built daily through mutual choice and respect for the wild essence still beating beneath that soft fur.
