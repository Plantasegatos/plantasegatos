The Link: What Science Reveals About Animal Cruelty and Human Violence

Can someone who deliberately harms an animal also pose a risk to other people? This question has been investigated for decades by researchers in criminology, forensic psychology, veterinary medicine, and behavioral science.

Scientific research has identified associations between animal cruelty and several forms of interpersonal violence. This relationship is commonly known as The Link.

The concept does not suggest that every person who harms an animal will eventually become violent toward humans. Instead, it recognizes that certain patterns of animal abuse may serve as warning signs requiring attention, investigation, and preventive intervention.

What Is The Link?

The Link describes the observed association between animal cruelty and other forms of violence, including child abuse, domestic violence, elder abuse, and antisocial behavior.

These behaviors may share underlying factors such as emotional regulation difficulties, coercive behavior, previous exposure to violence, and disregard for the suffering of others.

A scientific review published in 2017 examined 96 articles and found some association between animal abuse and violence toward humans in 94 of them.

However, the authors also identified methodological limitations and substantial geographical concentration, with most research originating in North America.

These findings support the importance of recognizing potential risks without treating statistical associations as reliable predictions of individual behavior.

What Has the FBI Found?

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) recognizes animal cruelty as a possible indicator of other forms of violence.

In January 2016, the FBI began collecting animal cruelty data as a distinct offense category through the National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS).

The system records incidents involving severe neglect, intentional abuse, torture, organized animal abuse, and other forms of cruelty.

Classifying animal cruelty separately allows investigators and researchers to identify crime patterns and explore potential connections with other offenses.

In a 2021 publication, the FBI highlighted links between animal cruelty and violence against intimate partners, children, and older adults.

The agency also emphasized an essential distinction: animal abuse does not always precede violence against humans.

Different forms of violence may occur simultaneously, and some offenders harm people before they harm animals.

What Do the Statistics Reveal?

A systematic review published by Longobardi and Badenes-Ribera in 2019 examined 32 studies involving animal cruelty during childhood and adolescence.

The findings associated animal cruelty with bullying, behavioral difficulties, experiences of abuse, juvenile delinquency, and other forms of antisocial behavior.

Recurrent animal cruelty was also associated with an increased risk of interpersonal violence in adulthood.

A study discussed in the FBI's 2021 publication examined 150 adult men arrested for animal cruelty. Among these individuals, 41% had previously been arrested at least once for interpersonal violence.

This finding must be interpreted carefully. The sample consisted of individuals already involved with the criminal justice system and does not represent the general population.

Widely circulated statistics, including claims that 88% of serial killers abused animals during childhood, are often repeated without sufficient information about their original sources, samples, or methodologies.

Such figures should not be presented as established scientific consensus without proper verification.

Animal Cruelty and Psychopathic Traits

One of the most sensitive questions concerns the relationship between animal cruelty and psychological characteristics associated with psychopathy.

In scientific literature, psychopathic traits may include emotional callousness, reduced affective empathy, manipulation, lack of remorse, and certain patterns of antisocial behavior.

Repeated and deliberate cruelty toward animals may occur in individuals who display some of these characteristics.

However, animal cruelty is not exclusive to psychopathy and may be associated with other psychological, social, and environmental factors.

Harming an animal is not, by itself, sufficient to diagnose psychopathy.

A clinical assessment requires validated instruments, detailed behavioral history, and consideration of multiple psychological dimensions.

Likewise, it would be scientifically inaccurate to assume that everyone displaying psychopathic traits engages in animal cruelty.

Domestic Violence and Companion Animals

One of the most important practical applications of The Link concerns domestic violence.

In abusive relationships, dogs, cats, and other companion animals may be threatened, injured, or killed as a means of intimidating partners, punishing family members, or preventing victims from leaving.

The emotional bond between a person and their animal can become a tool of coercive control.

A systematic review published by Cleary and colleagues in 2021 identified an important relationship between animal abuse and intimate partner violence.

These findings reinforce the need to consider companion animals in domestic violence prevention programs, emergency shelters, and victim protection strategies.

Why Does Animal Cruelty in Childhood Matter?

When a child repeatedly and intentionally harms animals, it is important to examine the broader circumstances surrounding the behavior.

Such actions may be associated with emotional difficulties, exposure to violence, traumatic experiences, or behavioral disorders.

However, isolated incidents cannot establish a psychiatric diagnosis or predict future criminal behavior.

Appropriate responses include protecting the animals, supporting the family, and seeking professional psychological assessment when necessary.

Early intervention can help address harmful behavior patterns and promote healthier relationships with both animals and people.

What Does The Link Have to Do with Electoral Responsibility?

The relationship between animal cruelty and interpersonal violence also raises broader questions about ethics, public responsibility, and the exercise of political power.

In a democratic society, evaluating candidates and elected officials may involve examining their documented conduct, public policy proposals, legislative records, and measurable results.

Animal welfare is a legitimate public policy issue involving law enforcement, education, public health, and violence prevention.

Nevertheless, scientific evidence must be distinguished from political judgment. The Link does not establish that a particular candidate will become violent toward people because of a political position or an isolated incident.

Responsible civic evaluation requires verified facts, context, due process, and consideration of public policies as a whole.

Understanding animal protection legislation and examining representatives' documented actions can contribute to informed civic participation.

Protecting Animals Also Means Recognizing Violence

The Link has helped researchers and institutions understand animal cruelty as a form of violence that may coexist with other abusive behaviors.

Its primary value lies not in labeling individuals or making absolute predictions, but in recognizing warning signs and encouraging appropriate preventive action.

Veterinarians, educators, social workers, psychologists, law enforcement officers, and animal protection organizations can all contribute to this process.

Above all, animal cruelty is a serious concern in its own right, regardless of whether it is associated with violence against humans.

An animal's suffering does not need to predict another tragedy to deserve attention, protection, and justice.
