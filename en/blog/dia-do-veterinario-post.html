Veterinarian's Day: Those Who Care Need Care Too

There are professions we choose. And there are professions that, somehow, seem to choose us as well.

Veterinary Medicine may be one of them.

Behind every consultation, every diagnosis, every overnight shift and every animal that returns home is someone who spent years studying and continues learning every day. Someone who must bring together science, technical expertise, responsibility and an extraordinary ability to care.

On September 9, we celebrated Veterinarian's Day. But our tribute could never end with a simple “congratulations.”

Because there is something we need to say: those who care need care too.

A profession made of extraordinary encounters

Some moments can explain why a person chose this profession.

A newborn taking its first breath. An animal finally eating again. A cat recognizing their human after days in hospital. A diagnosis made in time. A successful surgery. A patient being discharged and going home.

To someone watching from the outside, it may look like just another case. To the person who was there, it may become a small victory remembered for years.

Veterinary Medicine is built from these encounters between knowledge and life. Perhaps that is precisely what makes it so beautiful.

But not every day ends that way

The same person who witnesses recovery must also communicate difficult diagnoses. They deal with emergencies, severe disease, therapeutic limitations and, inevitably, death.

There are animals medicine cannot save.

There are decisions that must be made even when nobody wants to make them.

And there are stories that do not end when the clinic door closes. Some follow the professional home.

Caring can be exhausting too

Long shifts, emergencies, constant responsibility, repeated exposure to suffering and intense emotional demands are part of the reality faced by many veterinary professionals.

Scientific literature has drawn attention to mental health in Veterinary Medicine for years. Research conducted in different countries has identified concerning levels of psychological distress, burnout and suicidal ideation among portions of the veterinary workforce.

The causes are complex and cannot be reduced to a single factor. Workload, financial pressures, conflict with clients, repeated exposure to death and euthanasia, clinical responsibility and access to certain medications are among the factors discussed in the literature.

This does not mean suffering is an inevitable part of the profession. It means precisely the opposite: we need conditions that allow people to care without destroying those who provide that care.

And there is still misunderstanding

Few things seem as unfair as turning the person trying to help into the target of frustration.

Medicine has costs. Science has limits. Treatments can fail. Prognoses can change. And sometimes, even when everything is done correctly, the outcome everyone hoped for simply does not happen.

None of this justifies humiliation, threats or aggression toward veterinary professionals.

A veterinarian does not stop being human when they put on their scrubs.

They feel the pressure. They feel loss. They experience the fear of making a mistake. They can become frustrated. They can become exhausted. They may need to stop. And they may need to ask for help.

Recognizing this does not diminish their competence. It recognizes their humanity.

Respect is part of care

When we take an animal to a veterinarian, we are often placing someone we deeply love in their hands.

Fear is understandable. Questions are understandable. Wanting answers is understandable.

But there is an enormous difference between asking questions and attacking someone, between seeking clarification and showing disrespect.

Better Veterinary Medicine also depends on more humane relationships: valued professionals, dignified working conditions, safe workplaces, respect from pet guardians and an environment where mental health is treated as health.

Our small tribute is also an act of care

This is for those working today.

For those who finished an exhausting shift and still came back the next day.

For those who quietly celebrated when a patient finally began to recover.

For those who cried after a loss.

For those who needed to stop. For those who found another path.

And also in memory of professionals who left us too soon, amid suffering that too often remained unseen.

Perhaps the best tribute we can offer is not simply thanking veterinarians for caring for our animals.

Perhaps it is learning to take better care of those who care for them.

To veterinarians everywhere: our affection, our gratitude and, above all, our respect.

Caring for a life carries enormous responsibility. No one should have to become ill before their limits are recognized.

Those who care need care too.

If you are experiencing severe emotional distress, seek professional support and reach out to people you trust. In Brazil, the Centro de Valorização da Vida (CVV) provides free emotional support by calling 188.
