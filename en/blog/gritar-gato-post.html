🐾 Why Yelling at Your Cat Doesn’t Work

By Plantas & Gatos — understanding feline behavior with empathy and care

You know when your cat knocks over that favorite vase, scratches the brand-new couch, or jumps on the counter for the hundredth time — and you find yourself yelling or putting on that angry face? Well… your cat doesn’t understand any of that the way you think.

Cats simply don’t process “scolding” like humans do. When you raise your voice, point a finger, or startle them, they don’t connect your reaction to what they did. What goes through their little head is more like: “my human is acting weird… should I be afraid?”

A cat’s brain doesn’t work like ours. They don’t think, “oh, I did that and now my owner is mad.” What gets recorded instead is: “the person I trust sometimes becomes scary for no reason.” And that’s when trust starts to crack. Things can actually get worse, leading to behaviors like hiding all the time, peeing outside the litter box, or even becoming aggressive.

💔 What Happens Inside When You Yell

Imagine living somewhere where, out of the blue, someone yells at you and you have no idea why. That’s pretty much how your cat feels. Their body releases stress hormones — mainly cortisol — and suddenly the home no longer feels safe or peaceful.

Over time, this snowballs into visible signs of anxiety. You might notice things like:

  • Excessive grooming to the point of bald spots or wounds;
  • Loss of appetite;
  • Hiding under the bed or avoiding contact;
  • Urinating or defecating outside the litter box;
  • Restlessness, poor sleep, or running away when approached.

It’s not misbehavior — it’s distress.

🌱 What Actually Works (and It’s Simpler Than You Think)

You can absolutely teach a cat — but on their terms, not ours. Instead of punishing, try to understand the reason behind the behavior:

  • Scratching the couch? Place a scratching post right there — one they’ll love (vertical, horizontal, or with the right texture).
  • Jumping on counters? They crave height. Offer shelves, cat trees, or vertical spaces where they can climb safely.
  • Messing with plants? Provide safe alternatives, like cat grass or toys that satisfy curiosity and play.

And whenever they do something right — even something small — reward it. A pet, a cheerful “good kitty,” or a little treat goes a long way. Positive reinforcement builds trust and naturally encourages good behavior.

💬 In Short — From the Heart

Yelling might stop your cat in the moment, but only out of fear. And fear doesn’t teach — it damages. What truly transforms a “difficult” cat into an amazing companion is trust, calm routine, and an environment that makes sense to them.

In the end, love and patience always win. Yelling never does.
