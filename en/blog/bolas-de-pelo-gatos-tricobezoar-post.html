Hairballs in Cats: The Science Behind the Trichobezoar and How to Prevent Them

The technical name for that sound at 3 AM

Every cat owner knows the sound. A rhythm of convulsive retching that wakes you from deep sleep, followed by the ominous silence that precedes the discovery of a cylindrical, damp mass in the middle of the carpet. What your cat has just expelled has a name: a trichobezoar — from the Greek tricho (hair) + bezoar (any foreign material obstructing the gastrointestinal tract).

Despite the popular name, the "ball" of hair is rarely round. It is almost always cylindrical, cigar or sausage-shaped — molded by the narrow esophagus through which it passes during expulsion. The color varies between brown and dark, stained by bile and digestive juices, and the odor is mildly fetid but tolerable. That is, when things go well. When they don't, the trichobezoar can become a surgical emergency.

How the trichobezoar forms: the tongue that works against the cat

The origin of the problem lies in the most sophisticated tool in the cat's repertoire: the tongue. The filiform papillae — small conical projections facing backward that cover the dorsal surface of the tongue — are the same structures that allow the cat to untangle knots in fur, distribute saliva efficiently, and strip meat from bones. But these same papillae, by their orientation, function as a one-way street: any loose hair that touches the tongue automatically moves backward, toward the throat, with no possibility of return.

Once swallowed, the hair reaches the stomach. The main structural component of hair — keratin, a fibrous, insoluble protein — is completely indigestible. Most of the ingested hair passes through the gastrointestinal tract normally and is eliminated in the feces. But when ingestion exceeds intestinal transit capacity — through excessive volume, reduced motility, or gradual accumulation — the strands begin to tangle with one another. Over time, the mass grows until it reaches a size that can no longer pass through the sphincters of the digestive tract. The trichobezoar has formed.

From there, two outcomes: the cat regurgitates the mass through the esophagus — what most people call "vomiting a hairball," though technically it is regurgitation, not vomiting — or the trichobezoar migrates into the intestine and creates an obstruction. The first outcome is uncomfortable. The second can be fatal.

When it is normal — and when it is not

A frequency of one hair regurgitation every one to two weeks is considered within normal range for a healthy adult cat, according to the Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine. Kittens and young cats are less susceptible — they have not yet developed the meticulous grooming habits of adults. Senior cats, on the other hand, tend to regurgitate more frequently due to intensified grooming with age.

Long-haired breeds — Persian, Maine Coon, Norwegian Forest Cat, Siberian — present significantly higher risk than short-haired breeds, simply due to the volume of hair ingested per grooming session. Periods of intense shedding (spring and fall) also increase frequency.

The signs that require prompt veterinary consultation are:

  • Repeated unproductive retching — the cat attempts to expel something but cannot. May indicate obstruction.
  • Frequency greater than once per week — may signal overgrooming from anxiety, skin disease, parasites, or an underlying gastrointestinal problem.
  • Loss of appetite for more than 24 hours associated with retching.
  • Intense lethargy and arched posture suggesting abdominal pain.
  • Vomiting blood or very dark material along with fur.
  • Visibly distended abdomen.

Confusion between hairball retching and feline asthma symptoms is common and potentially dangerous — both produce a similar sound of respiratory effort. If no material is expelled after the episode, or if episodes recur without visible result, evaluating for asthma is essential.

Factors that increase risk

Not every cat forms trichobezoars with the same frequency. Beyond coat length and breed, the following factors increase risk:

  • Overgrooming due to stress or anxiety: cats that groom compulsively ingest far larger volumes of hair than normal. Skin disease, allergy, flea infestation, and feline idiopathic cystitis (frequently linked to stress) can trigger overgrooming.
  • Reduced gastrointestinal motility: any condition that slows intestinal transit — inflammatory bowel disease, hypothyroidism, autonomic neuropathy — increases the time hair remains in the digestive tract, favoring mass formation.
  • Dehydration: low water intake thickens intestinal secretions and reduces the ability to move the fecal bolus, including the hair within it.
  • Low-fiber diet: insoluble fibers function as an intestinal "broom," sweeping hair through the tract and preventing accumulation.
  • Intense artificial lighting: indoor cats exposed to prolonged artificial light cycles may have altered shedding patterns — with hair loss more distributed throughout the year — with a cumulative effect on ingestion.

Evidence-based prevention strategies

Regular brushing is the most effective intervention with the best available evidence of cost-benefit. By mechanically removing dead and loose hair before the cat ingests it during grooming, brushing directly reduces the volume of keratin reaching the stomach. For long-haired cats, daily brushing of 5 to 10 minutes is the recommended standard. For short coats, two to three times per week already produces significant effect. The tool should be appropriate to the coat type: wide-toothed comb for long coats, rubber grooming glove or fine-bristled brush for short coats.

Hydration is the second pillar. Cats are evolutionarily adapted to obtain water through food (prey animals are 70–75% moisture), not through drinking. Partial or total transition to wet food (pouches or cans) significantly increases water intake and improves intestinal transit. Water fountains also stimulate consumption — many cats prefer moving water to still water.

Dietary fiber is the third element. Insoluble fibers such as powdered cellulose, beet pulp, and psyllium (available in hairball-control foods or as supplements) increase fecal volume and intestinal transit speed, carrying hair through the tract before it accumulates. Transition to a higher-fiber food should be gradual — 7 to 10 days — to avoid digestive disturbances.

Petrolatum-based lubricating gels and pastes are another available resource: they act as mild laxatives, lubricating accumulated hair and facilitating its passage. They should be used exclusively under veterinary guidance — petrolatum interferes with the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) when used excessively or inappropriately.

When the trichobezoar won't come out: gastrointestinal obstruction

In a minority of cases, the trichobezoar migrates from the stomach into the small intestine and creates a partial or complete obstruction. This scenario is uncommon but potentially fatal if not treated promptly. Clinical signs include persistent vomiting (not just retching), progressive anorexia, increasing lethargy, abdominal pain on palpation, and abdominal distension.

Diagnosis is confirmed by abdominal radiography or ultrasound. Treatment of an established obstruction rarely responds to medical management — in most cases, surgical intervention is required to remove the trichobezoar. Surgery, when performed before significant intestinal damage occurs, carries a favorable prognosis, with most cats recovering well after hospitalization of one to three days.
