Ear Mites in Cats: A Tiny Parasite, a Major Discomfort

Anyone who lives with cats has probably seen it: a hind paw rapidly scratching an ear, followed by a vigorous shake of the head. At first, it may seem like ordinary feline behavior. But when scratching becomes persistent, something much smaller than we imagine may be responsible.

Meet Otodectes cynotis, a microscopic mite responsible for otodectic mange, commonly known as ear mite infestation.

Despite its tiny size, this parasite can trigger significant inflammation, compromise a cat's well-being and lead to complications if not properly diagnosed and treated.

What is otodectic mange?

Otodectic mange is a parasitic infestation primarily affecting the external ear canal of cats, dogs and other domestic carnivores.

Its causative agent, Otodectes cynotis, belongs to the family Psoroptidae and is classified as a non-burrowing mite.

Unlike certain other mange mites, it remains on the skin surface rather than tunneling into it.

The parasite feeds on epithelial debris, secretions and superficial tissue fluids. Its activity and associated substances stimulate local inflammation, frequently accompanied by pruritus, or itching.

Ear mites are among the most important parasitic causes of feline otitis externa. However, reported prevalence varies considerably according to the population studied, age, housing conditions and exposure to other cats.

The life cycle of Otodectes cynotis

The mite completes most of its biological cycle on the host. Development generally takes approximately three weeks, although the duration may vary.

The life cycle includes five stages:

  1. Egg: deposited by the female within the external ear canal.
  2. Larva: the first mobile stage, with three pairs of legs.
  3. Protonymph: an intermediate developmental stage.
  4. Deutonymph: the stage preceding adulthood.
  5. Adult: the reproductive stage that maintains the infestation.

Understanding this cycle helps explain why treatment protocols must consider the parasite's development and the properties of the selected medication.

How are ear mites transmitted?

The primary route of transmission is direct, close contact between animals.

Cats that sleep together, groom each other or frequently share close physical contact have increased opportunities for transmission.

Kittens, shelter cats and animals living in crowded environments deserve particular attention.

Some infested animals show only mild symptoms. Therefore, the absence of obvious scratching does not necessarily mean that a cat is free of mites.

Why do ear mites cause so much itching?

The presence of mites on the ear canal epithelium produces irritation and stimulates inflammatory and immune responses.

Hypersensitivity to mite-associated substances may significantly intensify discomfort in susceptible animals.

This explains why two cats with similar infestations may show very different symptoms. One may scratch intensely, while another exhibits only subtle signs.

Recognizing the clinical signs

Common signs of ear mite infestation include:

  • Frequent scratching: especially around the ears, head and neck.
  • Repeated head shaking: an attempt to relieve irritation.
  • Dark earwax: brown or black discharge often compared to coffee grounds.
  • Excoriations: superficial injuries caused by persistent scratching.
  • Localized hair loss: particularly around the ears.
  • Ear sensitivity: discomfort when the affected area is touched.

The characteristic coffee-ground appearance is an important clinical clue, but it is not exclusive to ear mite infestation.

Other causes of otitis externa can produce similar discharge. Therefore, the appearance of earwax alone cannot confirm the diagnosis.

When the problem becomes more serious

Ear mites should not be regarded as a minor inconvenience.

Persistent inflammation, scratching and vigorous head shaking can contribute to several complications.

Aural hematoma

An aural hematoma develops when blood accumulates within the tissues of the external ear flap, often following trauma or vigorous head shaking.

The ear may become swollen, painful or deformed, requiring veterinary assessment.

Secondary infections

Inflammation can disrupt the normal ear environment and promote excessive growth of microorganisms.

These may include yeasts such as Malassezia pachydermatis and bacteria belonging to the genus Staphylococcus.

In such cases, eliminating the mites may not be sufficient. Secondary infections must also be evaluated and treated appropriately.

Deeper ear involvement

Severe or complicated cases may involve damage to the tympanic membrane and disease affecting the middle ear.

More serious manifestations can include severe pain, balance disturbances, persistent head tilt and other vestibular signs.

These symptoms require prompt veterinary attention.

How is the diagnosis made?

Diagnosis involves clinical history, examination of the ear and specific diagnostic procedures.

Otoscopy

A veterinarian uses an otoscope to examine the external ear canal. In some cases, tiny whitish mites can be seen moving across the dark earwax.

Microscopic examination

A sample of ear debris may be collected and examined under a microscope, often with mineral oil.

This technique allows identification of adult mites and immature developmental stages.

Ear cytology

Cytological examination helps detect yeasts, bacteria and inflammatory changes associated with otitis.

It is particularly valuable when a secondary infection is suspected.

Is effective treatment available?

Yes. Modern veterinary medicine offers highly effective antiparasitic medications against Otodectes cynotis.

Available drug classes include isoxazolines, such as fluralaner, sarolaner and esafoxolaner, and macrocyclic lactones, such as selamectin, moxidectin and eprinomectin.

These compounds are available in different formulations, sometimes combined with other active ingredients.

The appropriate product depends on the cat's age, weight, health status, local product approvals and the intended indication.

Treatment frequency and duration also vary according to the selected medication.

Never use dog medications, homemade remedies or ear preparations without veterinary guidance. Some substances are toxic to cats, while certain ear products can cause complications if the eardrum is damaged.

Should the ears be cleaned?

In some cases, gentle cleaning can help remove excessive wax and debris, improving comfort and facilitating treatment.

However, ear cleaning should be guided by a veterinarian, particularly when inflammation is severe or the integrity of the eardrum is uncertain.

Improper use of cotton swabs, instruments or liquids may push debris deeper into the ear canal or cause additional injury.

What about the other animals at home?

Because ear mites spread mainly through close contact, other cats and dogs living with an infested animal should also be assessed.

When indicated, simultaneous treatment of exposed animals helps interrupt transmission and reduce reinfestation.

Regular cleaning of bedding, blankets, grooming tools and shared objects provides additional support.

Although the mite relies primarily on its host to complete its life cycle, it can survive temporarily away from the animal.

Environmental hygiene is therefore useful, but it does not replace appropriate treatment of affected animals.

Can humans catch ear mites from cats?

Otodectes cynotis is primarily adapted to carnivorous animals, particularly cats and dogs.

Human involvement is considered rare. Isolated reports describe temporary skin reactions following exposure to heavily infested animals.

However, humans are not the mite's usual hosts.

In households with multiple pets, transmission between animals remains the main concern.

Prevention is part of responsible care

Prevention involves observing feline behavior, maintaining appropriate parasite control, arranging regular veterinary assessments and paying special attention when introducing new animals into the household.

Persistent scratching, dark discharge, unpleasant odor or ear discomfort should never automatically be dismissed as ordinary dirt.

These signs may indicate ear mites or another condition requiring a different therapeutic approach.

Tiny parasites, significant consequences

There is something remarkable about the relationship between cats and the microscopic world: sometimes, what we cannot see is precisely what causes the greatest discomfort.

A mite measuring only a few hundred micrometers can initiate a chain of events involving inflammation, itching, injuries and secondary infections.

Fortunately, with accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment, the prognosis for otodectic mange is generally excellent.

If your cat frequently scratches its ears or develops dark, coffee-ground-like earwax, seek veterinary advice. Do not attempt to medicate your cat at home.

Observing, understanding and acting at the right moment are essential parts of caring for the animals who share our lives.
