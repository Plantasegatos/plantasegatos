Vaska: The Cat Who Fed Three Generations During the Siege of Leningrad

The city the world shut out

On September 8, 1941, the German army closed its siege around Leningrad. Roads, railways, supply lines — all cut. The city would be isolated from the world for 872 days. The bread ration fell to 125 grams per day — largely sawdust and pressed bran. People boiled wallpaper paste as broth. They searched bookbindings for anything remotely edible. More than 800,000 civilians died — more than the United States and the United Kingdom lost together in the entire war.

In 1942, a second catastrophe compounded the famine. With the city's cats gone — eaten by the besieged themselves, who had no other choice — rats took over Leningrad. Eyewitnesses recall that the rodent colonies were so dense that when they crossed the streets, even trams were forced to stop. No weapon, no poison, no fire could contain them. The only predator capable of effectively stopping a rat — the cat — had vanished from the city.

In one of the houses of that besieged city, there was a ginger cat named Vaska. Thin like everyone else. But she observed. And then she went to work.

What a cat can do that a human cannot

Vaska's story is not a tale about unconditional love — though it is that too. It is above all a story about biology. About what a domestic feline can do that no starving, weakened, besieged human being could replicate.

Every day, Vaska went out. And every day she came back with something in her mouth — a mouse, a large rat, sometimes a sparrow. The grandmother gutted the mice and made stew. With the larger rats, she managed a goulash. One prey per day meant one night survived. The granddaughter, who would tell this story many years later, summarized it with absolute precision: "My grandmother always said that both she and my mother survived the Siege of Leningrad thanks to their cat Vaska. If it hadn't been for that cunning red-furred rascal, they would both have starved, as many others did."

What allowed Vaska to do this was not courage in the human sense. It was the sum of capabilities that 10 million years of feline evolution had deposited in that small, thin body:

  • Crepuscular vision: the tapetum lucidum behind the retina reflects light back through the photoreceptors, allowing the feline eye to operate in luminosity six times lower than the minimum a human needs to see. Vaska hunted in the darkness of corridors and snow-covered alleyways with the same efficiency as a predator in broad daylight.
  • Ultrasonic hearing: cats detect frequencies up to 65 kHz — the exact range of rodents' ultrasonic communication. While the grandmother heard absolutely nothing, Vaska located a rat several meters away by the vocalizations it emitted to other rats. It was like having a biological radar tuned to the precise frequency of the only available food source in the city.
  • Preserved hunting reflex: unlike dogs, which were selected over millennia to depend on humans for specific functions, the domestic cat never lost its complete predatory circuit. The hunting drive is not linked to hunger — it is linked to visual and auditory stimuli. A well-fed cat still hunts. A starving cat in a war winter hunted with the silent precision of a predator that never needed training.
  • Shared thermoregulation: at night, the three of them — the grandmother, her daughter, and Vaska — slept under the same blanket. The body heat of an adult cat is considerable for its size, and in an unheated apartment at −20°C in a Leningrad winter, that warmth was not comfort. It was physiological survival.

The radar that never failed

There was a fourth capability of Vaska's that the family learned to respect with the gravity of a life-or-death matter: she sensed the bombings before any siren.

When Vaska began circling the apartment in growing agitation, meowing with that specific anguish that differed from any other sound she produced, it was time to grab the essentials and run to the shelter. She never failed.

Science offers more than one explanation for this. Large-scale bombardments generate infrasound — vibrations below 20 Hz, inaudible to the human ear, that travel through the ground and air long before the explosion itself. Cats possess a hearing system that, combined with vibration sensors in their paws and whiskers (vibrissae), makes them capable of detecting environmental disturbances that the human nervous system simply does not register. The same capability that makes cats react to earthquakes minutes before seismographs record any activity was, in that Leningrad apartment, functioning as an anti-aircraft early warning system.

The Red Army reached this conclusion independently. At least one anti-aircraft battery on the outskirts of Leningrad had a ginger cat — called by the soldiers sluhach, "the listener" — who had installed himself there spontaneously. The soldiers discovered that the cat responded to the approach of enemy aircraft with greater consistency than the sound detection devices they had available. He was officially attached to the unit, with one soldier assigned to care exclusively for him. And he never confused a Soviet plane with a German one.

In a city where a cat was worth more than a salary

Vaska's value was no secret to anyone living in Leningrad. In January 1944, the writer L. Panteleev recorded in his siege diary: "A kitten in Leningrad costs 500 rubles." A watchman's salary was 120 rubles. A kilogram of bread sold for 50 rubles on the black market. A live cat was worth more than four monthly salaries — not as a luxury, but as a survival tool.

In the bomb shelter, no one touched Vaska. She was recognized for what she was: not a pet, but a functional member of a survival unit. When the grandmother had to leave for any task, she locked Vaska in a separate room — not out of affection, but because a lost cat in Leningrad in 1942 was a death sentence for her family.

The best piece was always hers

The siege was finally broken on January 27, 1944. Food returned. Life returned, slowly, with the slowness of everything that has been destroyed and needs to be rebuilt from zero.

Vaska lived until 1949. In all the years that followed the end of the siege, the grandmother saved for the cat the best piece of every meal. She would stroke her and call her, with that mixture of gratitude and affection that doesn't quite fit in any single word: "our breadwinner."

When Vaska died, the grandmother buried her in the cemetery with a cross on which she wrote "Vasily Bugrov" — a human name for a being who had done what few humans could have done under those circumstances. When the grandmother died, her daughter buried her next to the cat. When the daughter died, the granddaughter reunited them both. Today, the three of them lie together under the same tombstone — as in the war, the three together under the same blanket.

The 5,000 who came after

Vaska was not alone in the story of Leningrad's cats — she was simply the one who had the privilege of having her story preserved by a granddaughter who knew how to tell it.

When the siege was breached in 1943, the president of the Leningrad Soviet signed a resolution ordering the "extraction from the Yaroslavl region and delivery of grey cats to Leningrad" — considered the best rat catchers in the Soviet Union. The residents of Yaroslavl did not fail the strategic order. Four railcars of cats arrived in the half-ruined city. The animals were distributed among the inhabitants — and ran out immediately.

Later, a second expedition brought 5,000 cats from Omsk, Tyumen, and Irkutsk — most of them Siberians, a breed historically celebrated for its hunting ability. Residents of those cities voluntarily brought their domestic cats to collection points to help Leningrad. The animals arrived having not eaten for the several days of the journey. When released into the basements of the Hermitage and the city's palaces, they unleashed their instincts within hours. Within days, the rats had been controlled.

The descendants of those heroic Siberian cats still work in the Hermitage today. There is a monument to their memory on Malaya Sadovaya Street in St. Petersburg: two bronze cats named Yeliseyev and Vasilisa, eternally posted on the facade rooftops, guarding the city their ancestors helped save.

What Vaska truly was

It is tempting to tell Vaska's story as a story about loyalty — and it is that too. But reducing it to loyalty means missing what is most fascinating about it: the practical demonstration, documented under extreme conditions, that the domestic cat is a functional predator that never relinquished the tools that made it what it is.

Vaska did not choose to be a heroine. She did what her body had always known how to do: hunt in the dark, detect the invisible, warm whoever was nearby. The fact that this saved three human lives over 872 days of one of the most devastating sieges in modern military history is a consequence — extraordinary, moving, real — of biology in its most essential state.

Every time a domestic cat stops suddenly in the middle of a room, turns its head toward a corner where you see nothing, and stands absolutely still for three seconds before launching — it is accessing exactly the same neurological architecture that Vaska used to feed a family in the winter of 1941.

The difference is that in your living room, the target is probably an insect.
