Did Curiosity Kill the Cat? What Science Reveals About Feline Exploratory Behavior

An empty cardboard box. A partially open door. An unfamiliar sound coming from another room. For many cats, any of these situations can trigger a thorough investigation.

The cat approaches slowly, moves its ears, sniffs, observes, and eventually decides whether to move closer. Sometimes the discovery is nothing more than a shopping bag. On other occasions, it may reveal an opportunity to play, a hiding place, or even a hazard.

But why do cats seem so interested in discovering what surrounds them? The answer involves evolution, learning, sensory perception, and an essential aspect of feline life: gathering information about the environment.

Curiosity as a survival tool

In nature, exploration is not simply recreational. For an animal that must locate resources, recognize threats, and identify hunting opportunities, understanding its surroundings can be essential for survival.

Domestic cats (Felis catus) retain many behavioral characteristics associated with their evolutionary history as predators of small animals.

Investigating a space helps them identify hiding places, potential prey, escape routes, territorial changes, and unfamiliar objects.

However, not all cats are equally curious. Age, temperament, previous experiences, socialization, and environmental conditions influence individual willingness to explore.

A confident cat may approach a new object immediately. Another may observe it for several minutes before deciding to investigate. Both are gathering information, but using different behavioral strategies.

A brain that learns through exploration

Exploratory behavior is closely associated with learning. When a cat interacts with an unfamiliar object, it receives sensory information that may influence future responses.

This process involves learning, memory, and neural plasticity — the nervous system's capacity to modify its functioning and connections in response to experience.

Brain systems involved in motivation and reward also contribute to exploration in mammals. Dopamine, a neurotransmitter associated with these systems, plays a role in motivation, learning, and reward prediction.

However, it would be an oversimplification to claim that every new stimulus automatically triggers dopamine release in a cat's brain. Responses depend on context, experience, and the significance of the stimulus.

Through exploration, animals can learn which experiences are beneficial, which are neutral, and which should be avoided.

Neophilia and neophobia: curiosity and caution

Two concepts from animal behavior science help explain how cats respond to unfamiliar situations.

Neophilia describes an attraction to novelty and a tendency to investigate unfamiliar stimuli. Neophobia, on the other hand, refers to caution or avoidance in response to something new.

These responses are not necessarily contradictory. The same cat may eagerly explore a newly delivered cardboard box while avoiding an unfamiliar household appliance.

Behavior depends on factors such as stimulus intensity, predictability, previous experiences, and perceived safety.

Hesitation therefore does not necessarily indicate a lack of curiosity. Observing from a distance can itself be part of the investigative process.

Why do cats love boxes, tunnels, and hiding places?

An ordinary cardboard box can quickly become one of a cat's favorite places.

Partially enclosed spaces provide opportunities for shelter, rest, and observation. They can also reduce visual exposure and give cats greater control over their surroundings.

A study published in Applied Animal Behaviour Science found that newly admitted shelter cats provided with hiding boxes showed lower behavioral stress scores than cats without them.

Although this does not mean that every cat prefers boxes under all circumstances, it supports the importance of appropriate hiding opportunities for feline welfare.

Boxes, tunnels, and similar structures may also encourage exploration and play.

However, safe hiding places should be distinguished from hazardous objects. Plastic bags, packaging with handles, and spaces where a cat might become trapped should not be offered as unsupervised toys.

The senses behind exploration

When cats investigate their surroundings, they rely on much more than vision.

Their sense of smell helps detect odors associated with other animals, food, people, and environmental changes. Hearing allows them to locate sounds, including those produced by small movements.

Whiskers, technically known as vibrissae, are specialized sensory hairs that detect mechanical stimuli and contribute to tactile perception of nearby spaces.

Cats also possess a vomeronasal organ, which is involved in detecting certain chemical signals.

Together, these sensory systems allow cats to experience their surroundings in ways that differ considerably from human perception.

Even a simple package brought in from outside may carry unfamiliar scents that make it especially interesting to investigate.

When curiosity becomes dangerous

Exploration is adaptive, but it does not guarantee that cats can recognize every danger found in modern homes.

Unprotected windows and balconies present fall hazards. Chewed electrical cables can cause electric shocks and burns. Cleaning products, medications, and certain foods may cause poisoning.

Some ornamental plants also pose serious risks. True lilies, including plants in the genera Lilium and Hemerocallis, are particularly dangerous to cats and can cause acute kidney injury even after relatively small exposures.

Other plants, such as dumb cane (Dieffenbachia spp.), can cause severe oral irritation. The type and severity of toxicity vary between plant species.

Washing machines, dryers, cabinets, and other enclosed spaces should also be checked before use because they may attract cats seeking shelter.

Protecting a cat therefore does not mean suppressing curiosity. It means reducing the hazards associated with exploration.

Environmental enrichment: exploring safely

Environmental enrichment involves providing resources and opportunities that allow animals to express natural behaviors safely.

For domestic cats, this includes opportunities to climb, hide, observe, scratch, play, search for food, and interact with their surroundings.

Secure shelves, scratching posts, suitable tunnels, safe cardboard boxes, interactive toys, and puzzle feeders can help create a more stimulating environment.

Play sessions that simulate aspects of hunting also provide opportunities for physical exercise and behavioral engagement.

Enrichment should always reflect individual preferences and abilities. An older cat may benefit from easily accessible structures, while a young, active cat may enjoy more complex challenges.

It is equally important to let cats choose when to interact with enrichment activities rather than forcing exposure to unfamiliar stimuli.

So, did curiosity kill the cat?

The familiar saying warns that excessive investigation can have dangerous consequences. There is some truth to that warning: exploring unsafe environments can result in accidents.

Yet from the perspective of behavioral science, curiosity is much more than a source of trouble.

It contributes to learning, resource discovery, and adaptation to environmental change. Together with caution, it forms an important part of the feline behavioral repertoire.

Our responsibility as caregivers is not to eliminate curiosity but to create conditions in which it can be expressed safely.

Perhaps the best answer to the old saying is this: curiosity does not have to kill the cat. In a suitable environment, it can contribute to a richer, more active, and more stimulating life.
