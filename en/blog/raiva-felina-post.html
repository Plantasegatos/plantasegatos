Rabies in Cats: The Virus That Hijacks the Brain

A virus with its own strategy

Few pathogens in nature are as elegantly brutal as the rabies virus. It is no exaggeration to say that it uses the host's nervous system as a vehicle — literally traveling through axons to the brain, reprogramming behavior, and securing its own transmission before killing the animal. It is one of the oldest infectious diseases documented by humanity, and even with effective vaccines available for decades, it still causes tens of thousands of human deaths per year worldwide.

For cat owners, understanding rabies goes far beyond knowing that "there's a vaccine." It means understanding why that vaccine is non-negotiable, how the virus acts inside the feline body, what signs to watch for — and, crucially, why a cat with rabies cannot be saved, only prevented.

The causative agent: Lyssavirus and its fatal architecture

Rabies is caused by Rabies lyssavirus, belonging to the family Rhabdoviridae, genus Lyssavirus. The genus name comes from the Greek lyssa, meaning "madness" or "fury" — a linguistic choice that precisely describes the effects of infection on the central nervous system. The virus has a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA structure, wrapped in a lipid envelope studded with glycoproteins that act as entry keys into nerve cells.

All mammals are susceptible to rabies. No species is immune. The difference between hosts lies in their epidemiological role: some act as natural reservoirs (bats, foxes, raccoons), while others — like dogs, cats, and humans — are accidental hosts that rarely sustain the chain of transmission.

How the virus reaches the cat

The primary route of transmission is the saliva of an infected animal inoculated directly into the bloodstream — almost always through a bite. Deep scratches can also transmit the virus, though less frequently. Superficial contact, such as petting or licking on intact skin, does not transmit the disease.

In the Brazilian context, bats play a central role in transmission to domestic cats. Cats with outdoor access have a natural predatory behavior that frequently exposes them to bats — and all types of bats, not just blood-feeding ones, can be vectors. Brazil's Ministry of Health recorded 241 rabies cases in dogs and cats between 2015 and 2024, and cats are considered high-risk precisely because of this interface with wild animals.

The virus's journey through the nervous system

Once inoculated, the virus does not immediately enter the bloodstream. First, it replicates in muscle cells near the bite site. Then it begins one of the most studied journeys in virology: retrograde axonal transport. The virus hijacks the transport mechanisms of motor and sensory neurons, using them as a track to slowly migrate toward the spinal cord and, subsequently, the brain.

The speed of this transport — estimated at 12 to 24 mm per day in experimental studies — explains the variability of the incubation period. In cats, this period can range from two weeks to several months, depending on the distance between the bite site and the central nervous system, the viral load inoculated, and the depth of the wound. A bite to the head has a much shorter incubation period than one on a hind paw.

Upon reaching the brain, the virus spreads rapidly throughout the central nervous tissue and migrates in an anterograde direction to the salivary glands, eyes, skin, and other organs. This is the point at which the animal becomes infectious — and it is also when clinical symptoms appear.

Two clinical forms: furious and dumb

In cats, rabies presents in two distinct forms, and it is important to know both because the behavior of an infected animal can be profoundly misleading.

The furious form is the most common in cats and corresponds to the popular archetype of the disease: the animal becomes progressively agitated, hypersensitive to stimuli such as light (photophobia) and noise, extremely aggressive, and exhibits erratic behavior. Abnormal vocalizations, excessive salivation, vomiting, and convulsive seizures are frequent. A naturally gentle cat may attack without provocation. The disease progresses to paralysis, coma, and death within three to seven days of the onset of clinical signs.

The dumb form (or paralytic) is more insidious: the animal does not exhibit marked aggression. Progressive paralysis — typically beginning in the hind limbs and advancing forward — is the predominant sign, often accompanied by inability to swallow, dropped jaw, and drooling. An unsuspecting owner may approach what appears to be an ordinary sick cat, unwittingly exposing themselves to the virus.

In both forms, the outcome is invariably fatal. There is no effective treatment once neurological symptoms begin.

The virus's signature: Negri Bodies

From a pathological standpoint, rabies leaves a unique microscopic mark: Negri bodies. Described by Italian physician Adelchi Negri in 1903, these are intracytoplasmic inclusions formed by aggregates of viral proteins within infected neurons — especially in Purkinje cells of the cerebellum and pyramidal neurons of the hippocampus. They are considered the only pathognomonic finding of the disease, meaning specific enough to confirm post-mortem diagnosis by histopathology.

Definitive diagnosis in living animals is difficult and rarely clinically justified: it involves nuchal skin biopsy, PCR of saliva or cerebrospinal fluid, and direct immunofluorescence — techniques that, even when positive, do not change the animal's prognosis. In practice, confirmatory diagnosis is performed post-mortem for epidemiological surveillance purposes and to define the post-exposure prophylaxis protocol for people who had contact with the animal.

Why cats are a critical link in the transmission chain

Historically, urban rabies was discussed primarily in relation to dogs. That perception has shifted. Public health studies have increasingly identified domestic cats — especially semi-domesticated or free-roaming ones — as an underestimated vector, for reasons specific to feline biology and behavior.

First, cats actively hunt bats, the primary viral reservoir in contemporary Brazil. Second, territorial and stalking behavior means that encounters with wildlife are frequent and discreet — owners often do not witness the bite. Third, antirabies vaccination in cats has historically been less rigorous than in dogs, creating gaps in population immunity.

A University of São Paulo study on rabies in domestic cats highlighted that predatory behavior and feline aggressiveness, combined with transmission potential via bite or scratch, make cats an epidemiologically relevant link at the interface between the sylvatic and urban cycles.

Vaccination: the only effective strategy

The antirabies vaccine is legally mandatory in Brazil and should be administered from four months of age, with annual boosters. In many municipalities, vaccination is provided free of charge at Zoonosis Control Centers during annual campaigns. For indoor cats, the vaccine remains strongly recommended: open windows, balconies, and the inadvertent entry of bats are sufficient to create exposure risk.

Globally, the World Health Organization leads the Zero by 30 program — the goal of eliminating human deaths from dog-transmitted rabies by 2030. The central strategy is achieving 70% vaccination coverage in the dog and cat population, the epidemiological threshold capable of interrupting sustained transmission. Twenty-eight of Latin America's 35 countries already report no human deaths from canine rabies — a result directly attributable to decades of vaccination campaigns.

What to do in case of exposure

If a person is bitten or scratched by a cat suspected of having rabies — or even by a cat of unknown origin — the protocol is immediate: wash the wound thoroughly with running water and soap for at least 15 minutes and seek healthcare for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) evaluation, which may include vaccine and antirabies serum depending on the exposure risk. Do not wait for symptoms. The window of opportunity for prophylaxis exists precisely because the virus is still in transit through the peripheral nervous system before reaching the brain.

The cat involved should be kept under veterinary observation for ten days (if alive) or have its brain collected for laboratory diagnosis (if deceased), as directed by local health authorities.
