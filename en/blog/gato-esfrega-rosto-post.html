Face, Cheek, and Butt: How Cats Use Pheromones to Welcome You Into Their Group

You are trying to read, work, or simply exist in peace, and your cat materializes out of nowhere, climbs onto your lap, rubs their cheek against your forehead, turns around, and presents their rear end directly at your face. Tail raised, anal glands a few centimeters from your nose. To an outside observer, it is at best perplexing. To feline behavioral science, it is one of the highest gestures of trust a cat is capable of performing.

This post covers the entire ritual — from bunting to the classic "catbutt in your face" — and the sophisticated chemical system that turns each of these interactions into a declaration of belonging.

A Chemical Laboratory Hidden in the Face

Cats have sebaceous glands distributed strategically across the head. The perioral glands sit around the mouth and chin; the temporal glands occupy the sides of the forehead; and the pinna glands are located at the base of the ears. Together, they secrete a complex of chemical compounds known as feline facial pheromone — and this substance is far from uniform.

Researchers have identified five distinct fractions, designated F1 through F5, each with specific functions. The roles of F1 and F5 have not yet been fully established. F2 is associated with sexual behavior, deposited mainly by intact males near females in estrus. F3 is used to mark objects in the environment — furniture, door frames, wall corners — functioning as an olfactory map that communicates "this place is familiar and safe." This is the fraction behind the synthetic product Feliway Classic.

And then there is F4. This is the fraction deposited during allorubbing — when a cat rubs their face against another animal or a person. F4 is directly linked to the formation and maintenance of social bonds. It does not mark territory: it marks relationships.

Allorubbing: The Language of Those Who Belong Together

The term comes from the Greek allos, meaning "other." It describes the behavior by which a cat rubs part of their body against another individual — human, feline, or animal of another species — with whom they have already established a relationship of familiarity and trust. Unlike territorial marking through urine or scratching, allorubbing is never directed at strangers. In feral cat colonies, it is observed almost exclusively among individuals sharing the same social group, most often as a greeting ritual after a period of separation.

Studies show that more than 80% of cats actively rub against their caregiver upon reunion after an absence — a figure that positions allorubbing not as a quirk but as an active social protocol. When your cat meets you at the door with that sequence — first the purr, then the cheek against your ankle, then the head into your hand — they are running the same script they would use with a trusted feline companion.

The behavior also includes what researchers call tail wrapping: when the cat curls their tail around your leg or arm during the approach. The tail base also carries sebaceous glands, and the wrapping is both a tactile gesture of affection and a pheromone deposit. It is the feline equivalent of a hug.

Why the Preferred Target Is the Face

A cat's preference for the human head region during bunting is not random. In cat-to-cat interactions, allorubbing occurs predominantly around the head and neck — precisely the areas a cat cannot lick on their own and which are prioritized in allogrooming, the mutual social licking behavior. By rubbing their face against your face, the cat is mimicking the care pattern they would use with a trusted feline. You are being treated as an equal.

There is also a relevant anatomical detail: cats have a dual olfactory system. Beyond the conventional olfactory cells in the nasal cavity, they possess the vomeronasal organ — also known as Jacobson's organ — a specialized structure located in the palate just behind the upper incisors. While the nose detects conventional volatile odors, the vomeronasal organ specializes in capturing pheromones: chemical molecules with no perceptible scent that carry social and reproductive information. A cat's total olfactory space is approximately four times larger than a human's, and Jacobson's organ adds an entirely different layer of chemical perception to that system.

This means that when your cat rubs their face against yours, they are not merely smelling you — they are reading a detailed chemical signature about you while simultaneously overwriting part of it with their own. The result is a shared scent: the smell of the group.

The Butt in Your Face: The Tribute Nobody Asked for and Everyone Deserves

And then comes the culminating moment: the cat turns around, raises their tail vertically, and presents the perianal region at a distance that leaves no room for ambiguity.

In feline ethology, the vertically raised tail is one of the most unambiguous signals of friendly intent. A 1997 study from the University of Southampton demonstrated that cats willingly approach silhouettes with raised tails and avoid those with lowered tails — the same signal kittens use to greet their mother. When your cat approaches you with their tail held high, they are signaling: "I am here, I am friendly, you may receive me."

The presentation of the posterior region goes beyond the visual signal. At the tail base and around the anus, glands produce individualized pheromones — a kind of unique chemical identity card for each animal. In cat-to-cat greetings, sniffing this region is the standard recognition protocol: the feline equivalent of a handshake with business card exchange, only infinitely more informative. By turning their rear toward you and raising the tail, the cat is making that access available. They are offering you the opportunity to "read" who they are.

The fact that most humans choose not to take advantage of the offer — for understandable reasons — does not diminish the gesture. On the contrary: the cat continues doing it because, from their perspective, it is the most natural and respectful thing in the world.

There is also the dimension of vulnerability. Exposing the posterior region is, evolutionarily, a risky act — it means leaving a blind spot unprotected in the presence of another individual. When your cat does this, they are communicating that they do not perceive you as a threat. That vigilance can be suspended. It is trust expressed in chemical and postural form.

The Elevator Butt: When You Scratch and the Cat Lifts

There is a related phenomenon every cat owner knows: you scratch the base of the cat's tail and they automatically raise their hindquarters, sometimes walking slightly forward as if trying to escape their own reaction. This has a name — the elevator butt — and both a neurological and behavioral explanation.

The tail base is one of the regions with the highest density of nerve endings and sensory neurons in the cat's body. Stimulation in that area activates the caudal glands and triggers a reflex response rooted in kittenhood: kittens raise the posterior region to allow their mother to clean the perianal area. The reflex persists into adult life as a motor memory associated with care and comfort. When a cat raises their hindquarters because you scratched the tail base, they are, at some level, responding to you as they would respond to a maternal figure.

It is simultaneously endearing and mildly disconcerting. As is almost everything in feline ethology.

What Happens When the Cat Does None of This

Not all cats are equally expressive in these behaviors. Breed, personality, early socialization history, and past experiences all influence the frequency and intensity of bunting and allorubbing. More reserved cats may express their bond in subtler ways — lying near their caregiver without touching, purring when observed, slow blinking. These are equally valid languages of trust.

But if your cat rubs their face against you, presents a raised tail in greeting, and occasionally decides that your face is the ideal destination for their rear end — consider yourself included. You have been assessed, approved, and chemically registered as a member of the group. That is no small thing for an animal that, until very recently on the evolutionary scale, was solitary by nature.
