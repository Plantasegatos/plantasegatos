Jock and Churchill: The Cat Who Lived Through History

A man who loved cats

Winston Churchill is remembered for his speeches, his cigar, and his leadership during the Second World War. But there is a lesser-celebrated side to the British statesman: his near-irrational devotion to cats. Churchill and his wife Clementine always kept both dogs and cats in their homes, but it was the felines that captured the Prime Minister's heart in a special way. Throughout his life, several cats crossed his path — and left their mark on history.

Nelson: the wartime cat

Churchill's most celebrated cat of the wartime period was Nelson, a black tomcat who lived at Admiralty House when Churchill served as First Lord of the Admiralty. The adoption was sealed the moment Churchill watched the cat chase a large dog off the premises. Impressed by the animal's courage, Churchill named him after Britain's greatest naval admiral. "Nelson is the bravest cat I ever knew," Churchill once said, while slipping pieces of salmon under the table for the cat.

When Churchill became Prime Minister in 1940, Nelson came with him to 10 Downing Street — where he quickly entered into a territorial rivalry with the cat left behind by predecessor Neville Chamberlain, ironically nicknamed the "Munich Mouser." Two cats, two opposing political worlds, one address.

Jock: the cat of farewell

If Nelson was the wartime cat, Jock was the cat of old age — and perhaps the most beloved of all. In 1962, on his 88th birthday, Churchill received a gift from his private secretary Sir John "Jock" Colville: a marmalade cat with a white bib and four white paws. The cat was named after the nickname of his giver.

Jock quickly became inseparable from the statesman in his final years. He appeared sitting on Churchill's lap in formal photographs taken at a grandson's wedding — a quiet, warm presence in the last public images of the British leader. On 27 July 1964, when Churchill visited the House of Commons for the last time, Jock appeared in the foreground of the photograph taken outside his London home. Churchill passed away on 24 January 1965, at the age of 90. Jock was by his side on the bed until the very end.

The cat outlived his owner and continued to live at Chartwell — Churchill's country estate in Kent — until his death in 1974, at the age of 13. He is buried in the pet cemetery on the family grounds.

The perpetual legacy: the Jocks of Chartwell

When Chartwell was donated to the National Trust to become a historic monument open to the public, the Churchill family included an unusual clause: the property must always be home to a marmalade cat with a white bib and white paws, always named Jock. A sentimental requirement transformed into institutional tradition.

Since then, the line of Jocks has never been broken. Jock II took over after the original's death. The succession continued through III, IV, V and VI — each one rescued, each one matching the coat pattern specified in Churchill's affectionate bequest. In May 2020, Chartwell welcomed Jock VII, a six-month-old RSPCA rescue kitten who quickly established himself as head of household — investigating the gardeners' work and exploring the lawns of the historic estate.

It is one of the most unusual traditions in British heritage: a cat with a lifetime appointment, with selection criteria set by a Prime Minister dead for more than half a century.

What this tells us about Churchill — and about cats

Churchill's relationship with his cats was not decorative. It was functional, emotional, and in his final years, profoundly human. The felines attended important meetings, slept in the statesman's bed, and featured in official correspondence. In 1935, Churchill wrote to his wife Clementine, who was on a cruise in the South Pacific, to report on the behaviour of Tango, another family cat: "The cat treats me with great graciousness and always wishes to sleep on my bed (which I resent). When I dine alone, and only then, she awaits me at the table."

From an animal behaviour science perspective, this is unsurprising. Cats form deep bonds with consistent caregivers, especially in stable environments. Churchill, despite all the turmoil of public life, offered his cats precisely that: presence, attention and routine — even as the world around him was in upheaval.

Jock was not merely a pet. He was a silent witness to decades of history. And he continues to be, with each new generation of marmalade and white padding through the gardens of Chartwell.
