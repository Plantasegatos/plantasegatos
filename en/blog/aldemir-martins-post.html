Aldemir Martins and Cats: When Line Meets Freedom

What did Aldemir Martins see when he looked at a cat?

Perhaps exactly the same animal we see. Triangular ears, enormous attentive eyes, a spine capable of drawing improbable curves, a tail that seems to have a will of its own, and that unmistakably feline combination of elegance, independence and mystery.

But a great artist does not merely look at what stands before his eyes. He chooses what remains.

And Aldemir Martins found in the cat an extraordinary subject for his visual language.

It is said that it all began with a commission

A story frequently associated with Aldemir's cats tells of a woman who supposedly entered his studio and asked him to paint a cat. The artist reportedly asked for several days to complete the work.

The first cat was finished quickly — only to be purchased by someone else before the original client returned. More cats followed. More found new owners. And Aldemir supposedly continued painting until he was finally able to fulfill that first commission.

It is a delightful story, often repeated in accounts of his work, although it is best understood as a story associated with his career rather than an episode firmly established by a primary source.

Still, it leaves us with an irresistible image: what may have begun as a commission eventually became a language.

Before color, there is line

When we encounter one of Aldemir Martins' cats, color often captures us first. Deep blues, reds, yellows, greens and intense contrasts make his felines seem to inhabit a universe of their own.

But try a small exercise: forget the color for a moment.

Beneath it lies the drawing.

Aldemir was an extraordinary draftsman. Contour, the synthesis of form and the ability to construct a figure with very few elements lie at the heart of his visual language.

And perhaps that is precisely why the cat worked so beautifully in his hands.

Look at a real cat

Look at a cat sitting still.

Two triangles form the ears. A long curve follows the back. Another defines the chest. There is a sinuous tail and two eyes that often seem far too large for the face.

Now remove the fur.

Remove the shadows.

Remove the small details.

Keep removing.

Eventually only a few lines will remain — and, remarkably, there will still be a cat before us.

This power of synthesis helps explain the strength of Aldemir's felines. He did not need to reproduce every strand of fur to preserve the animal's identity. Posture, proportion, curve, eyes and gesture could be enough.

Then comes color

And this is where reality stops making the rules.

A cat no longer has to be black, white, ginger or tabby.

It can be blue.

It can meet an intense red. It can emerge among yellows, greens and great fields of color. Color ceases to be merely a description of fur and becomes part of the construction of the image itself.

The cat remains recognizable, but it no longer belongs solely to the natural world. It now belongs to Aldemir's universe as well.

Perhaps this is one reason his cats remain so immediately recognizable: there is freedom, but the essence is never lost.

But Aldemir was never simply the artist of cats

To reduce Aldemir Martins to his felines would be to miss precisely what makes his work so rich and so deeply Brazilian.

Born in Ingazeiras, Ceará, in 1922, Aldemir built an extensive body of work populated by roosters, fish, fruit, flowers, cangaceiros, landscapes and characters connected to Brazilian life and imagination.

He worked across drawing, painting, printmaking and illustration, moving between different techniques and supports. Throughout much of his production we find intense colors, strong contours and a remarkable ability to transform everyday subjects into powerful images.

His cats belong to that universe.

They are not an isolated chapter. They are part of a gaze that found visual beauty and power in the things, animals and people around him.

A cat remains a cat

Perhaps this is the most beautiful part of the story.

We can change the color. Simplify the body. Exaggerate the eyes. Extend a curve. Remove details. Transform reality into drawing.

And it is still there.

Cat.

Perhaps because some forms are recognized not only with our eyes, but through what we learn after observing an animal for a very long time.

Anyone who lives with cats knows this.

We recognize a cat from the shadow behind a curtain. From the tip of an ear. From the curve of a tail disappearing behind the sofa. From two eyes silently appearing in the darkness.

Aldemir seemed to understand the power of that synthesis remarkably well.

And perhaps that is why, when looking at his cats, we never miss every individual strand of fur.

The essential is already there.

Villa-Lobos to accompany this gaze

To accompany this story, we chose O Trenzinho do Caipira by Heitor Villa-Lobos.

Not because we intend to establish a historical connection between Villa-Lobos and Aldemir Martins. This association is ours.

It comes from the feeling that both artists accomplished something extraordinarily difficult: creating a sophisticated and deeply personal language that at the same time feels unmistakably Brazilian without ever having to explain Brazil.

Villa-Lobos did it with sound.

Aldemir did it with lines and colors.

And among the many things Aldemir chose to observe were these creatures: curious, elegant, independent, sinuous and utterly impossible to ignore.

Cats. Always cats.
