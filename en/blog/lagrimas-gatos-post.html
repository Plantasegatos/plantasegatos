Feline Tears: When Your Cat's Eyes Need Attention

Have you noticed those dark stains around your cat's eyes? Or constant tearing that leaves the fur damp? Tears in cats are a subject that raises many questions among pet parents, and while it's not extremely complex, it deserves careful attention.

Let's explore what's behind those teary little eyes and when it's time to seek veterinary help.

The Anatomy of Tears

Cats, like us, have a complete lacrimal system. Tears are produced by the lacrimal glands and have essential functions: they lubricate the cornea, protect against infections, remove debris, and maintain overall eye health.

The feline tear film is composed of three layers: an outer oily layer, a middle aqueous layer, and an inner mucous layer. When everything works well, tears are drained through the nasolacrimal ducts — small channels that run from the eyes to the nose. That's why when we cry (or when a cat tears excessively), the nose also gets wet.

When Tears Are Normal

A small accumulation of discharge in the corners of the eyes in the morning is absolutely normal. During sleep, tear production continues, but drainage decreases. Upon waking, you may notice a small amount of discharge — usually clear or slightly brown.

Brachycephalic breeds like Persians and Himalayans tend to show more tearing due to their flattened facial conformation, which can compress the tear ducts. In these cases, regular cleaning with moistened gauze is part of the care routine.

Warning Signs

Tearing becomes concerning when:

The discharge changes color: yellow, green, or extremely thick discharge may indicate infection.

The volume increases significantly: constantly wet eyes or tears running down the face.

There's redness: inflamed conjunctiva is a sign of irritation.

The cat scratches its eyes: indicates discomfort or pain.

Permanent dark stains appear: the famous "tear stains", especially in light-colored cats.

These signs can point to various conditions, from simple allergies to more serious problems.

Main Causes

Conjunctivitis: Inflammation of the conjunctiva is one of the most common causes of excessive tearing. It can be viral (like feline herpesvirus), bacterial, or allergic. Besides tears, you'll notice redness and sometimes the cat keeping the eye partially closed.

Tear duct obstruction: When the small drainage channels become blocked — by congenital malformation, inflammation, or debris — tears have nowhere to go and overflow. Flat-faced breeds are particularly prone.

Foreign body: A speck of dust, lint, or grain of sand can cause intense irritation. The cat usually blinks excessively and tries to scratch the eye.

Corneal ulcer: Scratches or lesions on the eye's surface are painful and produce abundant tearing. They require urgent veterinary treatment.

Allergies: Pollen, dust, cleaning products, or even certain foods can trigger allergic reactions that affect the eyes.

Entropion: A condition where the eyelid turns inward, causing the lashes to constantly irritate the cornea.

Veterinary Diagnosis

When you take your cat to the veterinarian with a complaint of tearing, the professional will perform a complete ophthalmologic examination. This may include a Schirmer test (measures tear production), fluorescein test (detects ulcers), intraocular pressure examination, and evaluation of the nasolacrimal ducts.

Sometimes bacterial cultures are necessary to identify the causative agent of infections.

Treatment and Care

Treatment depends entirely on the cause. It may involve antibiotic eye drops for bacterial infections, antivirals for feline herpesvirus, anti-inflammatories to reduce inflammation, deobstruction or surgery of tear ducts, removal of foreign bodies, or surgical treatment for entropion.

At home, you can help by keeping the area clean. Use sterile gauze moistened with saline solution or warm filtered water to gently remove accumulated discharge. Always clean from the inner corner outward, using fresh gauze for each eye.

Tear Stains

Those reddish-brown stains so common in white or light-colored cats are caused by porphyrins — pigments naturally present in tears that oxidize when exposed to light. They're not dangerous in themselves, but may indicate chronic excessive tearing that deserves investigation.

Regular cleaning can minimize stains, but if tear production is above normal, it's important to discover and treat the root cause.

Prevention

Some simple measures help keep your cat's eyes healthy: keep the environment free of strong chemical irritants, carefully trim long hair around the eyes (in long-haired breeds), keep vaccinations up to date (preventing viral infections), schedule regular veterinary check-ups, and observe changes, reporting them to the veterinarian early.

When Not to Wait

Seek immediate veterinary care if the eye suddenly becomes cloudy or bluish, there's significant swelling around the eye, the cat shows intense pain, the pupil changes size or shape, or tearing is accompanied by other systemic symptoms (fever, lethargy, loss of appetite).

Eye problems can progress quickly, and vision is too precious to risk.

Final Reflection

Your cat's tears tell a story. Sometimes it's just the story of a night's sleep, but other times they may be signaling discomfort, illness, or pain. Knowing your animal, observing subtle changes, and maintaining a good relationship with your veterinarian are the best tools to ensure those expressive eyes remain healthy and bright.

After all, a cat's gaze is one of its most striking characteristics — it's worth taking good care of it.
