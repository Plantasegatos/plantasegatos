Peterbald: the hairless cat from Saint Petersburg

A breed born from an experiment

In 1994, in the city of Saint Petersburg, Russian felinologist Olga S. Mironova crossed a male Donskoy — also known as Don Sphynx — with a world champion Oriental Shorthair female named Radma von Jagerhov. The result was four kittens that would become the founders of a new breed: the Peterbald. The names of these pioneers entered feline genetics history — Mandarin iz Murino, Muscat iz Murino, Nezhenka iz Murino and Nocturne iz Murino. In 1996, the breed was recognized by the Russian Selectional Feline Federation (SFF). TICA granted Championship status in 2009.

The genetics behind the bare skin

What makes the Peterbald scientifically fascinating is its hair-loss gene, inherited from the Donskoy, which functions in an autosomal dominant manner — unlike the recessive gene of the Sphynx. This means a single allele is sufficient to express reduced or absent coat. Importantly, genomic sequencing studies have confirmed that the Sphynx alopecia variant (c.816+1G>A in the KRT71 gene) is not present in the Donskoy or Peterbald, indicating that these are genetically independent mutations converging on similar phenotypes. A 2021 study published in Scientific Reports also identified a novel candidate variant in the LPAR6 gene associated with atrichia in a Peterbald, suggesting that the genetic architecture of hairlessness in this breed is more complex than previously understood.

The five-coat spectrum

Contrary to what the name might suggest, not every Peterbald is born completely naked. The breed displays five distinct coat phenotypes. The Bald type is completely bare skin, warm and soft to the touch, with no visible hair. The Flock type has an ultra-fine, barely perceptible covering, similar to peach fuzz. The Velour type has short, dense fur with a velvety texture. The Brush type — recognized by TICA for Championship competition since May 2008 — has wavy, somewhat stiff-textured fur. Finally, the Straight type has a short, flat coat similar to the Oriental Shorthair. The dominant hair-loss gene can cause kittens born with a coat to progressively shed it during the first 24 months of life — making it impossible to predict the adult phenotype from neonatal appearance.

Morphology: the slender body of Saint Petersburg

The Peterbald inherits the body structure of the Oriental Shorthair: an elongated wedge-shaped head with a softly pointed muzzle, large wide-base ears set low on the skull, almond-shaped eyes and a tubular body on long, fine legs. The tail is long and thin like a whip. The feet are notably long, with agile fingers capable of manipulating objects — a behavior frequently observed by owners and cited in feline behavior literature. The TICA standard establishes weight between 3 and 6 kg, body length up to 43 cm and life expectancy between 12 and 20 years.

Temperament: canine loyalty in a feline body

The Peterbald is consistently described in the literature as a cat of extreme loyalty, developing intense bonds with its owners and displaying low tolerance for prolonged solitude. Unlike most domestic cats, which value independence, the Peterbald tends to follow owners from room to room, seek constant physical contact and vocalize actively to communicate needs. The breed's intelligence is high: individuals are capable of opening doors, manipulating objects and learning commands. Sociability with children, dogs and other cats is generally high, but specialists recommend adopting in pairs when owners spend long hours away from home.

Thermoregulation and skin care

The absence of fur has direct physiological consequences. Without the insulating keratin layer, the Peterbald loses body heat far more easily than coated breeds, which accelerates metabolism to compensate. This implies a caloric need above the feline average, with frequent meals and a high-protein diet. The exposed skin requires regular hygiene: weekly baths with neutral pH products are necessary to remove sebum buildup that, in coated breeds, is naturally absorbed by the fur. The large ears accumulate wax rapidly and require weekly cleaning with an approved veterinary solution. Direct sun exposure should be avoided — depigmented skin is vulnerable to burns and, in prolonged exposures, to actinic damage. A 100% indoor lifestyle is strongly recommended.

Genetic health: what to monitor

The main cardiovascular risk for the breed is Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM), the most common hereditary heart disease in felines, characterized by abnormal thickening of the left ventricular myocardium. Although the most studied mutations in the MYBPC3 gene have been identified in the Maine Coon (A31P) and Ragdoll (R820W) breeds, HCM can affect any breed, including the Peterbald. Annual echocardiogram from 12 months of age is recommended protocol. A relevant genetic finding identified by Anderson et al. (2022) in a study with over 11,000 cats is that 26.5% of tested Peterbalds carried the CEP290 variant, associated with late-onset Progressive Retinal Atrophy — the highest frequency among all breeds analyzed in the study. Skin fragility, while not a disease, is a structural condition that requires weekly skin inspection for lacerations, dryness and signs of infection.
