FIP in Cats: Unraveling Feline Infectious Peritonitis

The story of a once-feared disease gaining new chapters of hope

If you live with cats, you’ve probably heard of FIP — three letters that have long struck fear in the hearts of guardians and veterinarians alike. For decades, a diagnosis of Feline Infectious Peritonitis was almost synonymous with a death sentence. But science has been rewriting this story with renewed hope. Let’s understand what this disease is, how it works, and — most importantly — what has changed in the treatment landscape.

What is FIP?

Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP) is a serious viral disease that affects only cats — both domestic and wild. Despite the term “peritonitis” suggesting an inflammation of the abdominal membrane (the peritoneum), FIP is actually a systemic illness that can affect multiple organs throughout the body.

The villain of this story is a coronavirus — but don’t worry, it has nothing to do with COVID-19 in humans. We’re talking about the Feline Enteric Coronavirus (FCoV), a virus extremely common among cats, especially those living in groups.

The coronavirus connection (but not the one you’re thinking of)

Here’s an important distinction: yes, FIP is caused by a coronavirus, but one from a completely different family than the human COVID-19 virus. The feline coronavirus does not infect humans, and the human coronavirus does not infect cats. They are species-specific viruses that evolved separately.

Interestingly, FCoV in its original form is rather harmless. It naturally lives in the intestines of millions of cats worldwide, usually causing only mild diarrhea — or no symptoms at all. It’s estimated that 25–40% of domestic cats and up to 80–90% of cats in shelters, catteries, or multi-cat environments carry this virus.

The fatal twist: when the virus mutates

The real mystery of FIP lies in mutation. In a small percentage of cats, the benign FCoV undergoes a spontaneous mutation inside the animal’s body, transforming into a far more aggressive version: the FIP virus (FIPV).

This mutation drastically changes the virus’s behavior. While the original FCoV stays confined to the intestines and is shed in feces, the mutated strain gains the ability to infect immune cells called macrophages, spreading throughout the body and triggering a destructive inflammatory response.

Science has yet to fully explain what triggers this mutation, but several risk factors have been identified:

  • Age: Very young (under 2 years) or senior cats (over 10 years) are more vulnerable.
  • Stress: Environmental changes, surgeries, introduction of new pets.
  • Weakened immunity: Cats with FIV or FeLV are at higher risk.
  • Genetics: Certain breeds like Persians, Ragdolls, and Bengals seem predisposed.
  • High-density environments: The more cats in one space, the greater the risk of mutation.

The two faces of FIP: wet and dry forms

Wet (Effusive) FIP

The most common and fast-progressing form. It’s characterized by fluid buildup in body cavities:

  • Swollen abdomen: Due to accumulation of yellowish, viscous fluid in the abdomen.
  • Breathing difficulty: When fluid collects in the chest cavity.
  • Persistent fever: Unresponsive to antibiotics.
  • Loss of appetite and weight: Despite the enlarged belly.
  • Severe lethargy: The cat becomes withdrawn and unresponsive.

Dry (Non-effusive) FIP

A subtler form and harder to diagnose. Instead of fluid accumulation, inflammatory nodules (granulomas) develop in various organs:

  • Neurological symptoms: Seizures, behavioral changes, poor coordination.
  • Ocular symptoms: Uveitis, color changes in the iris.
  • Kidney or liver issues
  • Intermittent fever
  • Progressive weight loss

Some cats may show a mixed or transitional form between the two.

How is the virus transmitted?

The original feline coronavirus (FCoV) spreads mainly through the fecal–oral route:

  • Shared litter boxes
  • Direct contact
  • Vertical transmission: From mother to kittens during nursing.
  • Fomites: Shared food bowls, water dishes, and toys.

Key point: FIP itself is not contagious from cat to cat. What spreads is the common FCoV — the mutation into FIP occurs individually inside each affected cat.

Diagnosis: a clinical puzzle

Diagnosing FIP remains one of veterinary medicine’s greatest challenges. There’s no single, definitive test. Diagnosis relies on combining several clinical and laboratory findings:

  • History and clinical signs.
  • Analysis of effusion fluid (if present).
  • Bloodwork showing anemia, lymphopenia, and high globulin levels.
  • RT-PCR testing (detects virus but doesn’t distinguish FCoV from FIPV).
  • Biopsy (most definitive, but invasive).
  • Ultrasound and X-rays for organ changes.

Dr. Niels Pedersen, professor emeritus at the University of California, developed diagnostic criteria still used as a reference today.

What has changed: the treatment revolution

For decades, FIP was 100% fatal. Treatment was purely palliative. But starting in 2019, everything changed thanks to antiviral research.

The antivirals that changed the game

GS-441524 demonstrated cure rates between 80% and 95% by inhibiting viral replication. Another antiviral, molnupiravir — known from COVID-19 treatment — showed similar results. Its active metabolite, EIDD-1931, has been used for neurological or refractory FIP cases.

The regulatory dilemma

Despite its proven efficacy, GS-441524 remains unapproved for veterinary use in most countries, including the U.S. and Brazil. This has created a gray market where desperate owners seek underground sources. Fortunately, pharmaceutical companies are now pursuing official approval pathways for veterinary formulations.

Treatment protocol

  • Dosage: 4–6 mg/kg for non-neurological cases; higher for neurological or ocular forms.
  • Route: Subcutaneous or oral.
  • Duration: Minimum of 12 weeks.
  • Monitoring: Regular blood tests and clinical evaluations.

The treatment is costly and requires full commitment from the caregiver, but it has turned once-hopeless cases into recoveries.

Prevention: can FIP be avoided?

There’s no guaranteed way to prevent FIP, but good hygiene and management can lower the risk:

  • Provide one litter box per cat, plus one extra.
  • Clean litter boxes daily.
  • Keep food and water areas separate.
  • Reduce stress and crowding.
  • Quarantine new cats for at least two weeks.

And what about the vaccine?

An intranasal vaccine called Primucell FIP exists in some countries, but its effectiveness is widely disputed. Studies show limited protection, mainly in cats never exposed to FCoV — which is rare. The American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) does not recommend its routine use.

Looking ahead: hope for the future

  • Regulatory approval for antivirals.
  • Development of new and improved molecules.
  • Immunomodulatory therapies to enhance immune balance.
  • More accurate and faster diagnostic markers.
  • Genetic studies identifying susceptibility factors.

Final message: from despair to hope

The story of FIP is a remarkable example of science transforming despair into hope. A disease once universally fatal now has cure rates above 80%. Cats once given weeks to live are now thriving years later.

If your cat has been diagnosed with FIP, talk to your veterinarian about available treatment options in your region. Early diagnosis and accurate information are your strongest allies.
