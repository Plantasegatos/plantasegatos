Gli and Tombili: The Two Cats That Conquered Istanbul (and the World)

Istanbul: the city that belongs to cats

Istanbul has 15 million human inhabitants. The exact number of cats nobody knows, but estimates speak of hundreds of thousands living freely through the streets, squares, mosques, and markets. Nowhere else in the world has the coexistence between humans and urban cats reached this level of almost mythological naturalness — not as a problem to be solved, but as part of the city's identity.

The documentary Kedi, released in 2017, showed this to the entire world. But before any camera arrived, two cats had already done it in their own way: one with the quiet dignity of someone who lives in one of the most important monuments in human history; the other with the incomparable art of doing absolutely nothing with an elegance the internet had never seen before.

This is the story of Gli and Tombili.

Gli: the queen of Hagia Sophia

Hagia Sophia was built in 537 CE by Roman Emperor Justinian I and is considered one of the masterpieces of architecture of all time. It served as an Orthodox cathedral for 916 years, as a mosque for nearly 500 years after the Ottoman conquest of 1453, as a museum for 86 years — and, for 16 years, as the home of Gli.

Gli was born in 2004 inside Hagia Sophia itself, daughter of a cat named Sofya. She had two siblings, Pati and Kızım. Her name in Turkish means "union of love" — chosen after the original name, "Gri" (grey), was changed because of her slightly crossed eyes and the irresistible way she looked at people. She was a European Shorthair tabby with slightly crossed green eyes, who quickly became the most charismatic character in the entire complex.

Archaeologist Defne Bali, who worked at Hagia Sophia for years, was like a mother to Gli from birth. The cat grew up surrounded by museum staff, restorers, and tour guides — and by millions of tourists who arrived from every corner of the world to admire the Byzantine mosaics and ended up leaving with the most vivid memory of a green-eyed cat who greeted them at the entrance as if the place were hers. Because it was.

The day Gli met Obama

In April 2009, then-US President Barack Obama visited Hagia Sophia accompanied by Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan. Cameras captured the moment when both leaders bent down to pet Gli, who received the attention with the serenity of someone accustomed to important visitors. The photo went around the world.

It was the beginning of Gli's global fame — but in Istanbul, she had already been famous long before. Tourists spent hours trying to find her inside the complex. She had her favorite spots, her routines, and a personality that guide Umut Bahçeci, who created the @hagiasophiacat Instagram account, would describe with one word: queen. "She would pose for photos, but if the environment got too crowded, she would disappear into a quiet corner," he said. "In winter, she loved to sit in front of the light projectors because they kept her warm."

Gli had only one kitten in her entire life: Karakız, a completely black cat — whose name means "Black Girl." Karakız's fate was tragic: hit by a tour bus, she suffered a spinal fracture and required intensive veterinary treatment. Gli's story was also a story with shadows.

The mosque conversion and the end of an era

In July 2020, following a Turkish government decision, Hagia Sophia returned to functioning as an active mosque — ending 86 years as a museum. The entire world asked: what about Gli? Presidential spokesperson İbrahim Kalın answered in a way that was written into history: "That cat has become very famous, and there are others who haven't become that famous yet. That cat will be there, and all cats are welcome to our mosques."

Gli stayed. But in September 2020, at 16 years of age, her health began to deteriorate rapidly. She was transferred to a veterinary clinic in the Levent district, where she remained in treatment for weeks. On November 7, 2020, Gli died.

Istanbul Mayor Ali Yerlikaya personally announced it on Twitter: "We will never forget you, Gli." The @hagiasophiacat account had 118,000 followers at the time of her death. Gli was buried on the grounds of Hagia Sophia itself — the only place she had ever known as home in her entire life.

Tombili: the philosopher of Kadıköy

On the other side of the Bosphorus, in the Ziverbey neighborhood of Kadıköy, a different cat was building her legend without needing historic monuments or presidents. Tombili — whose name is an affectionate Turkish nickname for chubby pets — was a grey and white street cat with a generous belly who had discovered a way of existing in public space that no human could ever replicate with such naturalness.

She would rest her back against a curb, stretch one paw forward over her belly, and gaze at the street with the expression of someone who has already resolved all the important questions of life and now simply observes. She was not sleeping. She was not in a hurry. She was, simply, living in the moment with a completeness that made passersby stop and smile.

The residents of Ziverbey knew her well. She frequented the street in front of a butcher's shop, was fed by locals and shopkeepers, and at one point weighed 10 kilograms — later going on a diet, to her neighborhood vet's relief. She was, according to those who lived alongside her, extraordinarily friendly and completely indifferent to the concept of urgency.

The photo, the meme, and digital immortality

At some point in 2015 or 2016, someone photographed Tombili in her characteristic pose. The image ended up on 9GAG and exploded. Within days, the internet had found its new guru of relaxation. The memes came — Tombili in business meetings, Tombili in political debates, Tombili with prayer beads and Turkish tea, Tombili photoshopped next to politicians, Tombili as an icon of passive resistance to contemporary stress. Hundreds of people began appearing on Ziverbey street to find her. The owner of the transportation company whose sign appeared in the background of the famous photo received phone calls from people in other countries asking exactly where the cat was.

Tombili became t-shirts, caps, stickers. She became a symbol. But she, on the street, kept leaning against the curb with the same serene indifference as always. Fame changed nothing for her. Probably because she never needed fame to already be exactly what she was.

The death, the petition, and the stolen statue

In August 2016, Tombili died. The veterinarian Dr. Serdar Özalaybey, a geophysicist who lived nearby and had been caring for her in her final days, believes she succumbed to an infected abscess on her hind leg. She was approximately seven years old — double the average life expectancy of a street cat, according to local estimates.

The entire neighborhood stopped. Residents placed flowers and handwritten notes at Tombili's favorite spot. One of the notes, translated from Turkish, read: "You will live in our hearts. Mascot of our street, beloved Tombili lost her struggle for life and closed her eyes to life on the first of August."

Batu Aksoy, from Izmir, launched an online petition asking the Kadıköy municipality for a statue at the exact location where Tombili used to rest. The petition gathered more than 17,000 signatures. Mayor Aykurt Nuhoğlu agreed. Sculptor Seval Şahin, herself a cat owner, made the sculpture voluntarily. On October 4, 2016, World Animal Day, the bronze statue was inaugurated. Hundreds of people attended. Other street cats also showed up — and sat beside the statue.

One month later, the statue was stolen. The outrage was immediate and global. Turkish MP Tuncay Özkan declared: "They stole the Tombili statue. They are enemies of everything beautiful." The municipality announced the theft with a crying emoji. Days later, shopkeepers on the same street found the statue and returned it. It was restored and re-inaugurated. Tombili returned to her place.

Today the bronze statue stands on Güleç Çıkmazı Street in Ziverbey. Tourists come from other countries specifically to sit beside it and take a photo in the same pose. Street cats still show up there occasionally, as if they know the place has something special.

Why Istanbul is the city of cats

Gli and Tombili are not exceptions in Istanbul — they are the most famous expressions of a much older relationship between the city and its felines. The Islamic tradition has well-documented stories of affection for cats: the Prophet Muhammad is said to have cut the sleeve of his robe rather than disturb a cat sleeping on it. Cats inhabit mosques, bazaars, and historic cemeteries without anyone driving them away. They are fed by residents, treated by community vets, sheltered by businesses that place boxes and blankets on sidewalks in winter.

In Istanbul, a street cat is not a problem. It is a neighbor.

Gli and Tombili lived this fully, each in their own way: one inside one of the most photographed buildings in human history, receiving presidents with the elegant indifference of someone who has seen a great deal; the other leaning against the curb of an ordinary street, teaching the world that the best response to hurry is simply to have none.

Two cats. One city. And a memory that, like any good feline story, politely refuses any attempt at being forgotten.
