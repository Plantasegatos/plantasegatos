Dewey: The Cat Who Saved a Library and What Science Can Explain

On a freezing morning in January 1988, the staff of the public library in Spencer, Iowa, found a small orange kitten inside the metal book-return box. He had spent the night trapped there, exposed to severe cold. He was weak, but alive.

Library director Vicki Myron helped warm him and arranged the care he needed. After approval from the library board and the city council, the kitten was vaccinated, neutered and officially adopted. A community contest selected his full name: Dewey Readmore Books, a playful reference to the Dewey Decimal Classification and the phrase “read more books.”

What began as a rescue became a nineteen-year relationship. Dewey was not simply a cat living between shelves. He became part of the library's daily life, welcoming visitors, appearing at events and forming relationships with staff and patrons. His presence attracted news coverage, documentaries, letters, donations and travelers who wanted to meet him.

A cat at the heart of a community

Spencer was facing economic hardship when Dewey arrived. The agricultural crisis of the 1980s affected many communities across the American Midwest, placing pressure on families, local businesses and public services. In that setting, the library offered free access to information, shelter, social contact and a sense of continuity.

Dewey became part of that environment. Accounts collected by Vicki Myron describe a sociable cat who tolerated activity and paid close attention to people. He rested on desks, joined meetings, appeared at children's programs and, according to patrons, often approached people who seemed to need company.

It is tempting to describe this behavior as a “sixth sense.” A scientifically responsible interpretation, however, requires caution. There is no evidence that cats diagnose emotional suffering or understand complex human experiences in the same way people do. Research supports something less supernatural but still remarkable: cats perceive social signals, recognize familiar individuals, integrate visual and auditory information and adjust their behavior to emotional context.

Can cats recognize people and emotional signals?

Yes, within clearly defined limits. Studies have shown that domestic cats can distinguish their caregiver's voice from unfamiliar voices. This does not mean they understand every word, but it demonstrates that they identify familiar acoustic patterns and connect particular voices with previous experiences.

Research also indicates that cats may use human behavior as a source of information in ambiguous situations. In experiments involving unfamiliar objects, many cats looked at their caregiver's expression and attitude before deciding how to respond. This mechanism is known as social referencing: an animal gathers information from another individual's behavior to assess whether a situation may be safe or threatening.

Other findings suggest that cats combine facial and vocal cues to discriminate between positive and negative emotional expressions in humans and other cats. They do not read minds, but they are sensitive to tone of voice, posture, movement speed, routine predictability and changes in social interaction.

A person who is sad, anxious or physically unwell may speak less, move more slowly, sit for longer periods or alter familiar habits. For a cat living with that person, these changes provide detectable information. Approaching may reflect curiosity, warmth seeking, an expectation of contact, previous learning or an established social bond.

Attachment is more than dependence

Cats were long portrayed as essentially solitary and emotionally indifferent. That stereotype confuses their usually solitary hunting strategy with their social abilities. Domestic cats are highly flexible: they may live independently, form groups and develop individualized relationships with humans and other animals.

A 2019 study used an adapted secure-base test to investigate feline attachment. Most of the cats displayed behavior consistent with secure attachment: after separation and reunion with their caregiver, they sought contact and then returned to exploring the environment. The findings suggest that, for many cats, a familiar human can act as a source of security in novel or stressful situations.

Dewey spent almost his entire life in the same library, surrounded by familiar routines, smells, voices and people. Such an environment supports social learning. He could recognize regular visitors, anticipate schedules and learn which behaviors led to petting, a lap or play. His apparent ability to find people who needed comfort may have emerged from a combination of sociable temperament, experience and sensitivity to subtle human cues.

Can a cat “heal” someone?

The word “heal” must be used carefully. Living with a cat does not replace medical, psychological or psychiatric treatment, and contact with animals does not produce the same effects for everyone.

Positive relationships with animals can, however, provide companionship, predictability, physical contact, support for daily routines and a sense of belonging. In some contexts, these factors are associated with lower loneliness and subjective improvements in well-being. Outcomes depend on the quality of the relationship, the person's needs, the animal's welfare and the surrounding environment.

In Dewey's case, his greatest impact may not fit into a single physiological measurement. He helped make the library a place people wanted to visit. He created conversations between strangers, drew children toward books and offered a shared focus of affection during a difficult period for the community.

When a presence changes a place

Dewey became known far beyond Spencer. He appeared in newspapers, magazines and productions about library cats, and he received correspondence from admirers. Visitors traveled to meet him, increasing the library's visibility.

He died on November 29, 2006, from complications related to a stomach tumor. He also had hyperthyroidism and was receiving medication. He had turned nineteen only days earlier. According to the Spencer Public Library, he died in Vicki Myron's arms.

In September 2008, Vicki Myron and Bret Witter published Dewey: The Small-Town Library Cat Who Touched the World. The book brought his story to readers in many countries and established Dewey as one of the world's best-known library cats.

What Dewey truly teaches us

Dewey's story does not require supernatural powers to be extraordinary. An abandoned kitten survived, received care and found an environment in which his social qualities could flourish. In return, he became part of the identity of a public institution and of the emotional memory of thousands of people.

Science helps explain how this could happen. Cats recognize familiar signals, learn routines, perceive behavioral changes and can form attachment bonds with humans. Science also warns against excessive certainty: we cannot know exactly what Dewey felt when he approached a particular person, and individual stories cannot be turned into universal rules about the feline mind.

Perhaps his greatest contribution was simpler. Dewey was present. In a library, a place created to preserve stories and connect people, a cat became a living story. He did not save a town alone, but he helped restore warmth, curiosity and human connection to a community space. Some transformations begin that way: with an open door, consistent care and a small animal learning that, after the cold, safety can exist.
