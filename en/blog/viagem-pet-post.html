Can They Travel? The Rules for International Travel with Animals

A dog, a cat, a ferret, a guinea pig and a bird may all live with us as companion animals. From a legal, animal-health and environmental perspective, however, they do not necessarily belong to the same category when crossing an international border.

For that reason, "will the airline accept my animal?" should not be the first question when planning international travel.

A more fundamental question comes first: may this species legally leave the country of origin, transit through the countries involved and enter the destination under the conditions of this particular journey?

The answer may involve animal-health law, biosecurity rules, wildlife controls, species protection, veterinary documentation and specific import requirements. Only after those issues have been assessed do the airline's commercial and operational rules become relevant.

First, determine what kind of movement is taking place

Not every journey involving a companion animal is treated in the same way under the law.

This article primarily addresses international non-commercial movement of companion animals: movement that is not intended to result in the sale or transfer of ownership of the animal.

This distinction matters. Sale, adoption involving transfer of ownership, commercial importation, breeding, certain forms of unaccompanied transport or movement of larger numbers of animals may bring the journey under a different regulatory regime.

The European Union, for example, expressly distinguishes non-commercial movement from other forms of animal movement and entry. Great Britain also applies additional rules where animals are sold, rehomed, transferred, travel outside certain time limits in relation to the responsible person, or exceed specified numbers.

An airline does not grant permission to enter a country

This is one of the most important distinctions in international animal travel.

An airline decides whether it will transport a particular species and under which operational conditions. Whether the animal is legally admissible into the destination territory is determined by the competent authorities and applicable law.

An airline may therefore accept a species while the destination country prohibits its entry or makes admission conditional upon permits, certificates, tests or quarantine.

The opposite is also possible: a country may legally permit a species to enter, while a particular airline or route does not accept that animal.

Therefore: acceptance for transport and legal permission to enter are different decisions.

Dogs and cats have more structured movement regimes

Dogs and cats are among the species for which international travel procedures are most developed. This does not mean that a universal passport, certificate or protocol exists.

Depending on the jurisdiction and origin of travel, requirements may include:

  • individual identification by microchip;
  • valid rabies vaccination;
  • a minimum waiting period following vaccination;
  • rabies antibody titration testing;
  • a veterinary or official animal-health certificate;
  • specific preventive treatments;
  • prior authorisation or notification;
  • entry through designated points;
  • documentary and identity checks at the border.

Requirements depend on the combination of country of origin, destination, animal-health status and species. Rules that applied to a previous journey should therefore not automatically be assumed to apply to another destination.

European Union: the legal framework changed in April 2026

The European Union illustrates why travelers should check the rules in force on the actual date of travel.

For many years, Regulation (EU) No 576/2013 was the principal legal instrument governing non-commercial movement of pet animals. Its transitional application continued until 21 April 2026.

From 22 April 2026, the framework moved to Regulation (EU) 2016/429, the Animal Health Law, supplemented for non-commercial movement of pet animals by Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/131.

For pet dogs, cats and ferrets, the current regime continues to include requirements relating to individual identification, rabies vaccination and, depending on origin, additional requirements such as rabies antibody testing and animal-health documentation.

The familiar European pet passport is associated with the regime for dogs, cats and ferrets. It does not place every animal kept as a pet into one common regulatory category.

What about a guinea pig?

Guinea pigs demonstrate particularly well why the word "pet" alone cannot determine the rules governing international travel.

Official European Union guidance specifically states that guinea pigs are not covered by the EU pet passport. For other animals, including rodents, rabbits, birds and reptiles, travelers must check the national legislation of the destination country.

Two journeys within the same general region may therefore produce different requirements depending upon the species and destination state.

Brazil: other companion animals require specific assessment

In Brazil, the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock — MAPA establishes specific requirements for the entry of companion animals other than dogs and cats.

According to MAPA, animals including ferrets, rabbits, hamsters, guinea pigs and chinchillas may enter Brazil provided all applicable requirements are met.

These include an Import Authorisation issued in advance by MAPA, compliance with the conditions determined by the Brazilian federal veterinary authority, the required documentation and, where applicable, an environmental licence issued by the competent Brazilian authority.

MAPA also expressly notes that sanitary restrictions may vary according to the species and origin of the animal.

It is therefore not technically correct to automatically apply the procedure used for a dog or cat to a ferret, hamster or guinea pig.

Great Britain: species and origin can substantially change the rules

For this subject, it is important to distinguish Great Britain — England, Scotland and Wales — from Northern Ireland, which has separate guidance.

For animals arriving from outside the European Union, current British government guidance states that a pet animal other than a dog, cat or ferret requires an import licence.

Requirements can become considerably more species-specific. Pet rabbits and rodents coming from outside the European Union also require a rabies import licence and must undergo four months of quarantine following arrival under the current guidance.

Birds have their own requirements, including animal-health certification and conditions relating to approved points of entry.

The British example illustrates why the broad term "other pets" can conceal very different animal-health regimes.

United States: there is no single federal rule for every species

In the United States, several agencies may have jurisdiction over different aspects of animal entry.

Pet rodents provide a particularly useful example. USDA APHIS Veterinary Services currently states that it has no animal-health requirements of its own for bringing a pet rodent into the United States from a foreign country.

This does not mean unrestricted entry.

APHIS itself directs travelers to consider other authorities. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — CDC, for example, prohibits the importation of rodents of African origin under the applicable rules. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service may also regulate importation, while the destination state or territory may impose additional requirements.

Consequently, "USDA APHIS has no requirement" is not equivalent to "the United States has no requirement".

Australia: biosecurity may prevent the journey altogether

Australia maintains particularly strict biosecurity controls.

The Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry currently states that, among vertebrate pets, only dogs, cats, rabbits, horses and selected species of birds from approved countries may be imported as pets, subject to strict conditions.

The authority expressly states that no other vertebrate animals are approved for import into Australia as pets.

A guinea pig therefore cannot simply be prepared according to a veterinary protocol and flown to Australia as a pet: under the current system it is not within the categories of vertebrate animals approved for pet importation.

An unusual pet is not necessarily a wild animal

Another common mistake is to treat the terms "wild", "exotic" and "companion animal" as interchangeable legal categories.

They are not.

Hamsters, guinea pigs, rabbits and ferrets may be treated as companion animals under a particular legal system without automatically being subject to the same rules as dogs and cats.

Conversely, an animal lawfully kept as a pet may belong to a species subject to wildlife or conservation controls.

Where a species is covered by the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora — CITES, permits, certificates or other controls may apply to import, export or re-export.

Countries may also maintain national measures that are stricter than the international minimum.

Therefore: being a pet does not erase the legal status of the species.

Companion, emotional support and assistance are different categories

Another layer of complexity arises when an animal assists a person.

Definitions and rights are not universal and must always be understood within the relevant jurisdiction.

In the United States, for air transportation purposes under the Air Carrier Access Act — ACAA, the Department of Transportation currently defines a service animal as a dog, regardless of breed or type, individually trained to perform work or tasks for the benefit of a qualified individual with a disability.

Under the current U.S. federal air-travel rule, emotional support animals, comfort animals and companionship animals are not service animals merely because they provide such support.

This does not diminish the benefit an animal may provide. It means that emotional benefit and legal status as a service animal are not the same thing.

There is also a second important distinction: even an animal recognised as a service animal for air-transport purposes remains subject to the destination country's animal-health and entry requirements.

CITES and animal-health law address different risks

A single journey can involve several authorities simultaneously.

A veterinary authority may assess animal-health risks and certificates. An environmental authority may assess wildlife status and conservation requirements. Border and customs authorities may examine entry documents. The airline determines whether the animal can be carried operationally.

Compliance with one requirement does not replace compliance with another.

A veterinary certificate, for example, does not replace a CITES permit when one is required. Likewise, an environmental licence does not replace an animal-health certificate required by the veterinary authority.

Only then does the airline come into the process

Once the legal and animal-health admissibility of the journey has been established, travelers must determine whether and how the carrier will transport the animal.

Airlines may establish conditions concerning:

  • accepted species;
  • transport in the passenger cabin;
  • transport in an appropriate aircraft compartment;
  • transport as cargo;
  • weight of the animal and container;
  • container dimensions and construction;
  • aircraft type;
  • route and connections;
  • temperature restrictions;
  • age or condition of the animal;
  • documentation required for acceptance for carriage.

The International Air Transport Association — IATA publishes the Live Animals Regulations — LAR, currently in its 52nd edition for 2026. It is one of the principal international technical references for the transportation of live animals by air.

The LAR should not, however, be confused with a universal entry authorisation. It addresses transport and operates alongside State variations, operator variations and requirements imposed by competent authorities.

The correct order for assessing a journey

  1. Correctly identify the species. A common name may be insufficient where regulatory classification depends on taxonomic identification.
  2. Determine the nature of the movement. Is it non-commercial? Does it involve sale, adoption, transfer of ownership or another purpose?
  3. Determine whether the species is admissible at destination. There is little value in preparing certificates for an animal whose import as a pet is prohibited.
  4. Check animal-health requirements. Microchips, vaccines, tests, treatments, certificates, permits, inspections and quarantine may apply.
  5. Check environmental legislation. Legal origin, wildlife controls, CITES requirements and national measures may apply.
  6. Check transit countries. A connection may create additional requirements, particularly where formal entry into the territory occurs.
  7. Check the airline's transport conditions. Only then should cabin transport, container requirements, weight, route, booking and other operational conditions be confirmed.

What happens when the requirements are not met?

Consequences vary according to the jurisdiction and the nature of the non-compliance, but they can be serious.

Authorities may order veterinary measures, deny entry, return the animal to the country of origin, impose quarantine or apply other measures permitted by law.

International animal travel should therefore never be planned exclusively from social-media accounts, previous travel experiences or outdated airline information.

A simple rule to remember

International travel with an animal involves at least four separate questions:

1. May the species legally enter?
2. Does the animal meet the animal-health requirements?
3. Are wildlife or conservation requirements applicable?
4. Will the airline transport the animal on that route?

Only when those answers are compatible does the journey begin to become legally and operationally possible.

In short: being a pet does not automatically mean being allowed to cross a border.

And an animal being allowed to travel to one country does not mean it is allowed to travel to every other country.

Important: animal-health, environmental, customs and transport requirements may change in response to legislative, epidemiological or biosecurity developments. This article is for informational purposes and does not replace consultation with the competent authorities. Before travelling, always verify the rules currently applicable to the species, country of origin, transit countries and destination, as well as the airline's specific transport conditions.
