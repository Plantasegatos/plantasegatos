Somali: When Beauty, Intelligence and Affection Come Together

Some cats capture our attention with their beauty. Others win us over with personality. The Somali seems to have decided there was no reason to choose between the two.

It is difficult to look at one without first noticing the exuberance: the semi-long coat, magnificent tail, large ears, expressive eyes and elegant body create an almost wild appearance. But spend a little time getting to know the breed and its real story begins precisely where the photograph ends.

Because the Somali is not simply a beautiful cat. It is a cat that wants to participate.

A story hidden in the coat

The Somali's history is inseparable from that of the Abyssinian. The two breeds are extraordinarily close, with the Somali essentially representing the semi-longhaired expression of its Abyssinian relative.

During the twentieth century, longhaired kittens occasionally appeared in Abyssinian lines. Exactly how the recessive longhair trait entered those bloodlines remains uncertain. The Cat Fanciers’ Association notes that the dramatically reduced Abyssinian population after World War II, together with cats of uncertain ancestry appearing in pedigrees from that period, may have contributed to its presence.

For breeders pursuing the short Abyssinian coat, these kittens were initially undesirable.

Then someone looked again.

During the 1960s, breeders began preserving and developing the longhaired cats. Evelyn Mague became an important figure in the breed's development in the United States and helped establish the name Somali, reflecting the geographical relationship between Somalia and historic Abyssinia, now Ethiopia.

The CFA began registering Somalis in 1976, and the breed achieved championship status in 1979.

It is a remarkable example of how a genetic trait can remain hidden for generations and, through careful selection, eventually become part of a recognized breed identity.

Beauty written into every hair

The coat is perhaps the Somali's most recognizable visual signature.

Its characteristic ticking is an agouti pattern in which individual hairs contain alternating bands of pigmentation. Instead of appearing as a simple flat color, the coat develops depth, warmth and an almost shimmering quality.

This is why a Somali can seem subtly different as the light changes.

The effect becomes even more dramatic with the breed's soft, fine, semi-long coat, fuller ruff, breeches and spectacular plumed tail — features that have helped earn the Somali its familiar “fox cat” nickname.

Yet beneath all that coat is not a heavy cat. The breed standard describes an agile, muscular and graceful animal with an alert, lively presence.

A cat that needs to know what you are doing

This may be one of the Somali's most captivating qualities.

Living in the house is not enough. The Somali wants to participate in it.

Somalis are intelligent, curious, active and highly interactive with both people and their environment. A shelf is not merely a shelf — it is an observation platform. A new box demands investigation. An open door represents a possibility. A moving toy deserves immediate attention.

For a Somali, territory exists vertically as well as horizontally.

Tall scratching posts, shelves, cat trees, hiding places, secure windows and interactive play can therefore become important parts of an enriching home.

This also means the Somali may not be the ideal choice for someone seeking a cat whose presence is barely noticed.

The Somali wants to be part of the story.

Energy does not mean emotional distance

We sometimes associate highly active cats with emotional independence. The Somali challenges that simplification.

Behind the curiosity is a social animal. Somalis are described as loyal, affectionate and intensely interested in what their humans are doing.

Perhaps this combination is exactly what makes them so compelling.

They explore, climb, investigate and play, but they also build strong bonds. Affection does not necessarily mean remaining motionless on someone's lap for hours. Sometimes love is expressed by staying close, following, observing and participating.

It is active companionship.

Somali and Abyssinian: two expressions of one history

Place a Somali beside an Abyssinian and their relationship becomes immediately apparent.

They share an elegant structure, large ears, expressive eyes, intelligence, athleticism and the characteristic ticked coat.

The most obvious visual distinction is coat length.

The Abyssinian carries a short, close-lying coat. The Somali transforms the same underlying essence into something more luxuriant: semi-long fur, a more pronounced ruff and breeches, and an extraordinarily full tail.

They are not competing stories.

They are two closely connected chapters in feline genetics.

Beauty also demands responsibility

Truly understanding a breed means going beyond appearance.

Certain inherited conditions are particularly relevant to Abyssinian and Somali lines. One is pyruvate kinase deficiency (PKDef), an inherited disorder capable of causing hemolytic anemia. Veterinary research has documented the condition in Somalis, and genetic testing can identify associated variants.

Progressive retinal atrophy (PRA) is another consideration in Abyssinian and Somali populations, with genetic testing available for specific inherited variants.

This completely changes what it means to “choose a breeder.”

Responsible breeding is not simply about producing beautiful cats. It requires knowledge of pedigrees and genetics, appropriate health testing, selection for health and temperament, proper socialization and transparency with the families who will eventually welcome those kittens.

Preserving a breed must also mean preserving its health.

Before the breed, there is an individual

Perhaps this is the most important lesson in studying any breed.

A standard helps us understand recurring characteristics. Genetics explains part of the history. Breed organizations describe morphology and expected temperament.

But no standard replaces the individual.

Every Somali has a personality, preferences, boundaries and an individual way of building relationships. Understanding breed tendencies should help us understand the cat better, never reduce that cat to a rigid checklist of expected behaviors.

A free spirit that chooses to love

After exploring its history, genetics, ticking, temperament and remarkable desire to interact with the world, it becomes difficult to see the Somali simply as an extraordinarily beautiful breed.

Perhaps its real beauty lies in the harmony between appearance and character.

There is movement in that luminous coat.

There is curiosity in those enormous eyes.

There is power in that elegant body.

And there is affection in the cat that insists on discovering where you are and what you are doing.

The Somali seems to carry something ancestral in its appearance while remaining profoundly domestic in the way it chooses to share life with us.

More than a breed, the Somali is companionship, intelligence and affection wrapped in beauty.

A free spirit that chooses to love.
