Are White Cats With Blue Eyes Deaf? The Truth Behind the Myth

You've probably heard someone say: "white cats with blue eyes are deaf." And then you wondered — is it true or just another folk belief about cats?

The short answer: it's not a myth, but it's also not an absolute rule. The relationship between white coat, blue eyes, and deafness in cats is real, scientifically documented, and has a fascinating genetic explanation. But not every white cat with blue eyes is deaf — although the chances are quite high.

The W Gene: The Mastermind Behind It All

White cats (not albinos) owe their color to a dominant gene called W, for "White." This gene acts like a mask: it suppresses the production of melanin, the pigment responsible for giving color to skin, fur, and eyes. Beneath this white mask, the cat has a "hidden" color pattern that simply doesn't manifest.

But the W gene doesn't just affect coat color. It's what geneticists call a pleiotropic gene — meaning a gene that influences multiple characteristics at once. The same gene that makes the cat white can also make the eyes blue (due to lack of pigment in the iris) and cause congenital deafness (by affecting inner ear cells).

These three characteristics — white coat, blue eyes, and deafness — are genetically linked, but not mandatory. The W gene guarantees white coat, but blue eyes and deafness are possibilities, not certainties.

The Real Numbers

Here's where things get concrete. According to studies compiled by the ASPCA and veterinary universities, white cats with other eye colors (green, yellow) have between 17% and 22% chance of being deaf. White cats with one blue eye (heterochromia) have about 40% chance. And white cats with two blue eyes? Between 65% and 85% are deaf.

A 1997 study was even more specific: among the white cats analyzed, 72% had total deafness. In other words: most white cats with blue eyes are deaf, but not all. Approximately 15% to 35% of them hear normally.

Why Does Eye Color Matter?

The connection between blue eyes and deafness isn't coincidence — it's biological. Melanocytes (cells that produce melanin) don't exist only in the skin and fur. They're also present in the iris of the eyes and, crucially, in the inner ear. In the ear, these cells help maintain the chemical balance of the fluid surrounding the sensory cells responsible for hearing.

When the W gene suppresses melanocytes, it can affect all these areas simultaneously: no melanocytes in the skin results in white fur; no melanocytes in the iris results in blue eyes; no melanocytes in the inner ear causes degeneration of the cochlea and, consequently, deafness.

Deafness in white cats is congenital and usually manifests in the first few weeks of life. The organ of Corti (the inner ear structure responsible for hearing) degenerates, and the cat never experiences auditory sensation.

The Curious Case of Odd-Eyed Cats

Cats with heterochromia — one blue eye and one of a different color — offer an interesting clue about how this genetics works. When an odd-eyed cat is deaf in only one ear (unilateral deafness), the affected ear is almost always on the same side as the blue eye. This reinforces the direct connection between the lack of pigment in a specific region and hearing loss in that same area.

Long-Haired White Cats: Even Higher Risk

Studies indicate that long-haired white cats are three times more likely to be deaf in both ears than short-haired white cats. The exact reason isn't completely clear, but it probably involves genetic variations associated with different coat types.

White Is Not the Same as Albino

It's important to distinguish white cats from albino cats — they're different conditions. White cats have the W gene that masks other colors, can have blue, green, yellow, or heterochromatic eyes, and are more prone to deafness. Albino cats have a mutation that completely prevents melanin production, their eyes are pinkish or light red (because blood vessels are visible without pigment), and albinism is not associated with deafness in the same way.

How to Tell If Your White Cat Is Deaf

Deafness in cats can go unnoticed, especially if it's in only one ear. Cats with unilateral deafness behave very similarly to cats with normal hearing.

Some signs that may indicate deafness: doesn't respond to sounds like calls, food package noises, or doors opening; doesn't wake up to noises; startles easily because they can't hear you approaching; meows very loudly because they can't regulate their own voice volume; shows no fear of typical loud noises like vacuum cleaners, thunder, or fireworks.

The definitive diagnosis is made through the BAER test (Brainstem Auditory Evoked Response), a non-invasive exam available at specialized veterinary clinics.

Living With a Deaf Cat

Deaf cats can live perfectly happy and healthy lives. They just need some extra care.

Keep them indoors, as deaf cats can't hear cars, predators, or other dangers. Approach from within their field of vision, stomping your foot on the floor to create vibrations before approaching. Use visual signals — cats can learn commands through gestures. Be careful when waking them: touch the surface they're lying on to create vibration first, rather than touching them directly. And identify them properly with an updated microchip and collar with information that the cat is deaf.

The Breeding Question

Because of the strong genetic link between white coat, blue eyes, and deafness, responsible breeders avoid crossing two white cats — especially if one or both are deaf. When both parents are white, the prevalence of deafness in kittens can reach 52% to 96%.

Many breed associations now require BAER testing for white cats before they're used in breeding programs, precisely to reduce the incidence of hereditary deafness.

Conclusion: A Partially True Myth

So, are white cats with blue eyes deaf? Many are — between 65% and 85%. But not all. The connection is real, genetic, and well documented. If you have or are thinking of adopting a white cat with blue eyes, it's worth getting a hearing test. And if they are deaf, don't worry: with the right care, they'll have a life as full as any other cat.

After all, cats already ignore us most of the time anyway. The difference is that a deaf one has a legitimate excuse.
