Love Licks Don't Hurt: What Science Says About Cat Licking

Anyone who lives with cats probably knows the feeling: a small, rough tongue brushing against a hand, an arm, or even a face. Sometimes it happens during a quiet moment of affection. Other times, your cat suddenly decides that you need a bath.

It is tempting to interpret these licks as kisses. After all, they often occur during moments of closeness and relaxation. But do cats really lick their humans out of love?

Animal behavior research offers an interesting answer: licking can be part of feline social relationships, but its meaning depends on context, individual experience, and the cat's body language.

Affection begins early in life

To understand the importance of licking, we need to return to the earliest moments of a cat's life.

Immediately after birth, mother cats lick their kittens to remove birth fluids, clean their coats, and stimulate essential physiological functions. In newborn kittens, stimulation of the anogenital region also helps trigger urination and defecation.

Maternal grooming therefore combines hygiene, care, and physical stimulation. It occurs within a period of intense bodily contact, when kittens receive warmth, nourishment, and protection.

Although these early experiences involve familiar physical contact, we should not assume that every lick performed by an adult cat represents a continuation of maternal behavior.

Allogrooming: when one cat grooms another

In ethology, grooming directed toward another individual is known as allogrooming, or social grooming.

Among domestic cats, this behavior may occur between individuals with established social relationships. One cat may lick another around the head, ears, or neck.

A study by Van den Bos (1998), examining the function of allogrooming in domestic cats, showed that social grooming is more complex than a simple expression of affection.

Although it may be associated with maintaining social relationships, grooming can also occur in situations involving tension. Some grooming interactions are followed by agonistic behavior.

Therefore, not every instance of social grooming should be interpreted as evidence of friendship or as an automatic stress-reduction mechanism.

Its meaning depends on which cat initiates the interaction, how the recipient responds, and what happens before and afterward.

Why does your cat lick you?

When licking is directed toward humans, interpretation requires even greater care.

Cats establish social relationships with people, recognize familiar individuals, and may develop attachment bonds with their caregivers.

Research by Turner (2017, 2021) highlights the complexity of human-cat interactions, while Vitale and colleagues (2019) provided evidence of attachment bonds in domestic cats.

However, these studies do not establish that an isolated lick is a direct measure of love or attachment.

Several explanations are possible:

  • Social interaction: licking may occur during close contact with a familiar person.
  • Sensory exploration: scents and tastes on human skin may attract a cat's attention.
  • Learning: licking may be reinforced when it produces attention or another pleasant consequence.
  • Grooming behavior: some cats extend grooming movements to nearby surfaces, including human skin.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive. The same cat may lick its caregiver for different reasons on different occasions.

Why is a cat's tongue so rough?

Anyone who has received several consecutive cat licks knows that the feline tongue is not particularly soft.

Its surface contains specialized papillae, some of which are keratinized and backward-facing. These structures help remove loose hair and distribute saliva during grooming.

Research by Noel and colleagues (2018) demonstrated that certain papillae have structures capable of transporting saliva into the coat, contributing to efficient grooming.

This anatomy produces the familiar sandpaper sensation.

Therefore, although the title of this article is an affectionate metaphor, repeated licking can cause discomfort or skin irritation.

When licking turns into a little bite

Many cat caregivers recognize a familiar sequence: their cat begins licking peacefully and suddenly delivers a small bite.

It is easy to interpret this as an especially intense expression of love. However, there is no scientific basis for assuming that every bite following a lick is affectionate.

Depending on the situation, the behavior may reflect arousal, play, a change in motivation, or discomfort with the interaction.

During physical contact, watch for rapid tail movements, backward-facing ears, body tension, head withdrawal, or attempts to interrupt the interaction.

These signals should be interpreted together rather than individually.

If your cat appears uncomfortable, stop the interaction and allow the animal to decide whether to approach again.

When excessive licking becomes a concern

Occasional social licking should be distinguished from repetitive or excessive grooming.

A cat that repeatedly grooms its own coat to the point of hair loss or skin damage may be experiencing dermatological problems, pain, parasites, or behavioral disturbances.

Persistent changes in grooming patterns deserve veterinary attention, particularly when accompanied by irritation, wounds, or changes in daily behavior.

It is also wise to prevent cats from licking open wounds, mucous membranes, or skin treated with medications and cosmetics, as some substances may be harmful if ingested.

Feline love has many languages

Cats do not rely on a single behavior to establish and maintain social relationships.

Voluntary proximity, physical contact, resting near a familiar person, head-directed interactions, and certain forms of vocal communication may all contribute to these relationships.

A cat that never licks its caregiver is not necessarily less attached than one that offers frequent little licks.

Attachment should be understood through the overall pattern of interactions and the animal's freedom to approach or withdraw.

Perhaps the beauty of these little licks lies precisely here: they may be part of a relationship built through familiarity, trust, and shared experiences.

Not every lick is a kiss. But some happen during such meaningful moments that it is perfectly understandable to experience them as gestures of affection.
