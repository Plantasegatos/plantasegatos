The Cat That Stopped the Kremlin: The Real Story of Dorofei

In March 2012, while then-President of Russia Dmitry Medvedev was abroad on a diplomatic trip, a rumor took over the Russian internet: his cat had vanished. What few realized at the time was that this small feline diplomatic crisis concealed a behavior extremely common among house cats — one that any cat owner would instantly recognize.

Who was Dorofei

Dorofei (2004–2014) was a Neva Masquerade, the long-haired variety of the Siberian breed, with blue eyes and the dense coat typical of cats adapted to the Russian climate. He belonged to the family of then-President Dmitry Medvedev and his wife, Svetlana Medvedeva, who had picked out the kitten in Saint Petersburg, where the breed originates.

Cats of this lineage with good pedigree can be worth the equivalent of hundreds of dollars — a detail that helped fuel media interest once the "disappearance" story broke.

The rumor that stopped Russian Twitter

It all started with a report from the Russian tabloid Sobesednik, published without citing any sources, claiming that Dorofei had escaped from the presidential residence in Rublyovo-Uspenskoye, outside Moscow. The story detailed "missing cat" posters put up around the neighborhood and mentioned a presidential deadline to find the animal by the weekend.

The result was immediate: the cat's name, written in Cyrillic, shot to the top of Twitter's trending topics — outranking even international celebrities. Russian users created memes, parody accounts and jokes about the cat's fate, including an edited image of then-Prime Minister Vladimir Putin wearing cat ears.

Enter the police (officially, not really)

The original report even claimed Moscow police were searching the area "under every bush" for the animal. Authorities, however, denied any search operation, calling the story unfounded. Still, the rumor had already taken on a life of its own online — what started as a tabloid blurb had turned into an international phenomenon.

The presidential denial

In the middle of his diplomatic engagements abroad, Medvedev — known for being an unusually active Twitter user among world leaders at the time — decided to settle the matter himself. In a lighthearted tone, he wrote that sources close to the cat confirmed he "hadn't gotten lost anywhere," thanking everyone for their concern.

The witty, direct reply was received as yet another chapter in the meme culture that surrounded the then-president on the Russian internet — and officially closed the "Dorofei case".

What actually happened?

Months later, Medvedev even published photos of Dorofei himself, calmly lounging on the wooden floor of the residence, as final proof that the cat had never gone anywhere. In practice, everything suggests Dorofei had simply found a quiet, comfortable corner somewhere inside the house — away from human eyes — and stayed there, completely indifferent to the chaos unfolding outside.

Why cats hide around the house

Behind the comical episode lies a real feline behavior, extensively documented by ethologists. Cats are, at the same time, predators and potential prey in the wild — and this dual nature shapes much of their behavior at home. Seeking out a hidden, elevated, or single-access spot is, above all, a safety strategy: from the top of a wardrobe or the back of a closet, a cat can observe its surroundings without exposing itself to threats.

This search for refuge intensifies whenever the routine changes — visitors at home, rearranged furniture, unfamiliar sounds, renovations, or even a new scent in the environment. It's no coincidence that many owners report "losing" their cat precisely on busier days: the cat hasn't disappeared, it has simply activated its natural protocol of assessing the situation from a secure vantage point before returning to its routine.

Another important point is that hiding doesn't always mean fear or stress. For many cats, it's simply part of their natural resting repertoire: enclosed, dark, warm spaces — like the inside of a wardrobe or behind a curtain — retain body heat and reduce visual and auditory stimuli, encouraging deep sleep. In other words, most of the time a "missing" cat at home is simply sleeping in what is, for them, the most comfortable and secure spot available.

In the end, Dorofei's story is the story of every house cat: while the world (or the Kremlin) panics, he's just taking a nap in the most unlikely corner of the house — convinced, from the top of his hideout, that everything is under control.
