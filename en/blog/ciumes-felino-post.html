Do Cats Get Jealous? What Science Says About Feline Behavior

You are petting one cat when another suddenly approaches, positions itself between you, and seems to demand attention. In another situation, someone hugs your cat's favorite person, and the cat begins vocalizing, attempts to interrupt the interaction, or even starts hissing.

For many cat caregivers, the interpretation is immediate: the cat is jealous. But is jealousy really part of the feline emotional and behavioral repertoire?

Science offers some fascinating answers, although current evidence does not allow us to conclude that cats experience jealousy in exactly the same way humans do. To understand these behaviors, we need to explore social bonds, competition for resources, and how cats perceive changes in their environment.

What Is Jealousy?

In humans, jealousy is commonly defined as an emotional response to a real or perceived threat to a valued social relationship.

Unlike envy, which generally involves wanting something another individual possesses, jealousy is associated with the possibility of losing a relationship, a position, or a form of attention to a third individual.

This dynamic involves three elements: the individual displaying the response, a valued social partner, and a potential rival.

In nonhuman animals, researchers investigate whether similar behavioral responses exist without assuming that they depend on the same cognitive and emotional processes observed in our species.

What Has Science Discovered About Feline Jealousy?

In 2020, a study published in Pet Behaviour Science directly investigated how domestic cats respond to a potential social rival.

Bucher and colleagues evaluated 52 cats in situations where either their caregivers or an unfamiliar person petted a realistic stuffed cat or a cushion.

The researchers wanted to determine whether cats would respond differently when an object resembling another cat received attention from an important person.

The results revealed behavioral differences between the experimental conditions. Cats living in households showed greater interest in the stuffed cat when it had previously been petted by their caregivers.

However, the researchers did not find sufficiently clear evidence to conclude that the animals were experiencing jealousy itself.

It is important to emphasize that the experiment used an object resembling a cat rather than a living rival. Therefore, the observed responses may also reflect curiosity, recognition of social stimuli, or interest in the caregiver's interaction.

The most cautious conclusion is that cats may display jealousy-like behaviors, but the emotional nature of these responses remains incompletely understood.

Do Cats Really Form Emotional Bonds With Humans?

For a long time, cats were considered essentially independent animals with little interest in social relationships with humans.

Modern animal behavior research presents a much more complex picture.

In 2019, a study by Vitale and colleagues, published in Current Biology, investigated attachment bonds between domestic cats and their caregivers.

Using a behavioral separation and reunion protocol, the researchers identified secure and insecure attachment patterns that resemble, in certain respects, those observed in studies involving dogs and children.

Cats classified as securely attached demonstrated an ability to use their caregiver's presence as a source of security, balancing environmental exploration with proximity-seeking behavior.

These findings suggest that relationships between cats and humans can represent much more than an association with food and shelter.

For many cats, the presence of a familiar person provides an important source of security, predictability, and social interaction.

Why Do Some Cats Seem to Have a Favorite Person?

In households with several people, it is common to observe cats that seek out a particular family member more frequently. They may choose that person's lap, sleep beside them, follow them around the house, or request their attention more often.

These preferences may be influenced by several factors:

  • A history of positive interactions with a particular person;
  • The frequency and quality of play sessions;
  • Respect for the cat's individual boundaries and preferences;
  • Predictability of human behavior and daily routines;
  • Previous experiences of security and comfort;
  • Individual personality and socialization history.

In 2017, Vitale Shreve and colleagues demonstrated that social interaction with humans can be highly valued by cats. Many of the individuals evaluated preferred human interaction over food, toys, or olfactory stimuli.

The study also revealed important individual differences. Not all cats share the same motivations or social preferences.

This helps explain why some cats appear particularly interested in the attention of certain people, while others display greater independence.

When a Cat Appears Jealous of Another Person

Imagine a cat resting on its favorite person's lap. Someone else approaches, places a hand on that person's shoulder, or initiates a hug. The cat interrupts the contact, vocalizes, or displays signs of tension.

To a human observer, the situation may appear to be an unmistakable demonstration of jealousy.

However, several alternative explanations are possible.

  • Attention-seeking: the cat may attempt to restore an interaction it finds rewarding.
  • Learning: if interrupting a conversation or a hug results in affection, the behavior may be reinforced and become more frequent.
  • Insecurity: another person's approach may alter a situation previously perceived as comfortable and predictable.
  • Fear or discomfort: movements, physical proximity, odors, or previous experiences may trigger a defensive response.
  • Competition for access: the cat may attempt to maintain access to a lap, resting place, or social interaction at that moment.

These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. A single behavior may result from a combination of different motivations.

Therefore, a cat that hisses at someone approaching its caregiver is not necessarily attempting to protect that person or expressing jealousy.

Hissing is primarily a warning signal that communicates discomfort and may function to increase distance from a stimulus perceived as threatening.

Competition for Resources Between Cats

Although cats can form social groups and establish affiliative relationships, their ability to live together depends on factors such as familiarity, previous experiences, individual compatibility, and resource availability.

In domestic environments, important resources include:

  • Food and water;
  • Litter boxes;
  • Safe resting places;
  • Hiding places and elevated spaces;
  • Toys and opportunities for exploration;
  • Access to positive social interactions with humans.

When these resources are limited, poorly distributed, or easily blocked by another animal, social tension may arise.

One cat may prevent another from using a passageway, approaching a food bowl, or accessing a resting place. In some cases, a cat may also interfere with interactions between another cat and a caregiver.

The AAFP and ISFM Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines, published in 2013, emphasize the importance of providing sufficient resources in separate locations, allowing cats to make choices and avoid unwanted interactions.

This approach reduces the need for competition and promotes more harmonious coexistence.

Can the Arrival of a New Cat Trigger Jealousy?

Introducing a new animal changes the social and sensory environment of resident cats.

Unfamiliar odors, changes in daily routines, altered access to resources, and the presence of an unfamiliar individual can become significant sources of stress.

Some cats begin requesting more attention from their caregivers, while others hide, avoid certain areas, or display defensive behaviors.

These changes are frequently interpreted as jealousy, but they may also reflect difficulties adapting to a less predictable environment.

The 2024 AAFP intercat tension guidelines emphasize that social conflict does not always involve obvious physical confrontations.

Persistent staring, blocking passageways, chasing, and avoidance may reveal social problems even when cats appear to coexist without fighting.

How Can We Recognize Signs of Tension and Discomfort?

Body language is one of the most important tools for understanding a cat's emotional state.

Signs that deserve attention include:

  • Ears turned sideways or flattened backward: may indicate discomfort, fear, or preparation for a defensive response.
  • Tail agitation: intense or repetitive movements may accompany increased arousal, frustration, or irritation.
  • Fixed staring: in certain contexts, may indicate vigilance or social tension.
  • Rigid or crouched posture: may indicate discomfort or readiness to react.
  • Hissing and growling: warning signals that frequently serve to increase distance between individuals.
  • Blocking and chasing: may indicate social tension, especially when one cat repeatedly avoids another.

None of these signals, in isolation, can establish that a cat is experiencing jealousy. Interpretation depends on the situation, the behavioral sequence, and the animal's individual characteristics.

It is also important to remember that purring, rubbing against people, or seeking a lap are not necessarily signs of competition. These behaviors may be part of normal affiliative interactions.

How Can We Reduce Conflict and Promote Feline Welfare?

Regardless of whether a particular behavior is classified as jealousy, several strategies can reduce tension and improve the quality of social relationships.

  • Distribute resources: provide water, food, litter boxes, and resting places in different areas of the home.
  • Respect individual preferences: allow each cat to choose when and how to interact.
  • Provide individual attention: offer play sessions and positive interactions suited to each animal's needs.
  • Create safe spaces: provide hiding places, elevated surfaces, and escape routes.
  • Introduce new animals gradually: allow unfamiliar cats to become accustomed to one another through scent exchange, controlled contact, and positive experiences.
  • Avoid punishment: shouting, spraying water, or forcing proximity can increase fear and damage social associations.
  • Respect warning signals: if a cat hisses or growls, stop approaching and allow the animal to move away voluntarily.

When behavioral changes appear suddenly, become intense, or involve frequent aggression, it is important to seek veterinary advice.

Pain, illness, and sensory changes can also affect a cat's tolerance of social interactions. In persistent cases, consultation with a professional specializing in feline behavior may be necessary.

Jealousy or Communication?

Cats are socially complex animals capable of forming emotional bonds, developing individual preferences, and modifying their behavior in response to changes in social relationships.

Whether they experience some form of jealousy remains an intriguing scientific question. However, current evidence does not allow us to attribute this emotion to every situation that appears to involve competition for affection.

Before interpreting your cat's behavior as jealousy, observe the context. Your cat may be expressing insecurity, discomfort, fear, frustration, or simply seeking an interaction it considers important.

Understanding cats does not mean denying their emotions. It means recognizing that those emotions may be expressed in ways that differ from our own.

What about your cat? Does your cat interrupt affection, approach when you interact with another animal, or display discomfort when someone gets close to its favorite person?
