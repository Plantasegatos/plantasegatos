Egyptian Mau: The Pharaohs' Cat That Conquered the World

The only domestic cat with real spots

When you look at an Egyptian Mau, you are looking at 4,000 years of living history. This is no exaggeration: Egyptian papyri and frescoes dating back to 1,550 BC already depicted cats with the same random spots, almond-shaped eyes, and the "M" marking on the forehead that we see today. The name couldn't be more literal — mau simply means "cat" (and also "sun") in ancient Egyptian.

But what makes this breed truly unique isn't just the history. It's the biology: the Egyptian Mau is the only domestic breed whose spots occur completely naturally, without any selective crossbreeding to create the pattern. The spots appear not only on the fur, but on the skin itself.

From Egyptian deity to near extinction

In ancient Egypt, killing a cat was a capital offense. When a cat died, the family would shave their eyebrows as a sign of mourning. The sun god Ra was depicted as a spotted cat slaying the serpent Apep. In the "Book of the Dead," a spotted cat appears among the deities of the afterlife.

The Romans were likely responsible for bringing these spotted cats from Egypt to Europe. Roman mosaics — including one found at Pompeii — already depicted cats with markings and body structure identical to the modern Mau. For centuries, the breed survived on the streets of Cairo and among Egyptian diplomatic nobility.

Near extinction came with World War II. The conflict devastated feline breeding programs across Europe, and the Egyptian Mau teetered on the edge of disappearing. Salvation came from an unlikely heroine: Princess Nathalie Troubetskoy, a Russian noblewoman in exile living in Rome. In 1953, upon seeing a cat belonging to the Egyptian ambassador in Italy, she was so captivated that she used her diplomatic contacts to import specimens directly from Cairo. In 1956, when she emigrated to the United States, she brought three Maus with her — a silver female named Baba, another named Liza, and a bronze male named JoJo — founding the Fatima cattery, from which most of today's American Maus descend.

The genetics behind the spots

What makes the Egyptian Mau's spots genetically special is that they are a naturally occurring spotted tabby pattern, not produced by crossbreeding with wild felids (as in the Bengal or Savannah). The gene responsible for the active spotted pattern is the Tsp allele (Spotted tabby), which suppresses the characteristic stripes of the mackerel tabby, transforming them into random spots.

Genomic analyses published in Science and in studies by Leslie Lyons (UC Davis) confirmed that the Mau belongs to the western breeds group, with shared ancestry with the Turkish Angora and Turkish Van — a reflection of Mediterranean trade routes that carried cats from the Middle East to Europe and back over the centuries.

The eyes are another notable genetic detail: the characteristic color is described as "gooseberry green" — a specific shade between green and yellow that doesn't appear frequently in other breeds. Kittens are born with amber eyes, which only acquire their definitive green color between 18 months and 2 years of age.

The body of an elite athlete

The Egyptian Mau is considered the fastest domestic cat in the world, capable of reaching 30 mph (48 km/h). This performance is no accident — it is the result of anatomy exceptionally adapted for speed:

  • Hind legs longer than forelegs, creating the characteristic "tiptoe" stance
  • A skin flap extending from the flank to the hind knee — similar to the primordial pouch but functionally distinct — that amplifies stride length during running
  • Compact, dense musculature, balancing the Burmese's compactness with the Siamese's elegance, according to the CFA standard

This conformation allows leaps of up to 6 feet from a standing position. It's no wonder the Egyptians used these cats to hunt alongside humans — frescoes show Maus capturing waterfowl in papyrus marshes.

Coat pattern and recognized colors

The Mau's spots are random in size and shape — no two individuals are alike, like fingerprints. They cover the body and limbs, while the head and tail display tabby stripes. All Maus have the scarab mark (the "M") on the forehead, a dark dorsal stripe from head to tail, and the characteristic "mascara lines" extending from the outer corners of the eyes.

The colors recognized by the main associations (CFA, TICA, FIFe) are:

  • Silver: pale silver base with black spots — the most common variation
  • Bronze: copper-toned base with dark spots, recalling the coat of Egyptian deities
  • Smoke: hairs with white base and black tips, creating a smoky effect with ghost spots visible in motion
  • Black, blue/pewter, and caramel: accepted in some registries, rarer in shows

Personality: loyal as a dog, selective as a pharaoh

The Egyptian Mau is intensely devoted to its family group — and largely indifferent to everyone else. With "their" humans, they are affectionate, vocal (with a soft, melodious voice characteristic of the breed), and involved in everything. With strangers, they can be reserved or clearly aloof, sometimes choosing a single person as "their human."

They are highly intelligent, high-energy cats: they truly hunt, learn to fetch thrown objects, open doors and drawers, and love exploring heights. The CFA describes the Mau in its own territory as "completely outgoing, with absolutely no fear and a ton of curiosity." They need daily mental and physical stimulation to avoid developing destructive behaviors.

A curious behavioral detail: the Mau tends to have a longer gestation period than most domestic cats — an average of 73 days, versus the usual 63–67 — a characteristic possibly related to its closer ancestral ties to North African wild cat populations.

Health: what every owner needs to know

In general, the Egyptian Mau is a naturally robust breed with good longevity (12 to 15 years). However, there are health conditions with elevated prevalence in this breed that deserve attention:

  • Urate urolithiasis: formation of urate urinary stones. Studies with over 5,000 cases indicate the Mau has an odds ratio of 44.41 for this condition — significantly above the feline average. Adequate hydration and monitored diet are essential.
  • Pyruvate Kinase (PK) Deficiency: hereditary condition that can cause hemolytic anemia. Genetic testing is available; responsible breeders exclude carriers from breeding programs.
  • Leukodystrophy: rare neurological disease described in the breed, still understudied.
  • Umbilical hernia: reported in some lines, surgically correctable during spay/neuter.

When acquiring a Mau, always request the parents' genetic test results from the breeder — especially for PK deficiency and urolithiasis.

A rare breed worldwide — and even rarer in Brazil

The Egyptian Mau ranks among the rarest breeds in the world. In 2022, only 39 kittens were registered with the British GCCF. In 2024, FIFe registered 113 specimens worldwide — 0.1% of total registered cats. In the 2018 CFA ranking, it appeared at position 27 among 45 breeds.

Anyone wishing to acquire an Egyptian Mau should research breeders registered with recognized associations and be prepared for waiting lists.
