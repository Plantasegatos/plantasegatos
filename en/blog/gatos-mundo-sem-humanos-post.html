Their World: How Cats Would Thrive Without Humans

The question science can answer

Imagine that tomorrow, humans simply ceased to exist. Empty cities, abandoned farms, supermarkets shuttered forever. For most domestic animals, the scenario would be catastrophic. For cats, it would simply be the beginning of a new chapter — and probably one in which they would fare remarkably well.

This is not a fantasy: it is a legitimate scientific question. And the answers, grounded in genetics, biomechanics, and ethology, point to a surprising conclusion: of all the animals that share our homes, the domestic cat is the one that least needed us to begin with — and the one that would least need us to continue.

An almost wild genome

The first clue lies in the DNA. Biologists describe the Felis catus genome as having a "highly conserved ancestral mammal genome organization" — in practical terms, this means the domestic cat has not diverged far from the wildcat at the molecular level. While dogs accumulated thousands of years of intense selective pressure to serve specific human functions — herding, guarding, hunting —, cats were never selected with that same rigor. They entered Neolithic villages on their own terms, drawn by rodents in grain stores, and stayed because they chose to.

A study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences identified only 281 genes with signs of recent selection in domestic cats. Dogs, by comparison, show far deeper genetic alterations in cognitive, morphological, and behavioral functions. In cats, the most notable changes involve glutamate receptors — linked to reward-based learning — and genes governing neural crest cell migration, which influence temperament and coat color. The survival architecture remained intact.

A body that needs no gym

From a physical standpoint, the domestic cat retains a body plan remarkably similar to its wild relatives. Slightly shorter legs, a marginally longer intestine — an adaptation to the opportunistic behavior of exploiting human scraps —, and a fractionally smaller brain. That is all. The rest is pure predator.

Retractable claws that preserve a sharp edge for the precise moment of use. Vision adapted for low-light conditions with an efficient tapetum lucidum. Ears capable of detecting frequencies up to 65 kHz — the exact range of rodents communicating ultrasonically. A vestibular system that enables the famous righting reflex, reorienting the body during a fall in fractions of a second. Fast-twitch muscles optimized for explosive sprints and leaps up to five times the animal's own body length.

None of these capabilities were built by humans. All of them existed before any cat ever crossed the threshold of a house.

Born hunters, even when well fed

There is a behavior that frequently puzzles — and sometimes frustrates — cat owners: the well-fed cat that still goes out to hunt. This is not ingratitude or excess energy. It is the normal operation of a nervous system that never decoupled hunting from feeding.

Ethological studies show that domestic cats in environments with outdoor access maintain active hunting behavior regardless of food availability. The predatory drive is triggered by visual and auditory stimuli — movement, frequency, texture —, not by hunger. In a world without humans, this circuit would simply resume its original function without any necessary adaptation period.

Feral populations already demonstrate this in practice. Oceanic islands where cats were introduced by sailors record dramatic impacts on bird and small mammal populations — direct evidence that Felis catus functions as an efficient predator even many generations after contact with humans.

Social plasticity as an evolutionary advantage

A common misconception is to imagine that cats are solitary by nature and would therefore face social difficulties in a world without humans. The reality is more sophisticated: cats are facultatively social. This means they can operate both as solitary animals and within collective structures, depending on resource availability.

Researchers have documented this phenomenon in urban and island feral populations. In environments with high prey density, cats form matrilineal colonies with cooperative kitten rearing. In environments with dispersed prey — such as the sub-Antarctic archipelagos —, they adopt solitary territories and individual hunting behavior. The same species, the same genome, two completely different modes of social organization — activated according to the local environment.

This plasticity is a tremendous advantage. In a post-human world with heterogeneous ecosystems — ruins full of rodents, open fields with birds and rabbits, forests with medium-sized prey —, cats would not need to choose a single strategy. They would adapt their social mode to the local context, generation after generation.

The mathematics of reproduction

Feline reproductive capacity is another factor that sets them apart from many other domesticated animals. An unspayed female can produce two to three estrous cycles per year, with litters of four to six kittens each. Under favorable conditions, it is estimated that a breeding pair and their descendants could generate more than 5,000 individuals in seven years — with natural selection continuously operating to eliminate the least fit.

The result, over just a few generations, would be a progressively more robust population: animals with greater parasite resistance, better hunting ability, and a temperament less dependent on human interaction. Domestication would be reversed not traumatically, but as a gradient — cats becoming increasingly felis, decreasingly catus.

The real challenges

It would be naive to ignore the difficulties. Contemporary urban feral cats have a short life expectancy — often less than five years — due to road accidents, infectious diseases without veterinary care, and conflicts with other predators such as coyotes, foxes, and feral dogs. Without humans, automobiles would cease to be a risk within weeks. Diseases — panleukopenia, rhinotracheitis, feline infectious peritonitis — would remain significant selective pressures.

But this is precisely where evolutionary logic takes over: these diseases would not extinguish cats, but would select for the genetically most resistant individuals. Within a few generations, herd immunity to common pathogens would consolidate in surviving populations. The same process that generated natural resistances in isolated feral populations — such as the cats of the Kerguelen Islands in the southern Indian Ocean — would repeat itself on a global scale.

A species built for two worlds

What makes the domestic cat especially fascinating in this speculative exercise is not merely its capacity to survive without us — it is the fact that this capacity was never lost. Unlike dogs, sheep, or cattle, which were genetically and behaviorally redesigned for human dependence, the cat maintained a foot in each world throughout the entire domestication process.

Domestic cats are, in the words of evolutionary biologist Stephen J. O'Brien, "free agents" — a classification that says more about the particular nature of this relationship than any behavioral analysis could. We chose to live together. And if that choice ended, only one side would need to start over from scratch.

Cats already know exactly where they left their wild roots. They never wandered far enough to forget the way back.
