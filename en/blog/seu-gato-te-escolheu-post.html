Did Your Cat Choose You? The Science Behind Favorite Humans

In a household with several people, it is common to notice something intriguing: the cat seems to have chosen one person in particular. This is the person the cat seeks out for sleep, follows from room to room, greets at the door, or chooses as a companion during quiet moments.

Interestingly, that person is not always the one who provides food, cleans the litter box, or spends the most time at home.

Why does this happen? Is it love, instinct, recognition, or some form of imprinting?

Ethology, the scientific study of animal behavior, offers fascinating answers. These answers reveal that relationships between cats and humans are considerably more complex than we often imagine.

Do cats really choose a favorite person?

Domestic cats (Felis catus) are capable of establishing individualized social relationships with both other cats and humans.

Although their ancestors evolved predominantly solitary survival strategies, domestication and coexistence with humans have contributed to remarkable social flexibility.

This means that a cat may develop different degrees of closeness, tolerance, trust, and affection toward people living in the same household.

Some cats seek particular people for resting, others for play, and others for physical affection.

These preferences do not have to be exclusive or permanent.

The expression "favorite human" is therefore a popular way of describing an observable social preference rather than a formal behavioral diagnosis.

Imprinting or attachment? An important distinction

The term imprinting became widely known through the work of ethologist Konrad Lorenz.

In certain bird species, newly hatched young can rapidly develop a preference for a particular stimulus during a sensitive developmental period. This learning may produce long-lasting effects.

However, directly applying this concept to relationships between cats and humans can be scientifically misleading.

For cats, the more relevant concepts are socialization, associative learning, individual recognition, and attachment bonds.

A cat does not need to have met someone during its first weeks of life to develop a strong relationship with that person as an adult.

Unlike the popular interpretation of imprinting, feline social bonds can continue to develop and change throughout life.

It begins early: kitten socialization

The first weeks of life are particularly important for feline behavioral development.

The sensitive period for socialization with humans is commonly placed at approximately two to seven weeks of age, although individual differences and variations between studies exist.

During this stage, positive, gradual, and respectful experiences with people can influence how a cat responds to human interaction later in life.

Kittens receiving gentle, predictable, and appropriate handling tend to become more comfortable with human proximity.

However, this does not mean that cats lacking early socialization are destined to avoid people.

Later learning, individual temperament, and environmental experiences continue to influence behavior throughout life.

Similarly, positive early socialization does not guarantee that a cat will be equally affectionate toward everyone.

Your scent, your voice, and your movements

To understand feline preferences, we must consider how cats perceive the world.

Smell plays a fundamental role in communication and environmental exploration. Familiar odors help cats recognize contexts and identify important elements of their daily routines.

Hearing also contributes to individual recognition.

In a study published in 2013, Atsuko Saito and Kazutaka Shinozuka demonstrated that domestic cats can distinguish their owners' voices from those of unfamiliar people.

Recognition, however, does not necessarily mean obedience or immediate approach.

Cats may show that they recognize a voice through subtle ear or head movements, even when they do not move toward the speaker.

Subsequent research has also investigated how cats respond to speech specifically directed toward them.

Thus, auditory, olfactory, visual, and behavioral cues contribute to a cat's familiarity with particular individuals.

Your cat may recognize far more about you than its apparently indifferent reactions suggest.

The person who feeds the cat is not always the favorite

Food is a powerful learning stimulus.

A cat may associate a particular person with the arrival of food, the sound of packaging, or regular feeding times.

However, social relationships are not limited to resource provision.

Pleasant interactions, opportunities for play, predictability, and respect for behavioral signals may also influence individual preferences.

In other words, the person who feeds the cat may be important without necessarily being the person the cat most frequently seeks for rest or physical contact.

A cat may prefer one person for play, another for sleeping, and another for requesting food.

These relationships reflect different experiences rather than necessarily forming a single hierarchy of affection.

The human who respects boundaries

One of the most important features of positive interactions between cats and humans is the opportunity for choice.

Cats often appear more comfortable when they can control the approach, duration, and intensity of physical contact.

Someone who observes feline body language, avoids forced interactions, and respects the cat's desire to withdraw is more likely to encourage positive experiences.

Conversely, repeatedly holding a cat that wants to leave, pursuing it to offer affection, or ignoring signs of discomfort may increase stress.

These signs include sudden tail movements, ears turned sideways or backward, body tension, and attempts to move away.

Respecting these signals does not mean showing less affection.

It means allowing the relationship to develop in a way that accommodates the animal's behavioral needs.

Can you be your cat's safe haven?

One of the most interesting discoveries concerning relationships between cats and humans involves attachment theory.

In 2019, Kristyn Vitale and colleagues published a study in Current Biology investigating attachment patterns between cats and their caregivers.

The researchers used an adaptation of an experimental procedure observing animal behavior during separation and reunion.

The results identified behavioral patterns consistent with secure and insecure attachment, resembling some aspects of attachment observed in other species.

In certain cases, the caregiver's presence appeared to function as a secure base, encouraging environmental exploration after reunion.

These findings do not mean that every cat depends emotionally on its caregiver in the same way.

Nor do they establish that cats perceive their caregivers exactly as kittens perceive their mothers.

Nevertheless, they provide important evidence that relationships between cats and humans can involve attachment processes rather than merely food-related associations.

Social bonds also involve biological processes

Social relationships are accompanied by complex physiological processes.

Oxytocin, for example, participates in mechanisms associated with social behavior and bonding across several mammalian species.

Cortisol is a hormone involved in the physiological stress response.

Research on human-animal interactions investigates possible changes in these and other physiological indicators during social experiences.

However, findings depend on species, context, individual characteristics, and research methodology.

It would therefore be an oversimplification to claim that every exchange of glances with a cat triggers oxytocin release in both individuals or that every petting session necessarily reduces cortisol.

What can be stated more confidently is that positive, voluntary interactions may support well-being, while unwanted contact can have the opposite effect.

How can you tell whether your cat prefers you?

There is no simple household test capable of scientifically determining a cat's favorite person.

However, certain behaviors may indicate familiarity, comfort, and voluntary social interaction:

  • Spontaneously seeking your company during resting periods.
  • Choosing to sleep near you when other options are available.
  • Approaching with relaxed body language and an upright tail.
  • Rubbing its head or body against you during friendly interactions.
  • Initiating physical contact and remaining nearby without discomfort.
  • Seeking your presence in certain unfamiliar or stressful situations.
  • Responding in distinctive ways to your voice or routines.

These behaviors must be interpreted together and within their context.

A cat that dislikes being held, for example, may still have a very strong bond with someone and express it simply by remaining nearby.

Likewise, purring or slow blinking alone does not definitively establish an exclusive preference.

Can your cat change its favorite person?

Yes. Feline social preferences can change.

Changes in routine, moving to a new home, the arrival of new people, positive or negative experiences, and changes in the animal's needs can all influence social interactions.

An older cat may begin seeking quieter environments. Another may spend more time with someone who regularly engages in play.

This does not necessarily mean that the cat has stopped liking someone.

Social relationships are dynamic, and different individuals may play important roles at different times.

Sudden behavioral changes, particularly when accompanied by withdrawal, irritability, or changes in appetite, may also indicate pain or illness and warrant veterinary assessment.

Feline affection does not have to be exclusive

Perhaps one of the most common misconceptions about cats is the idea that they must choose only one person to form a meaningful bond.

In reality, cats can maintain positive relationships with several family members.

Each interaction may have its own characteristics: play, physical proximity, shared rest, vocal communication, or simply peaceful companionship.

The fact that a cat seeks one person more frequently does not mean that everyone else is irrelevant to its well-being.

A bond does not need to be exclusive to be meaningful.

So why did your cat choose you?

Perhaps because your presence is predictable.

Perhaps because you have learned to recognize when your cat wants affection and when it prefers to be left alone.

Perhaps because your voice, movements, and routines form part of a familiar and secure environment.

Or perhaps because, through countless small experiences, you have built a relationship that works particularly well for both of you.

Science cannot directly translate these preferences into human emotions, but it demonstrates that cats can recognize people, learn from social experiences, and establish individualized bonds.

What we describe as being chosen by a cat may actually be the result of something built every day: familiarity, respect, security, and trust.

And perhaps that is the most beautiful part of the story.

Because a cat's trust cannot be demanded. It must be earned.
