The Mixed-Breed Cat (SRD): The Science, History, and Care of Brazil's Most Common Feline

It's everywhere: on the neighbor's wall, on the bakery corner, sleeping in the sun on the rooftop. The mixed-breed cat — known in Brazil as the SRD ("sem raça definida," or "no defined breed") and affectionately called the "stray" — is so common that almost no one stops to think about what it really is. And the answer is surprising: the SRD is not a "downgraded" version of pedigree breeds. It's exactly the opposite. It is the origin of all of them.

The stray paradox: the origin, not the exception

Before aesthetic standards, cat shows, and pedigree books, all cats were mixed-breed. They weren't chosen for their appearance, but for their ability to hunt rodents and live alongside humans. The modern breeds we know today — Persian, Siamese, Maine Coon — are recent, artificial derivations, created through human selection from that ancestral population.

Mixed-breed cats are estimated to make up roughly 80% to 90% of the world's feline population. In other words: the "stray" is not the exception to the rule — it is the rule. Purebreds are the statistical exception.

From the caravels to Brazil

Domestic cats share a single ancestral origin in the Near East, from where they followed human expansion across millennia. They reached Brazil aboard Portuguese ships during the colonial period, carried along for a very practical job: controlling rats and protecting the ships' food and ropes.

Once ashore, these cats spread and bred freely, without any human control over reproduction. It was this mixture, refined by generations of natural selection on the streets, cities, and farms of the country, that produced the Brazilian SRD as we know it: athletic, alert, and extraordinarily adaptable.

Hybrid vigor: what's true

The popular belief that "strays are tougher" does have a biological basis — and it has a name: hybrid vigor (or heterosis). Because they don't go through restricted, artificial crossbreeding, SRDs maintain far greater genetic diversity than most purebreds.

That diversity works as a buffer. It reduces the probability that recessive hereditary diseases — the ones that require two copies of the same defective gene to appear — will surface. The more genetically similar the parents are, the greater the chance a kitten inherits two copies of the same problem. The SRD's diversity dilutes that risk.

But careful: diversity is not immunity

This is where many people overstate the case — and where science calls for caution. Hybrid vigor reduces some risks associated with inbreeding. It does not make a cat immune to disease.

It's worth stressing: inbreeding doesn't create diseases out of nothing — it merely increases the chance of revealing problematic genes that already exist in the population. Likewise, the SRD's genetic diversity reduces the chance of revealing them, but doesn't eliminate the possibility. An SRD cat can develop kidney disease, dental problems, infections, and every other condition that affects any feline. Hybrid vigor is a statistical advantage, not a magic shield.

Behavior: caution as intelligence

The largest feline behavioral survey ever conducted, carried out by Professor Hannes Lohi's group at the University of Helsinki with thousands of cats, identified seven personality traits and showed that real differences exist between groups of cats — although the authors themselves are careful to note that they did not make direct rank comparisons between breeds.

Among the findings, non-pedigree cats were among the most sensitive to new things and unfamiliar situations. This is often read as "shyness," but there's a better interpretation: the SRD tends to observe before it trusts. That caution isn't a temperament flaw — it's a survival strategy refined over generations living in unpredictable environments. They assess risk before making contact. That's why early socialization and patience make all the difference: an SRD's trust is rarely automatic, but once earned, it tends to run deep.

Something almost no one knows: the SRD is not the "Brazilian Shorthair"

It's worth clearing up a common confusion. Brazil does have an internationally recognized cat breed: the Brazilian Shorthair (Pelo Curto Brasileiro), standardized starting in the 1980s through the work of Paulo Samuel Ruschi and officially recognized by the World Cat Federation in 1998.

But the Brazilian Shorthair is a breed — a selected, standardized lineage. The SRD is not. Although they look alike physically, they're different things: one is the result of a closed standard; the other is the free, diverse population from which that standard was extracted. When we talk about the Brazilian stray, we're talking about the SRD — and it's precisely the absence of standardization that gives it its genetic identity.

Care: prevention extends lives

With proper care and an indoor life, an SRD cat can live well for 15 to 20 years. But that longevity isn't automatic — it rests on four pillars.

Quality nutrition, balanced for age and weight, with constant hydration. Environmental hygiene, with a clean litter box and always-fresh water. Medical prevention: up-to-date vaccination (protection against panleukopenia, rhinotracheitis, rabies, and FeLV), regular parasite control, and neutering. And environmental enrichment: scratching posts, vertical space, and play that stimulate body and mind, reducing the stress behind many behavioral problems.

The SRD's hybrid vigor does not cancel out the biological need for science-based prevention. Know in order to care; prevent in order to protect.

Love without labels

Breed defines neither character nor intelligence. The Brazilian cat carries a history of adaptation, resilience, and hard-won trust — and delivers what no pedigree can buy. Adopting an SRD isn't a moment of charity: it's taking on responsibility for a complex biological life for up to two decades. And for those who accept it, the reward is rare: when an animal with a sharp survival instinct chooses to sleep in your lap, that is the most authentic form of trust there is.
