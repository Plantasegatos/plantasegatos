Cat Grass: What It Is, What It Does, and Which Species Are Best

If you share your home with a cat, you have probably witnessed this behavior: the animal approaches a small pot of grass, sniffs it carefully, and begins chewing the blades with apparent satisfaction. The habit is so common that it has generated an entire market of products generically called cat grass. But what exactly is this plant? Why do cats eat it? And does the species make a difference?

What cat grass actually is

The name "cat grass" does not refer to a specific botanical species — it is a functional category. Any germinated cereal offered to a cat for direct consumption can carry this label. The most common species belong to the Poaceae family and include wheat, barley, oats, rye, rice, and corn. What unites them is not taxonomy but use: young sprouts cultivated indoors and made voluntarily available to the cat.

Why cats eat grass

Science has not reached a definitive consensus, but the main hypotheses are well documented.

Evolutionary parasite control

The most scientifically supported hypothesis is evolutionary. Studies involving wild felids and other carnivores indicate that ingesting indigestible plant fiber increases muscular contractility in the intestine, helping to expel helminth parasites before they can attach or reproduce in the digestive tract. Modern domestic cats — without worms, living in apartments — retain this instinct encoded in their DNA, and cat grass is simply the form they have found to access it.

Natural emetic

Cat grass mildly irritates the stomach lining, facilitating the expulsion of substances the body cannot digest: hairballs formed during grooming, feather fragments, or small bone pieces in cats that hunt. Although vomiting is frequently associated with this behavior, studies show it occurs in only 25 to 30 percent of grass-eating episodes — suggesting that inducing regurgitation is not the primary goal.

Folic acid supplementation

The juice present in young sprouts is rich in folic acid (vitamin B9), essential for hemoglobin production and oxygen transport in the blood. The hypothesis was originally proposed by ethologist Desmond Morris and remains one of the accepted explanations, although most complete commercial cat foods already contain adequate levels of this vitamin.

Environmental enrichment and sensory stimulation

For cats living exclusively indoors, chewing grass represents a form of sensory exploration: a texture different from their usual food, a fresh vegetal aroma, resistance when biting. Environmental enrichment studies consistently show that cats actively seek novel stimuli — and cat grass offers exactly that in a safe and accessible way.

The main species and their characteristics

Wheat — Triticum aestivum

The most popular species in the market. It germinates quickly, between 5 and 7 days, forming dense tufts with thin blades and a mild flavor that most cats accept readily. Rich in B-complex vitamins and vitamin E in young sprouts. It is the safest choice for those introducing cat grass for the first time.

Barley — Hordeum vulgare

Produces slightly wider and softer blades than wheat. The mineral profile is somewhat superior, with the presence of selenium and chromium. Germination is slower, but the result is a robust plant with good palatability. Some studies suggest a more pronounced effect on intestinal microbiota due to the predominant fiber type.

Oats — Avena sativa

A delicate sprout with a shorter life cycle before the blades begin to collapse. Rich in beta-glucans, a type of soluble fiber potentially beneficial for cats with a tendency toward constipation. Requires attention to substrate moisture, as it is more susceptible to fungal overgrowth in hot and humid climates.

Rye — Secale cereale

Less common in the market but technically viable. A more intense flavor than wheat may lead some cats to refuse it. Its advantage is greater resistance to mold in humid environments, a relevant characteristic for owners in cities with high year-round humidity.

Rice — Oryza sativa

A rarity in the cat grass market. Germination is more demanding and requires precise moisture control. Naturally gluten-free — a growing marketing differentiator, though of questionable relevance to feline physiology. Cats tend to show less interest in rice sprouts than in winter cereals.

Corn — Zea mays

Young corn sprouts are consumable, but the plant grows quickly and the blades harden within days, reducing palatability. Cat interest is generally lower compared to winter grasses. Rarely used in commercial products specifically designed for feline consumption.

Essential care guidelines

Offering cat grass safely requires attention to a few key points. The substrate must never contain chemical fertilizers, synthetic nutrients, or pesticides — opt for simple potting soil or certified organic substrates. The plant should be replaced every 7 to 10 days to prevent mold growth, especially in humid climates. Blades above 15 cm tend to yellow and lose palatability. Finally, never substitute cat grass with ornamental houseplants — lily, pothos, and ZZ plant are among the most toxic species for cats and represent a genuine risk of serious poisoning.

The cat that shows no interest in grass

Not every cat shows interest in cat grass — and this is entirely normal. The behavior is individual and does not indicate nutritional deficiency when absent. If the animal consistently ignores the grass, there is no need to insist. What matters is that the option is safely available for those who wish to access it.
