Devon Rex: the genetic pixie born in a tin mine

On July 15, 1959, on a rural property in Buckfastleigh, Devon, England, a single kitten was born unlike all the others in his litter. While his siblings had straight coats, this male was covered in soft waves, with enormous ears and a gaze that seemed to belong to another world. Beryl Cox adopted him and named him Kirlee. She had no idea, in that moment, that she was looking at the founding cat of an entire breed — and one of the most fascinating stories in feline genetics.

A tin mine and a mysterious father

Kirlee's father was never identified. The most widely accepted theory is that he was a large, black tomcat with ringlets hanging from his tail, who lived in the ruins of an abandoned tin mine nearby. Kirlee's mother was an unregistered domestic tortoiseshell and white female. Later genetic reconstruction suggests she was herself a daughter of that feral tom, and that the inbreeding between mother and son — both heterozygous for the mutation — was what allowed the recessive allele to manifest visually in Kirlee.

Beryl Cox, intrigued by the kitten's rarity, learned of a curly-coated breed being developed in the neighboring county of Cornwall — the Cornish Rex, which had appeared in 1950. The natural assumption was that the two cats shared the same gene. Kirlee was entrusted to breeder and judge Brian Stirling-Webb for experimental crossings. The results were puzzling: all the kittens born had straight coats. Further matings in 1961 with a purebred Cornish Rex female confirmed what breeders suspected: Kirlee carried an entirely different mutation. The two rex types were then developed independently — the Cornish Rex as Gene 1, and the Devon Rex as Gene 2.

The genetics behind the curl: the KRT71 gene

For decades, the molecular basis of the Devon Rex's wavy coat remained a mystery. The answer came in 2010, in a study published in Mammalian Genome by the laboratory of Dr. Leslie A. Lyons (UC Davis / University of Missouri): the responsible mutation is a complex insertion-deletion alteration in the KRT71 gene, which encodes keratin 71, a protein essential for hair follicle structure (Gandolfi et al., 2010; PMC2974189).

A normal cat's coat consists of three fiber types: guard hairs (long and straight, the outer protective layer), intermediate awn hairs, and the fine, undulating down hairs. In the Devon Rex, the KRT71re mutation is autosomal recessive and eliminates virtually all guard hairs, leaving only an extremely soft, short, and wavy down layer. For the phenotype to manifest, the cat must inherit two copies of the mutant allele — one from each parent.

The Gandolfi et al. study also revealed a fascinating allelic hierarchy at the same locus: KRT71+ > KRT71hr > KRT71re. The wild-type allele (+) produces a normal coat. The hr allele, present in the Sphynx, is responsible for near-hairlessness and is dominant over the Devon allele (re). This means a cat carrying one copy of each — as some Sphynx do, silently carrying the Devon gene — will not breed true for the hairless trait if crossed with a Devon carrier. The Devon mutation is entirely independent from the Cornish Rex mutation, as the historical 1961 crossings proved and molecular biology confirmed.

Morphology: the pixie who arrived from another planet

The Devon Rex is frequently described as a "pixie" or "elfin cat," and the comparison is no exaggeration. The head is short and broad, with prominent cheekbones, a slightly curved forehead, and a strong chin. The eyes are large, oval-shaped, and highly expressive — they can be any color, including unusual combinations. The ears are the most iconic feature: enormous, wide-based, set low on the head with rounded tips and often adorned with tufts of fur.

The body is medium-sized, with surprisingly robust musculature for such a light cat — slender legs with large paws and long toes that allow the Devon Rex to use its paws with exceptional manual dexterity. The tail is thin and tapering. The CFA standard distributes points between head (35), body (30), and coat (30), with 5 points for color — reflecting that both the unique head morphology and the wavy coat are equally important breed markers.

The coat is the most sensory characteristic: incredibly soft to the touch, like velvet or silk, with waves covering the entire body in some individuals and extending down the legs and tail. Texture varies individually — kittens from the same litter may differ in density and degree of curl — but a quality adult should present complete and uniform coverage. The Devon Rex accepts all colors and patterns genetically possible in the species: solid, shaded, smoke, tabby, bi-color, and pointed.

Temperament: the monkey in a cat suit

Breeders and owners consistently use the same terms to describe the Devon Rex: mischievous, playful, highly intelligent, and completely people-oriented. The breed has been nicknamed "a monkey in a cat suit" — a reference both to its acrobatic agility and to its curious, hand-manipulating behavior.

Devons are not ornamental cats. They follow their owners from room to room, investigate everything, learn tricks easily, open latches, and insert themselves into any container that sparks curiosity. Kirlee himself impressed Beryl Cox with his ability to walk a tightrope and retrieve toys — behaviors that persist in the breed today. They are extremely sociable cats, adapting well to families with older children and other animals, but they suffer from prolonged solitude.

The Devon Rex is an "eternal kitten" — its energy and mischief do not diminish with age the way they do in other breeds. This is enchanting for active owners, but should be considered by those seeking a calmer companion.

Health: the legacy of a single founding cat

The fact that the entire breed descends from a single cat, Kirlee, created a genetic bottleneck with health implications. The conditions most closely monitored by responsible breeders are:

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM): thickening of the heart muscle that can lead to heart failure. It is the leading cause of death in adult cats across all breeds, but a 2021 study identified elevated prevalence in Devon Rex (Longeri et al., 2021). Periodic echocardiographic screening is recommended for all breeding cats.

Patellar Luxation: knee joint instability, classified from grades 1 to 4. It can be asymptomatic or require surgical intervention in severe cases.

Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD): cyst formation in the kidneys, with an identifiable genetic basis through DNA testing.

Hip Dysplasia: malformation of the hip joint, more common in compact-bodied breeds.

Both CFA and TICA permit Devon Rex outcrossing with American Shorthair and British Shorthair to broaden the gene pool. First-generation kittens are born with straight coats but carry the Devon allele and are registered as Devon Rex AOV (Any Other Variety). Ethical breeders perform echocardiograms on breeding cats, blood typing (Devon Rex can carry any of the three groups — A, B, and AB), and available genetic testing before any crossing.

The curly coat and the allergy question

The Devon Rex is frequently cited as "hypoallergenic" — a claim that requires careful qualification. The primary feline allergen is Fel d 1, produced by the sebaceous and salivary glands and transferred to the coat during grooming. Since the Devon Rex produces less coat and sheds less (the absence of guard hairs drastically reduces allergen dispersal into the environment), many allergy sufferers report better tolerance with this breed. However, Fel d 1 production itself is not reduced — so there is no guarantee of tolerance, and people with severe allergies should make test visits before adopting.

Practical care

The coat requires dedicated attention. The fine, wavy fur breaks easily with aggressive brushing; the recommendation is to use a soft cloth or bare hands to remove loose hair. The large ears accumulate wax and dust more easily than average breeds and should be inspected weekly. The skin — more exposed due to the absence of guard hairs — can retain sweat and sebum, making occasional baths beneficial. The Devon Rex also tolerates cold poorly for the same reason: the lack of a guard hair protective layer reduces thermal insulation. In colder environments, light sweaters or warm spots are functional accessories, not merely aesthetic ones.

One starting point, 65 years later

Kirlee died in 1970, but his spontaneous mutation — encoded in a recessive allele in the KRT71 gene — lives on in every Devon Rex in the world. From the tin mines of Buckfastleigh to show halls and homes on every continent, this breed is proof that nature occasionally produces works of art that no human breeder could have imagined. A pixie with science woven into its coat.
