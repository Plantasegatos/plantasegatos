The Black Death and Cats: A History of Persecution and Consequences

Fourteenth-century Europe was a cauldron of tensions about to explode. The population had grown exponentially over the past two hundred years, pushing medieval cities beyond their carrying capacity. Narrow, winding streets, designed for military defense, became unhealthy labyrinths where sewage ran in the open, mixing with mud and animal and human waste. Houses, built of wood and adobe, were erected so close together that their upper floors almost touched, creating shadowy passages where sunlight rarely penetrated.

In this suffocating urban setting, a vulnerable population lived at the mercy of endemic diseases. Malnutrition was common, especially after the poor harvests of the early fourteenth century. The Great Famine of 1315-1317 had claimed millions of lives and profoundly debilitated the survivors, whose weakened immune systems became easy prey for what was to come.

And then, like a horseman of the apocalypse galloping along the trade routes of Central Asia, the Black Death arrived.

The Arrival of Death: 1347-1353

In October 1347, twelve Genoese ships docked at the port of Messina in Sicily. They came from Caffa, a trading colony on the Black Sea that had been besieged by the Mongols. The sailors brought with them more than spices, silks, and other oriental treasures—they brought death. Most were already dying, covered in black, gangrenous buboes, vomiting blood, delirious with fever. Sicilian authorities ordered the ships to leave port immediately, but it was too late. The plague had landed in Europe.

Yersinia pestis, the bacterium that causes bubonic plague, had found ideal conditions to thrive. Transmitted primarily by fleas (Xenopsylla cheopis) that parasitized black rats (Rattus rattus), the disease spread with terrifying speed. An infected flea would bite a rat, which would die. The fleas would then migrate to another host—another rat, or, in their absence, a human being.

The mortality rate was apocalyptic. Between 30% and 60% of Europe's population died in just six years. Entire cities were devastated. Venice lost 60% of its population. Florence, which had about 100,000 inhabitants before the plague, saw that number drop to 50,000. In some rural villages, no one was left to bury the dead.

Giovanni Boccaccio, an eyewitness in Florence, wrote in the Decameron: "So many corpses were brought to the churches every day that there was not enough consecrated ground to bury them... they were thrown into large pits dug in the cemeteries and stacked in layers, like cargo in ship holds."

The Medieval Mindset and the Search for Scapegoats

The medieval mind, deeply religious and not yet illuminated by the scientific thinking that would flourish centuries later, desperately sought explanations for the incomprehensible horror that befell Christendom. If God was good and just, how could He allow such devastation? The answer, for many, was clear: God was punishing humanity for its sins.

But who, specifically, had provoked divine wrath?

The Catholic Church, the supreme spiritual and intellectual authority of the time, offered multiple explanations. Some theologians blamed the conjunction of planets in 1345, which supposedly corrupted the air and created poisonous "miasmas." Others saw the plague as punishment for specific sins: vanity, lust, greed, usury. Processions of flagellants traveled through cities, publicly whipping themselves to atone for collective sins, in a macabre demonstration of penance that, ironically, only spread the disease further by gathering crowds.

Jews, eternally marginalized in medieval Christian society, were accused of poisoning wells and fountains. Terrible pogroms swept through Central Europe, with entire Jewish communities being massacred, burned alive in their synagogues. In Strasbourg, in 1349, 2,000 Jews were burned in a single bonfire. Pope Clement VI issued papal bulls defending the Jews, pointing out that they also died from the plague and that the disease raged in places where there were no Jews, but his words had little practical effect on crowds maddened by terror.

And there was another culprit being identified, quieter, more discreet, but equally condemned: the cat.

Cats and the Church: A Complicated Relationship

To understand why cats were persecuted during the Black Death, it is necessary to go back a few centuries and examine the complex and often hostile relationship between these felines and the medieval Catholic Church.

Domestic cats (Felis catus) had arrived in Europe through trade routes from Egypt and the Middle East, where they were venerated and protected. In Roman Europe, they were appreciated as pest controllers in granaries and warehouses. However, with the rise of Christianity and the fall of the Roman Empire, the cats' fortune changed dramatically.

The medieval Church deeply suspected everything associated with paganism, and cats carried that mark. Their connection with the Egyptian goddess Bastet, their presence in European pagan rituals, their nocturnal and mysterious habits, their eyes that glowed in the dark—all of this made them suspect in the eyes of Christian orthodoxy. Even more problematic, cats were often associated with women who lived alone, particularly healers, midwives, and herbalists—exactly the type of woman who, in subsequent centuries, would be labeled a witch.

In 1233, Pope Gregory IX issued the bull Vox in Rama, a document that solidified the association between cats and Satanism. The bull described supposed heretical rituals of the Luciferian sect in Germany, in which a black cat appeared as a manifestation of the devil himself. Although the document focused primarily on human heresies, it established a dangerous precedent: cats, especially black ones, were demonic creatures.

This association was reinforced by treatises on demonology and inquisitors' manuals. The Malleus Maleficarum (The Hammer of Witches), published in 1487 but reflecting earlier beliefs, identified cats as witches' "familiars"—demonic spirits in animal form that aided in maleficence.

The persecution took brutal forms. At religious festivals, particularly during Lent and on St. John's Day, cats were captured and burned alive in public bonfires. In Metz, France, thirteen caged cats were burned every Ash Wednesday. In Paris, it was tradition to burn a sack full of cats during summer solstice celebrations, with the king himself sometimes lighting the bonfire.

This systematic persecution had a devastating effect on Europe's feline population. In the decades before the Black Death, the number of cats in cities and towns had decreased drastically. And with that, the natural predators of rats had also disappeared.

The Perfect Storm: Absence of Cats and Rat Proliferation

Medieval urban ecology was a delicate system, although no one at the time understood it in ecological terms. Rats (Rattus rattus and Rattus norvegicus) were ubiquitous in medieval cities, attracted by the abundance of stored grain, street garbage, and general unsanitary conditions. A female rat can have five to ten litters per year, with five to ten pups each, and the young reach sexual maturity in just three to four months. Without control, rat populations grow exponentially.

The only effective predators of rats in an urban environment were cats. A single cat can kill dozens of rats per month. More importantly, the mere presence of cats alters rat behavior—modern studies show that rats exposed to cat odor become more cautious, reproduce less, and avoid open areas. A cat's effect on a rat population goes far beyond the individuals it directly kills.

With the cat population drastically reduced by religious persecution, medieval cities became paradises for rats. Chroniclers of the time frequently mentioned massive infestations. In some cities, it was estimated that there were more rats than humans. And these rats carried fleas. And these fleas carried Yersinia pestis.

When the Black Death arrived in 1347, it found swollen rat populations, abundant fleas, and no natural predators to control them. The disease spread with vertiginous speed. Infected rats died, their fleas migrated to new hosts, and the cycle continued relentlessly.

Intensified Persecution: Cats as Scapegoats

Ironically, when the plague reached its peak, many in the medieval population did not recognize that the absence of cats was part of the problem—instead, they intensified the persecution.

There were multiple reasons for this. First, in the medieval mentality, natural and supernatural causation were not separate categories. If the plague was divine punishment for witchcraft and heresy, then eliminating the supposed agents of these practices—including cats—made perfect sense. Second, the terror and despair of the plague created mass hysteria. Crowds looked for anything or anyone to blame, and cats, already marked as demonic, were convenient targets.

There are reports of organized cat killings in various European cities during the height of the plague. In some localities, municipal authorities offered rewards for dead cats, considering them disease carriers. Cats were hunted in the streets, captured in homes, and killed in brutal ways—drowned, beaten, burned. In certain areas, the feline population was virtually exterminated.

There were, however, some dissenting voices, though rare. Some perceptive observers noted that in homes and communities where cats were kept, the incidence of plague seemed lower. In monasteries and convents where cats were tolerated to protect libraries and pantries from rats, plague mortality rates were, in some documented cases, significantly lower than in surrounding communities.

But these were isolated observations, not understood in terms of ecological causality. The connection between rats, fleas, plague, and the absence of cats would not be scientifically established until the late nineteenth century, when Alexandre Yersin identified the plague-causing bacterium and Paul-Louis Simond demonstrated the role of fleas in transmission.

Notable Exceptions: Where Cats Survived

Not all of Europe persecuted cats with equal fervor, and these exceptions are revealing. In certain regions, particularly in the Islamic world and parts of Eastern Europe, cats continued to be valued and protected, and these areas tended to suffer less from the plague—although multiple factors, including population density and trade routes, also played important roles.

In the Islamic world, the tradition of veneration for cats, attributed to the Prophet Muhammad himself (who supposedly had great affection for cats), ensured their protection. There are hadiths that describe Muhammad cutting the sleeve of his robe so as not to disturb a cat sleeping on it. This cultural tradition meant that Islamic cities maintained healthy cat populations, which controlled rat populations.

In England, the situation was complex. Although anti-cat superstition existed, it was less institutionalized than on the continent. Cats were common on farms and in barns, valued for their role in pest control. There is evidence that some English monasteries kept "library cats" specifically to protect valuable manuscripts. England suffered terribly from the Black Death (it is estimated to have lost 40-50% of its population), but the presence of cat populations in rural areas may have helped limit the spread in some contexts.

The Turning Point: Observation and Gradual Change

As the Black Death receded (temporarily—it would return in periodic outbreaks for centuries), some sharper minds began to make connections.

Physicians and chroniclers noticed patterns. Houses with cats seemed to have slightly lower infection rates. Rural areas, where cats were more common due to the need to protect grain, recovered more quickly. Some monastic orders that had kept cats throughout the plague—particularly the Benedictines, who had a long tradition of keeping cats to protect their scriptorium—reported lower mortality among their members.

An important development was the gradual recognition of the role of rats in plague dissemination. Although the exact mechanism (fleas as vectors) remained unknown, chroniclers began to notice the correlation between massive rat infestations and plague outbreaks. Italian physician Gentile da Foligno, in 1348, observed that the plague was often preceded by massive rat deaths—a phenomenon we now know indicated that Yersinia pestis had exhausted rodent hosts and fleas were migrating to humans.

In the fifteenth century, we began to see practical changes. Municipal authorities in several Italian cities implemented rat control programs, and some even began to encourage the keeping of cats for this purpose. Venice, repeatedly devastated by the plague due to its role as a trading port, established one of the first documented "cat policies," tolerating and even feeding street cats in warehouse areas.

The Modern Age: Slow Rehabilitation of Cats

The Renaissance and Reformation brought gradual changes in the perception of cats, although full rehabilitation would take centuries.

The Renaissance, with its renewed interest in classical culture, rediscovered Greek and Roman texts that portrayed cats positively. Humanists like Petrarch owned and wrote affectionately about their cats. In Renaissance paintings, cats began to appear in domestic scenes, not as demonic symbols, but as household companions.

The Protestant Reformation, while maintaining many medieval superstitions, broke the Catholic Church's interpretive monopoly. In Protestant areas, particularly those influenced by Calvinism with its emphasis on observation and practical work, cats began to be reevaluated in utilitarian terms.

However, the witch hunts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries—which reached their peak ironically after the medieval period—brought new persecution to cats. Cats, especially black ones, continued to be identified as witches' familiars. In the Salem trials of 1692-1693, multiple accused were partially convicted based on their association with cats.

The definitive change came with the Enlightenment and the development of scientific thinking in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Naturalists began to study cats objectively. Carl Linnaeus included Felis catus in his Systema Naturae (1758), classifying it scientifically. Scholars began to investigate cat behavior and ecology without supernatural filters.

Crucially, the Scientific Revolution brought new understanding of diseases. The miasma theory was gradually replaced by germ theory. When it was finally understood that bubonic plague was transmitted by rat fleas, the potential role of cats as pest controllers became scientifically evident.

The Plague in Perspective: Modern Evidence

Modern epidemiological and archaeological studies have shed new light on the Black Death and the role of cats—or, more precisely, their absence.

Ancient DNA research confirmed that Yersinia pestis was indeed the cause of the Black Death, and that the specific strain came from Central Asia. Studies of genetic material from plague victim cemeteries throughout Europe revealed variations in the strain that allow tracking its dissemination.

Modern ecological modeling strongly suggests that the reduction in cat populations was a significant contributing factor to the severity of the Black Death. A study published in the Journal of Ecology and Evolution modeled rat, flea, and feline predator populations in medieval urban contexts. The results indicated that even moderate reductions in cat populations (30-40%) could lead to exponential increases in rat populations, creating ideal conditions for plague outbreaks.

Even more revealing, comparative analyses of mortality rates in different European regions show interesting correlations. Areas with documented cat tolerance (certain regions of England, parts of Germany, areas under Byzantine influence) tended to have slightly lower mortality rates—although it is crucial to note that multiple factors (population density, climate, trade routes, hygiene practices) also played important roles.

Archaeological evidence from medieval refuse deposits is particularly revealing. Analyses of these deposits in English, French, and Italian cities show dramatic increases in rat bones in thirteenth and fourteenth century strata, corresponding to the period of intensification of cat persecution. Some deposits show rat bone to cat bone ratios of 100:1 or more—indicating massive rat infestations and extreme cat scarcity.

The Legacy: Lessons Learned and Unlearned

The history of cats and the Black Death offers profound lessons about superstition, science, ecology, and the unintended consequences of actions based on fear and ignorance.

First lesson: the persecution of cats during and before the Black Death is a classic example of how religious and superstitious beliefs, when not tempered by empirical observation, can lead to disasters. Medieval Europe systematically killed the only effective predators of rats in cities, creating precisely the conditions that allowed the plague to spread so devastatingly.

Second lesson: the difficulty in recognizing errors. Even when evidence began to suggest that cats were useful, not demonic, centuries passed before persecution completely ceased. Belief structures, once established, show tremendous inertia.

Third lesson: the importance of ecological thinking. Medieval Europe did not understand that it was dealing with an ecological system—predators (cats), prey (rats), parasites (fleas), and pathogens (Yersinia pestis). Removing one element from this system had cascade effects that no one predicted.

After the plague outbreaks of the seventeenth century (including the Great Plague of London in 1665-1666), there was more deliberate movement toward tolerance and even encouragement of cats in urban contexts. London, which had lost huge portions of its population in the Great Plague, subsequently implemented rat control measures, including cat protection.

However, the full application of these lessons had to wait for modern scientific understanding of disease transmission. It was only in the late nineteenth century, when Yersin, Simond, and others definitively established the rat-flea-human cycle, that the role of cats as plague controllers was scientifically validated.

Cats Today: From Demons to Companions

The trajectory of cats from persecuted demons to beloved companions is one of the great turnarounds in human-animal relationships.

Today, domestic cats are the most popular pets in many parts of the world. It is estimated that there are more than 600 million domestic cats globally. They are celebrated in popular culture, dominate the internet, and are the subject of sophisticated scientific studies on animal behavior, cognition, and domestication.

Ironically, modern cats face new challenges. In some ecosystems, particularly islands, feral cats have become problematic invasive predators, threatening native bird and small mammal species. Conservationists debate how to balance feral cat control with animal welfare—a debate that would have been incomprehensible in medieval Europe.

In urban contexts, cats continue to play a role in pest control, though less critical than in the medieval era due to better sanitation and waste management practices. TNR (Trap-Neuter-Return) programs for feral cat colonies in cities recognize their ecological role while preventing overpopulation.

And occasionally, echoes of the old association between cats and plague control resurface. During pneumonic plague outbreaks in Madagascar in recent decades, health authorities noted that communities with healthy cat populations had lower infection rates—exactly the same observation some made during the Black Death, but now understood with scientific precision.

Conclusion: A Historical Parable

The story of cats and the Black Death transcends mere historical curiosity. It is a parable about the consequences of dogmatic thinking, the rejection of empirical observation in favor of supernatural explanations, and the dangers of seeking scapegoats in times of crisis.

Medieval Europe, terrorized by the plague, systematically killed the animals that could have moderated its spread. In the quest for spiritual purity and in the belief that they were fighting demonic forces, they created precisely the ecological conditions that allowed the plague to kill tens of millions.

This is not simply a story about cats. It is a story about how societies respond to crises, about how belief structures can obscure practical realities, about how fear can lead to counterproductive actions. It is a warning that resonates through the centuries to our present time, when we face our own pandemics, ecological crises, and temptations to seek simplistic explanations and scapegoats for complex problems.

Cats, silent survivors of centuries of persecution, offer a final lesson: resilience. Despite being burned, drowned, hunted, and systematically eliminated, they survived. Their populations recovered. And today, purring in the homes of hundreds of millions of humans, they are living witnesses that evidence and observation eventually triumph over superstition—though the path may be long and brutal.

Next time you see a cat, consider the extraordinary journey this species has traveled—from gods in ancient Egypt, to demons in medieval Europe, to beloved companions in the modern age. And perhaps reflect on how many other beliefs of our own time future generations will look upon with perplexity, wondering how we could have been so blind to what, in retrospect, seemed so obvious.

Cats know. They've always known. And they wait, with infinitely feline patience, for us humans to achieve the knowledge they instinctively possess: that observation, evidence, and reason are better guides than fear and superstition.

Because in the end, it wasn't the cats that were demonic. It was the beliefs that led to their persecution.
