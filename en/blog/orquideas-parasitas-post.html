Are Orchids Parasites? Understanding How They Live on Trees

An orchid clinging to a tree trunk, with its roots spread across the bark, may lead to an apparently logical conclusion: if it lives on another plant, it must be taking something from it.

But that is not what is happening.

Many orchid species are epiphytes. They use other plants as physical support without penetrating the tree's vascular system to extract its sap. In other words, the tree provides a place to live — not a direct source of food.

Epiphyte does not mean parasite

A parasitic plant establishes a direct physiological connection with its host. Many parasitic plants possess specialized structures known as haustoria, which penetrate host tissues and provide access to water, minerals or organic compounds.

Epiphytic orchids follow a different strategy. Their roots adhere to the surface of the bark, allowing the plant to remain attached to trunks and branches. They do not need to extract sap from the tree to survive.

For this reason, epiphytism is commonly described as a form of commensalism: the epiphyte mainly gains physical support and access to a particular microenvironment, while the host tree usually experiences no significant harm.

This does not mean that epiphytes can never affect their host. Large accumulations may add weight, intercept water and locally modify conditions on a branch. That, however, is very different from parasitism.

So where do water and nutrients come from?

Living away from the soil creates an interesting challenge. Without roots buried in the ground, how can an orchid obtain water and minerals?

Epiphytic orchids exploit resources available in their immediate environment. Rain wets their roots and may carry small amounts of dissolved minerals. Water flowing across leaves, branches and bark can also transport nutrients. Dust, airborne particles, decomposing leaves and other organic debris may accumulate around the roots and contribute additional nutrients.

Microorganisms in these environments also participate in nutrient cycling. As with orchids more broadly, associations with mycorrhizal fungi are fundamental during different stages of the orchid life cycle, particularly during germination.

It is an economy based on small amounts: resources may be scarce and intermittent, but these plants have evolved remarkable ways to exploit them efficiently.

The secret is also in the roots

The aerial roots of many orchids possess a characteristic structure called the velamen. It is an outer layer composed, at maturity, of specialized dead cells.

When water becomes available, the velamen can take it up rapidly and participates in water and nutrient dynamics around the living tissues of the root. It also contributes to mechanical protection and helps the root function in an environment where periods of wetting may quickly be followed by periods of dryness.

This is one reason why the roots of many epiphytic orchids look so different from those of plants growing in ordinary soil.

They are adapted to take advantage of water quickly when it becomes available and endure its absence afterward.

Why live on a tree?

The epiphytic lifestyle allows orchids to occupy parts of the forest that would be inaccessible to plants restricted to the ground.

Depending on their position on the trunk or within the canopy, orchids may encounter different levels of light, airflow, temperature and humidity. They also avoid direct competition with terrestrial plants for space in the soil.

Life above the ground, however, is not necessarily easy. Water and nutrients may be less predictable, exposure to wind can increase, and roots must tolerate repeated cycles of hydration and drying.

This is why not every branch provides the same habitat.

A tree creates a microenvironment

Bark texture affects the ability of roots to attach. Branch orientation changes the amount of light received. Position within the canopy influences airflow, temperature, humidity and how quickly roots dry after rain.

Even two different areas of the same tree can provide remarkably different environments for an orchid.

This helps explain why particular orchid species may preferentially occur in certain positions or on certain trees even though they do not feed on those trees.

Can we grow orchids directly on trees?

Yes. For species and hybrids adapted to an epiphytic lifestyle, mounting an orchid on a tree can reproduce several characteristics of its natural environment.

The choice should nevertheless take the species or hybrid, local climate and available microenvironment into account. Different orchids have different requirements for light, humidity, temperature and airflow.

The tree should be healthy and have a surface that allows roots to establish themselves. During establishment, the orchid can be gently secured using suitable natural fibers or other appropriate materials without constricting its roots or rhizomes.

As the plant grows, new roots may naturally attach to the bark and the temporary ties can eventually become unnecessary.

One principle remains especially important: roots adapted to receiving water also need appropriate conditions in which to dry. Keeping an epiphytic orchid permanently waterlogged can encourage root deterioration and disease.

What if there are cats in the garden?

Cats and orchids can coexist quite comfortably. Several widely cultivated ornamental orchids, including representatives of Phalaenopsis, Cattleya, Dendrobium and Oncidium, are listed as non-toxic to cats by veterinary references such as the ASPCA.

That does not mean cats should be encouraged to eat them. Even non-toxic plant material may cause gastrointestinal upset when ingested.

Furthermore, the greater hazard may sometimes come not from the orchid itself but from products used around it. Fertilizers, pesticides, fungicides and other gardening chemicals should always be kept away from cats.

It is not stealing from the tree

What may initially look like dependence is actually a remarkable ecological strategy.

The orchid has evolved a way to occupy trunks and branches, exploit brief opportunities for water and nutrients, and survive without extracting sap from the plant physically supporting it.

The tree provides bark, height and a microenvironment.

The orchid has found an address — not a pantry.
