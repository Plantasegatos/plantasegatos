Ship Cats: The Little Paws That Crossed History

When we think about History, we remember kings, emperors, generals, battles and great political decisions. But History was also shaped by much smaller characters — some of them with four paws, whiskers and an extraordinary talent for hunting.

And perhaps few images are as fascinating as that of a cat staring toward the horizon from the deck of an old sailing ship.

To understand why, we need to travel back a few years before September 7, 1822.

An entire court crosses the Atlantic

In late November 1807, as Napoleon's troops advanced into Portugal, the Portuguese Court departed Lisbon for Brazil. Queen Maria I, Prince Regent João, members of the Royal Family, officials, soldiers, servants and part of the kingdom's administrative apparatus were among those who crossed the Atlantic.

It was an extraordinary event: the center of a European monarchy was crossing the ocean to establish itself in its American colony.

The voyage was difficult.

The ships were overcrowded. Water and food had to be rationed. Hygiene was poor, and discomfort accompanied the passengers throughout the long Atlantic crossing.

After 54 days at sea, on January 22, 1808, João and part of the fleet reached Salvador, Bahia.

But there was another problem that accompanied countless long sea voyages of the period.

Rodents.

The passengers nobody wanted

Ships carried large quantities of stored food: grain, flour, biscuits and other provisions needed to sustain dozens or even hundreds of people for weeks or months.

For rats and mice, this could become a floating banquet.

Rodents did not merely consume and contaminate provisions. They could damage stored materials and equipment and contribute to sanitary problems in spaces that were already cramped and difficult to keep clean.

It was in this environment that one of the domestic cat's oldest talents became particularly valuable at sea.

Enter the ship cats

For centuries, cats accompanied sailors in many parts of the world.

There was an extremely practical reason.

Cats hunt rodents.

In an age without modern pest-control methods, having a small predator roaming decks and holds could be enormously useful in protecting provisions.

Maritime records and museum collections document cats living aboard ships and helping crews keep populations of rats and mice under control.

Over time, many also became mascots and companions to their crews.

Among ropes, sails, barrels and the endless motion of the ocean, there was room for silent paws.

But were there cats aboard the Portuguese Royal fleet?

Here we need to distinguish between a beautiful story and something that can be stated as documented fact.

We know that cats were frequent companions aboard ships and were valued for controlling rodents. We also know a great deal about the difficult conditions faced during the Portuguese Court's Atlantic crossing.

However, we have not found sufficiently strong historical documentation to state that particular cats were aboard the vessels that carried João to Brazil, much less to identify individual animals.

It is therefore more accurate to place those little hunters within the broader maritime culture of their time rather than turn them, without evidence, into specific characters in that voyage.

And somehow, that does not make their story less beautiful.

Perhaps it makes it even more so.

Small crew members on great voyages

For hundreds of years, thousands of cats crossed the seas without ever finding their way into history books.

They did not command ships. They did not draw maps. They did not sign treaties.

But they walked across wet decks, slept among ropes and barrels, endured storms and did what cats have done alongside humans for thousands of years: occupied that remarkable space somewhere between usefulness and companionship.

While sailors watched the stars to find their way, many cats were probably watching something else — listening for the slightest movement in some dark corner of the hold.

Small workers in an enormous world.

From 1808 to 1822

The arrival of the Portuguese Court profoundly transformed Brazil. The opening of the ports, the creation of new administrative, cultural and economic institutions, and a major political reorganization permanently altered the relationship between Portugal and its American colony.

Fourteen years later, on September 7, 1822, Brazil declared its independence.

These were different events, separated by time and immense political complexity. Yet they belong to the same sequence of transformations that changed the country's destiny.

And that is why, on this September 7, we wanted to look at History from a slightly different angle.

Not only at those whose names appear in books.

But also at those who crossed oceans anonymously.

Because great journeys are not always made only by great characters.

Sometimes they also leave tiny pawprints across the deck.

To the cats that crossed oceans alongside humans, our respect.

Happy Brazilian Independence Day.
