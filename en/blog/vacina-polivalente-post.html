Cat Limping After a Multivalent Vaccine: Understanding Post-Vaccination Polyarthritis

Your cat received a multivalent vaccine, returned home apparently well and later began to limp. Perhaps they became quieter, appeared painful when walking or developed a fever. Understandably, this can be concerning — and veterinary literature does describe an uncommon reaction capable of producing this combination of signs.

It is generally referred to as calicivirus-associated limping syndrome and, when occurring in the post-vaccination context, may also be described as febrile limping syndrome or post-vaccination polyarthritis.

The phenomenon deserves recognition, but also perspective: it is uncommon, usually transient, and a cat limping after vaccination does not automatically mean that the vaccine caused the lameness.

What does calicivirus have to do with the joints?

Feline calicivirus (FCV) is best known for its role in feline respiratory disease and oral ulceration. However, FCV also has a well-documented association with a syndrome characterised by acute lameness and fever.

Affected cats can develop joint inflammation, pain and difficulty bearing weight on one or more limbs. The lameness may even shift rapidly from one limb to another.

This phenomenon has been observed both during some natural FCV infections and, rarely, following immunisation with certain modified-live FCV vaccines.

Can vaccination cause lameness?

Yes, this has been documented in veterinary literature. The statement, however, requires context.

Feline vaccines are available in different formulations and technologies. Some FCV vaccines contain modified-live virus, while others use inactivated virus. It would therefore be inaccurate to state that every feline multivalent vaccine contains live attenuated calicivirus.

Limping syndrome has been reported following certain modified-live FCV vaccines. However, investigations have also identified cats that developed lameness after vaccination in which the detected virus was a circulating field strain rather than the vaccine strain. In such cases, vaccination and clinical disease occurred close together in time without necessarily having a causal relationship.

Why can pain and fever occur?

The exact mechanism behind the syndrome has not yet been completely established.

Affected joints may develop synovitis, an inflammation of the membrane lining the joint. Thickening of the synovial membrane and increased synovial fluid have been described.

One proposed mechanism involves the immune response itself. Immune complexes — structures formed through interactions between antigens and antibodies — have been suggested as possible contributors to joint inflammation.

It is important to distinguish hypothesis from certainty: immune-complex involvement is a proposed mechanism and should not be presented as the proven explanation for every case.

What signs can occur?

The most noticeable sign is lameness. A cat may avoid placing weight on one paw, walk abnormally or appear uncomfortable while moving.

Other possible signs include:

  • pain while walking;
  • involvement of one or multiple joints;
  • lameness shifting between limbs;
  • fever;
  • lethargy;
  • reduced activity.

The combination of fever and lameness explains why the condition is also referred to as “febrile limping syndrome.”

How long does it last?

The classic FCV-associated presentation generally has a favourable outcome. Veterinary reviews report that complete recovery frequently occurs within 24–48 hours, without permanent joint damage.

That does not mean owners should simply wait for the problem to disappear. A cat experiencing pain, fever or difficulty walking should be assessed by a veterinarian.

When appropriate, supportive care and medications may be prescribed to control pain and inflammation. Never give a cat human painkillers or anti-inflammatory medication without veterinary guidance.

Limping after vaccination does not automatically mean polyarthritis

This is perhaps the most important point.

When one event follows another, it is natural to assume that the first caused the second. In medicine, however, temporal association alone does not establish causation.

A cat that begins limping after vaccination may instead have injection-site discomfort, trauma, orthopaedic disease, infection or another condition capable of causing lameness.

Vaccination history is therefore valuable information for the veterinarian, but it must be interpreted together with the clinical examination and, when indicated, additional diagnostic tests.

How is it investigated?

Assessment begins with a detailed clinical history and physical examination: when the vaccine was administered, which product was used, when the signs began, which limbs appear painful and whether fever or other abnormalities are present.

Depending on the clinical presentation, laboratory tests or diagnostic imaging may be recommended primarily to investigate other causes of lameness and inflammation.

There is therefore no rule that every cat limping after vaccination should automatically be diagnosed with post-vaccination polyarthritis.

Is this a reason to stop vaccinating?

No.

FCV is widespread among cats and can cause respiratory disease, oral ulceration, pneumonia in some patients and other clinical manifestations. Vaccination against calicivirus remains part of essential feline vaccination programmes.

Vaccines are biologically active interventions, and adverse events can occur. The appropriate response is neither to ignore adverse reactions nor to turn an uncommon event into a reason to abandon vaccination.

If a cat develops a suspected reaction following vaccination, the episode should be discussed and documented with the veterinarian. Before subsequent doses, the veterinarian can consider the cat's history, the vaccine product previously administered, the patient's individual risk and the most appropriate vaccination strategy.

Observation is part of responsible vaccination

Understanding post-vaccination polyarthritis should not create fear of vaccination. It means recognising that, although uncommon, a lameness syndrome associated with the calicivirus component has been scientifically documented and deserves appropriate attention.

If your cat develops lameness, fever, marked lethargy or significant pain following a multivalent vaccine, seek veterinary advice.

Vaccination protects. Careful observation matters too.
