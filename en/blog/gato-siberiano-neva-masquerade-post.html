Siberian Cat and Neva Masquerade: The Forest That Became a Living Room

A cat shaped by extreme cold

For at least a thousand years, Slavic folklore and rural records from Russia have mentioned a large, resilient, thick-coated cat inhabiting the dense forests of Siberia. It was not created by selective breeders, nor was it designed to serve any specific function: the Siberian Cat is a landrace — a natural breed shaped exclusively by environmental pressure, surviving winters that reach −40 °C through a combination of size, musculature, and coat that has no equivalent among domestic cats.

It is the national cat of Russia. And in many respects, it is also the domestic cat most closely connected to wild life without ever having stopped living alongside humans.

From Siberia to the world: a recent story

Despite its ancient origins as a landrace, the Siberian only began formal breeding in 1987, when the Soviet Felinological Association registered the breed — including both traditional colors and the colorpoint variant, the Neva Masquerade. On June 28, 1990, the first Siberians arrived in the United States, imported by Elizabeth Terrell: Ofelia Romanova, Naina Romanova, and Kaliostro Vasenjkovich — three names that carried the weight of a centuries-old lineage.

International recognition was gradual. The WCF was the first international organization to adopt the breed standard in 1991. TICA included the Siberian in 1992 and granted championship status in 1996. FIFe recognized the breed in 1997. The CFA accepted registration in 2000 and championship status in 2006. By 2023, the Siberian ranked 4th among the most popular breeds in FIFe, with 4,538 kittens registered that year — remarkable growth for a breed that was still in 15th place in 2017.

The physical standard: circles, not triangles

The Siberian is a medium to large cat, with males weighing between 4.5 kg and 9 kg — females are considerably smaller. But what immediately distinguishes the breed is the visual impression of roundness: the head is broad and slightly wedge-shaped, with soft contours; the eyes are large, almost round; the body has the shape of a barrel, with depth and width that convey compact strength. CFA breeders define this characteristic precisely: the Siberian is a breed of circles and roundness, not rectangles and triangles like the Maine Coon or the Norwegian Forest Cat.

The hind legs are slightly longer than the front legs, creating a slight arch in the back that is not a fault — it is structure. This conformation gives the Siberian considerable jumping power and a resting hunter's posture that recalls the wild feline. The tail is thick at the base, slightly shorter than the torso, and carried with pride. The paws are large and rounded, with tufts of fur between the toes — an adaptation for walking on snow.

The triple coat: natural climatic engineering

The Siberian's coat is its most extraordinary characteristic from a functional standpoint. It is composed of three distinct layers — guard hair (outer protective layer), awn hair (intermediate layer), and down hair (soft, dense undercoat) — forming a thermal insulation system that natural selection took centuries to perfect.

The guard hairs are water-resistant: the cuticle structure repels moisture efficiently, which allowed the cat to hunt in snow without saturating the undercoat. The undercoat, in turn, retains body heat even when the outer layer is damp. The result is a functional coat that most breeders compare to a natural waterproof protection.

The Siberian has two molting cycles per year: the heavy spring molt, when the winter coat is shed (a process triggered by increased photoperiod, not temperature), and a lighter molt at the end of summer in preparation for the winter coat. During molts, daily brushing is essential to prevent matting. Outside these periods, the Siberian's coat is surprisingly easy to maintain — it does not mat easily like Persian fur, for example, due to its naturally drier, less silky texture.

An important detail: the breed matures slowly. A young Siberian's coat can take up to three years to reach full development.

Colors and patterns: almost everything is permitted

The Siberian is one of the most open breeds in terms of color variation. Virtually all colors and patterns are accepted by major registries: solids, tabbies (mackerel and classic), spotted, ticked, bicolor, with any amount of white. The most common color is brown mackerel tabby with or without white.

A genetic peculiarity exclusive to the breed is the sunshine (golden) coloration, caused by the CORIN gene located at the wide band locus. The allele responsible for this specific golden color is found exclusively in Siberians — the other two identified alleles at this locus produce the golden coats in tigers and British Shorthairs, respectively. The silver-sunshine variant is called bimetallic or silver sunshine.

Eye color follows the variety of the coat: from golden to green in all shades. Clear, vivid colors are the most desirable in show standards. The exception is the colorpoint pattern — the Neva Masquerade — which requires exclusively blue eyes.

The Neva Masquerade: the mask of the Neva River

The Neva Masquerade is the colorpoint variant of the Siberian — and its name carries history in every syllable. Neva refers to the river that flows through St. Petersburg, where the variety is said to have originated; masquerade alludes to the masked balls held on the river's banks, and more directly, to the dark mask that the color points form around the cat's eyes and muzzle.

The origin of the colorpoint pattern in the Siberian is a topic of debate among breeders and organizations. The most accepted hypothesis is that colorpoint cats of Asian origin — descendants or relatives of breeds related to the Siamese — were naturally or intentionally crossed with Siberians in the urban areas of St. Petersburg between the 1960s and 1980s. The CFA, however, records that Siberian cats already displayed the colorpoint pattern before the formal registration of the breed began in 1987, and that the coloration is considered a legitimate part of the landrace's genetic heritage. There are reports of colorpoint kittens appearing spontaneously in traditional catteries with classic coat colors, even after 30 generations of crosses without external introduction.

From a genetic standpoint, the Neva Masquerade carries the same colorpoint mutation present in the Siamese: the c.904G>A variant of the TYR gene, which encodes the tyrosinase enzyme. This enzyme is temperature-sensitive — it functions efficiently in the cooler regions of the body (extremities: ears, muzzle, paws, and tail), where it deposits melanin normally, and is inhibited in the warmer regions of the trunk, resulting in light fur on the body and dark fur at the points. The same mechanism is present in the Birman, Himalayan (colorpoint Persian), Ragdoll, and Toybob.

The Neva colorpoint standard: colors, points, and eyes

The body of the Neva Masquerade is light — white, cream, or beige — with visibly darker points on the face, ears, paws, and tail. Points can be solid or in tabby pattern (called lynx point in North America). Accepted base colors include:

  • Seal point: warm cream to white body, dark brown to near-black points.
  • Blue point: cool white to light bluish-grey body, medium blue-grey points.
  • Red point: white to cream body, orange-red points.
  • Cream point: very light white to cream body, soft cream points.
  • Tortie point: points blended in two colors, exclusive to females.
  • Tabby (lynx) point: any base color with visible striping on the points.

Some registries do not permit the colors chocolate, lilac, cinnamon, fawn, caramel, and apricot in the colorpoint variety. The eyes of the Neva Masquerade are mandatorily blue — intensity can range from light blue to deep sapphire, with the most vivid shades being the most valued in shows. Solid white Siberian cats can have blue eyes, but this is due to the absence of melanin — a different mechanism from the colorpoint gene — and does not classify them as Neva Masquerade.

Structurally, the Neva Masquerade is identical to the traditional Siberian: same body standard, same water-resistant triple coat, same temperament, same slow maturation. The only differences are the coloration and the mandatory blue eyes. Organizations that register the Neva separately — such as FIFe, WCF, and ACF — do so for show classification purposes, not due to any difference in origin or physical standard.

The hypoallergenic question: what science actually says

The Siberian's reputation as a hypoallergenic cat is real — but it requires scientific context to be properly understood. The primary feline allergen is the protein Fel d 1, a secretoglobulin produced mainly in the sebaceous glands, anal glands, and saliva of cats. When grooming, cats deposit Fel d 1 on their fur, from which the protein disperses into the air and onto surfaces.

The genes Ch1 and Ch2 encode Fel d 1 production. Research published in scientific journals identified a considerable number of mutations in these genes in Siberian cats — some of them in exonic regions, which may directly affect the allergenic properties of the protein produced. Siberian Research Inc., a non-profit association, compiled data from more than 300 adult Siberians and found that approximately 50% of tested individuals produce Fel d 1 levels substantially lower than other breeds. About 15% produce very low levels — appropriate for homes with severely allergic individuals.

There are important nuances. Fel d 1 levels vary individually — two kittens from the same litter can have completely different profiles. Intact males produce more allergen; after neutering, levels equalize with those of females. Pregnant and nursing females show production peaks. Young kittens also produce more Fel d 1 than adults — families with severe allergies are advised not to adopt kittens younger than 12 weeks without first testing their reaction to that specific animal.

Coat length, contrary to what many believe, does not influence Fel d 1 levels. The protein is in the saliva and sebaceous glands, not in the fur itself. The fur acts only as a dispersal vehicle.

The conclusion from available scientific literature is clear: no cat is 100% hypoallergenic, but the Siberian has the highest documented proportion of individuals with reduced Fel d 1 production among all studied breeds.

Temperament: the dog that meows

Those who live with Siberians report a personality that combines canine sociability with the characteristic independence of felines. They are vocally expressive cats — with a repertoire of sounds including trills, chirps, and soft vocalizations — but not excessively noisy. They follow their owners around the house without being clingy, participate in household activities with genuine curiosity, and adapt well to families with children and other animals.

The relationship with water is another defining characteristic: many Siberians show an active fascination with faucets, containers with water, and even showers. This is not a quirk — it is a direct inheritance from an ancestry in which hunting in snowy environments and near waterways was part of daily life. The breed's water-resistant coat is not merely aesthetic; it is the same adaptation that allowed these cats to operate in conditions that would immobilize any other coat type.

Health and care

The Siberian is a robust breed, with an immune system strengthened by centuries of natural selection without human intervention. The most monitored hereditary condition is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), present in several feline breeds. Responsible breeders perform periodic echocardiograms on breeding cats to monitor the condition.

In terms of management, the coat requires daily brushing during molting periods (spring and late summer) and maintenance brushing two to three times per week at other times. Unlike Persian fur, the Siberian's coat does not mat easily outside of molts due to its texture and structure. Bathing is possible — many individuals tolerate it well when accustomed from kittenhood — but the extremely water-resistant coat requires thorough rinsing and careful drying.
