Kidney Disease in Cats: A Practical Guide for Guardians

By Plants & Cats — Your trusted guide to feline health and pet-safe living

Why this guide matters

If you have a cat — or are thinking about adopting one — you should know about kidney disease. It’s very common in felines, especially seniors, and it can develop silently. The good news: with the right info, you can prevent, spot it early, and care for your cat effectively.

The kidneys are your cat’s “filters.” They clean the blood, balance body water, and even produce key hormones. When they don’t work properly, it’s as if your cat’s “house” starts getting messy on the inside.

How your cat’s kidneys work

Cats are masters at conserving water — a legacy from desert-dwelling ancestors. Their kidneys are extremely efficient. The downside: when something goes wrong, cats can “mask” the issue for a long time, until it’s quite serious.

Think of the kidneys as two magic sponges filtering blood 24/7. They remove “bad stuff” (toxins) through urine and keep the “good stuff.” They also help control blood pressure and support red blood cell production.

Types of kidney problems

Acute problems (come on suddenly)

Kidneys “shut down” quickly, over days. Common causes include:

  • Poisoning: ingestion of toxic substances (e.g., lilies, antifreeze, certain meds)
  • Severe infection: one that has spread systemically
  • Severe dehydration: significant body water loss
  • Surgical complications: issues during anesthesia

Signs appear fast and are obvious: vomiting, diarrhea, lethargy, very little or no urination, even seizures. This is an emergency.

Chronic problems (worsen slowly)

Kidneys “wear out” over months or years. This is most common, especially in older cats. About half of cats over 15 have some degree of chronic kidney disease.

Causes include age, genetics (some cats are predisposed), high blood pressure, diabetes, repeated UTIs, and even untreated dental disease.

What is azotemia?

Azotemia is a fancy term meaning there’s too much “waste” in the blood. Normally, kidneys remove this waste (urea and creatinine) in urine. When they can’t, these compounds build up.

Three main types:

  • Pre-renal: circulation issue before the kidneys (e.g., dehydration)
  • Renal: actual kidney damage
  • Post-renal: obstruction preventing urine from exiting

Stages of chronic kidney disease

Vets classify CKD into four stages, often using a blood test called creatinine (plus other markers):

  • Stage 1 — Silent onset: cat seems normal, but early kidney changes exist.
  • Stage 2 — Early signs: may drink more and urinate more.
  • Stage 3 — Noticeable: lots of urine, increased thirst, weight loss, reduced appetite.
  • Stage 4 — Severe: weakness, frequent vomiting, strong uremic breath, apathy.

How to tell if your cat has kidney issues

Home signs to watch for:

Early clues (pay attention!):

  • Drinking and urinating more
  • Litter box gets soaked more quickly
  • Accidents outside the litter box

Progressing signs:

  • Weight loss despite normal eating
  • Less active or playful
  • Dull coat
  • Picky appetite or nausea
  • Seeking cool places to rest

More serious signs:

  • Frequent vomiting
  • Ammonia-like breath
  • Marked weakness
  • Mouth ulcers
  • In extreme cases, seizures

How vets confirm the diagnosis

  • Bloodwork: creatinine, urea (BUN), and others
  • Urinalysis: concentration, protein, infection
  • Ultrasound: kidney size and structure
  • Blood pressure: many CKD cats are hypertensive

Treatment: helping your cat feel better

Dietary changes

Renal-support diets: lower phosphorus, high-quality proteins, and more omega-3s.

Hydration is essential

  • Multiple bowls of fresh water
  • Water fountains
  • Wet food
  • Subcutaneous fluids in advanced cases

Medications can help

  • Blood pressure control
  • Anti-nausea meds
  • Supplements
  • Phosphate binders

Prevention: the best medicine

From kittenhood

  • Always encourage hydration
  • Quality diet
  • Maintain ideal weight

Home safety

  • Remove toxic plants (lilies, azaleas)
  • Store cleaning products safely
  • Beware of antifreeze

Regular checkups

Young adults: yearly | 7–10 years: every 6–8 months | Seniors: every 4–6 months

Dental health matters!

Oral infections can impact the kidneys.

Living with kidney disease: a good life is possible

  • More litter boxes
  • Easy access to water and food
  • Calm, low-stress environment

Red flags — see your vet urgently

  • No eating for > 24 hours
  • Frequent vomiting
  • Straining or unable to urinate
  • Marked lethargy or hiding
  • Breathing difficulty

Quality of life first

  • Follow the treatment plan
  • Observe daily
  • Keep follow-up appointments
  • Plenty of affection

What’s new & hopeful

  • New medications
  • Targeted diets
  • Stem-cell therapies
  • Earlier screening tools

Myths to ignore

  • “It’s normal that cats drink very little water.”
  • “Grocery-store food is the same as therapeutic diets.”
  • “If they’re eating, they’re fine.”
  • “Only old cats get kidney disease.”

Your role as a mindful guardian

You’re the best “detective” to spot changes in your cat. Early detection saves lives!

In closing

Caring for a cat with kidney disease can feel daunting, but a happy life is absolutely possible with veterinary guidance. Prevention is the best path.

⚠️ IMPORTANT: Always — always consult veterinarians. They’re the most reliable source to diagnose accurately, plan treatment, and follow up on our beloved cats!
