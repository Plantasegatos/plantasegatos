Feline Sporotrichosis: What Changed in Diagnosis and Treatment

If you've heard sporotrichosis described as "that gardener's fungus," it's time for an update. Over the past few years — especially in Brazil — this fungal disease has become one of the most pressing challenges in feline medicine. Understanding what's happening matters both for cat owners and for veterinary students and professionals.

What Is Feline Sporotrichosis?

Sporotrichosis is an infection caused by fungi of the Sporothrix complex. In cats, the agent responsible for the vast majority of cases in Brazil is Sporothrix brasiliensis — a species with significantly greater virulence and transmissibility than the others in the group.

The disease primarily manifests as skin wounds — especially on the face, ears, and tail — but can progress to severe forms with systemic involvement. And what concerns researchers most: it is zoonotic, meaning it can be transmitted from cats to humans.

Why Is Sporothrix brasiliensis Different?

S. brasiliensis has established itself as the most virulent pathogen within the complex, with documented expansion beyond Brazil into other Latin American countries. Transmission between cats and to humans occurs primarily through scratches and bites, or through contact with saliva and wound exudate — both containing viable yeasts well-adapted to feline salivary pH and body temperature.

This explains why free-roaming and stray cats are the primary reservoirs and spreaders of the disease in Brazilian urban centers.

How to Recognize It: Clinical Signs

The most characteristic sign is nodules and ulcers on the head — especially around the nose and muzzle, in a pattern informally called "clown nose" by veterinarians. But clinical awareness must go beyond the classic sign.

Extracutaneous signs such as frequent sneezing, nasal discharge, and breathing difficulty indicate systemic progression and are the main predictive factors for treatment failure and death. If your cat shows skin lesions combined with any respiratory symptom, seek veterinary care immediately and urgently.

How the Veterinarian Diagnoses It

Diagnosis follows two main pathways:

  • Cytology (imprint or FNA): a rapid test stained with panoptic or Giemsa, identifying characteristic yeasts (3–5 µm, with a clear halo) inside immune cells. In cats, sensitivity is high due to elevated fungal load.
  • Fungal culture: the gold standard, with superior sensitivity — around 95% versus just over 52% for cytology alone. Biopsy with histopathology may also be indicated, revealing the typical pyogranulomatous pattern of the disease.

In practice, cytology guides the immediate start of treatment, while culture confirms and directs management of more complex cases.

Treatment: What Science Says in 2026

Itraconazole remains the first-line medication, administered at 100 mg per cat or 10–15 mg/kg once daily. The average treatment duration is around 147 days — and this needs to be made very clear to the owner from the start.

A 2026 publication raised an important clinical alert: cats treated with compounded itraconazole showed a significantly higher mortality rate (76%) compared to those treated with the commercial reference product (45%), with a statistically relevant difference (p=0.036). The central hypothesis is erratic bioavailability of compounded formulations. Whenever possible, the commercial reference product is the safer choice.

When Treatment Doesn't Work?

In cases of resistance or slow response, the veterinarian may resort to combined strategies:

  • Itraconazole + potassium iodide: a combination frequently used in severe cases.
  • Ozonized sunflower oil: evaluated as a topical adjunct to itraconazole, with significant reduction in relapse rates, especially in FIV-positive cats (p=0.0027).
  • Cryosurgery: an excellent tool for localized residual lesions after primary treatment.

When to Stop Treatment? The Most Common Mistake

This is one of the most critical points — and where most relapses occur. Clinical cure (wound closure) is not mycological cure. The fungus can persist in tissues even after complete re-epithelialization.

The evidence-based recommendation is to maintain treatment for at least 30 to 60 days after complete clinical cure. Stopping earlier is the leading cause of relapse and cases that evolve toward resistance.

Sporotrichosis, Public Health, and the Owner's Role

Feline sporotrichosis is a compulsory notifiable disease in Brazil, with the regulatory framework strengthened from 2025 onward. This means the veterinarian plays a direct role in public health by diagnosing and treating these cases.

For cat owners, several measures are essential:

  • Keep the cat isolated from other household animals during the active phase of the disease.
  • Use gloves when handling lesions, applying medications, or cleaning the environment.
  • Do not consider euthanasia as a solution: cats with sporotrichosis have an excellent prognosis when treated correctly and for an adequate duration.
  • Seek medical advice if any family member develops unusual or slow-healing wounds after contact with the cat.
