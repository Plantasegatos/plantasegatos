Love Beyond Life: Cats, Loss, and the Bonds That Remain

Editorial note: the image is illustrative, not documentary photography of the incident.

A cat, a grave, and a difficult question

In November 2016, a story from Central Java, Indonesia, drew international attention. A cat was repeatedly seen on or beside the grave of her former caregiver. A local man, Keli Keningau Prayitno, said he tried to take her in, but she kept returning to the same place. News accounts also described her leaving to eat at her former home before going back to the cemetery. The behavior reportedly continued for roughly a year.

What we know — and what we do not

These details come from news coverage and local eyewitness accounts, not from a longitudinal scientific study. The cat’s name was not consistently established in the sources consulted, and the complete sequence of events cannot be reconstructed with certainty. The episode was reported as a real occurrence, but claims such as “she knew her caregiver was buried there” or “she stayed out of love” remain interpretations rather than demonstrated findings.

Do cats form bonds with people?

Yes. In 2019, Vitale and colleagues studied attachment-related behavior in domestic cats and their human caregivers. Their findings identified patterns consistent with secure attachment in some of the cats studied. This challenges the assumption that cats associate with humans solely for food. Attachment, however, does not mean that every cat responds to separation in the same way.

What research says about feline grief

In 2024, Greene and Vonk surveyed caregivers about changes in cats following the death of another household animal. Respondents reported changes in appetite, vocalization, attention seeking, and other activities. Associations with the closeness of the animals’ relationship were consistent with responses to social loss. Yet caregiver questionnaires are subject to human interpretation and cannot directly reveal a cat’s subjective experience. Importantly, this study concerned the death of another animal, not specifically a human caregiver.

Why did she return to the grave?

Several explanations are plausible: familiarity with the territory, learned routines, scents associated with the location, a sense of safety, and the effects of losing an important person. These possibilities can coexist. Without systematic observations before and after the death, we cannot identify a single cause for the Indonesian cat’s behavior or claim that she understood death as humans do.

Helping a cat after a loss

When a cat loses a person or another animal, predictable routines, safe hiding places, opportunities for play, and social contact on the cat’s terms may help. Monitor persistent changes in eating, drinking, sleep, and behavior. A cat that stops eating or shows signs of illness needs veterinary attention; such signs should not automatically be attributed to sadness.

A story that stays with us

We may never know what the cat experienced when she returned to the grave. The accounts describe repeated behavior that moved people around the world. Science helps us appreciate the significance of feline bonds without claiming certainty about thoughts we cannot observe. Respecting cats’ emotional complexity also means respecting the limits of our knowledge.
