Cats and Earthquakes: The Science Behind Enkaz's Survival

129 hours under the rubble

On February 6, 2023, two successive earthquakes of magnitudes 7.8 and 7.7 devastated southern Turkey and northern Syria. More than 41,000 people died. Entire cities were reduced to rubble. On the 9th day of search and rescue operations, amid the ruins of Nurdagi in Gaziantep province, firefighter Ali Cakas found a black and white cat still alive inside the collapsed remains of an apartment building.

The animal had spent more than 129 hours trapped in the debris without food or water. When rescued, it did not run. It climbed onto Cakas's shoulder and stayed there. The firefighters named it Enkaz, the Turkish word for rubble or debris. "We wanted the name to serve as a memory," the firefighter explained.

The story moved the world. But the question science asks is different from the one social media asks: how did Enkaz survive? And does some biological capacity in the feline body make it simply resistant to what earthquakes do to structures and living things?

Can cats sense earthquakes? What the science says

Accounts of animals displaying anomalous behavior before earthquakes have been documented for millennia, from ancient Greece to feudal Japan. Over the past fifty years, researchers have begun investigating these accounts with scientific methodology.

The most robust hypothesis involves P-waves, or primary seismic waves. When the Earth's crust ruptures, it generates two main types of waves: P-waves, which travel faster and compress the ground, and S-waves and surface waves, which arrive later and produce the destructive shaking humans perceive. The interval between them can range from seconds to a few minutes depending on the distance from the epicenter.

Studies indicate that cats are highly sensitive to infrasonic vibrations, detecting frequencies far below the human threshold of perception. This sensitivity, combined with whiskers that function as air pressure sensors and paws that detect microvibrations in the ground, may allow a cat to register the arrival of P-waves before the main tremor is perceptible to humans.

A second line of investigation involves the geomagnetic field. Earthquakes locally alter the Earth's magnetic field moments before the main event, possibly through a piezoelectric effect in rocks under pressure. Researchers such as Joseph Kirschvink have identified magnetite crystals in the nervous tissue of various animals, including felines — structures that would function as biological compasses sensitive to variations in the geomagnetic field.

Neither of these hypotheses has been definitively proven. But the accumulated evidence is sufficient for the USGS to maintain an active research area on animal behavior and seismic precursors since the 1970s.

The feline body: an engineering of survival

Even if Enkaz did not sense the earthquake, the question of how it survived 129 hours in the rubble has a clear anatomical answer: cats are biologically designed to survive in confined spaces.

The most relevant element is the clavicle. In humans, the clavicle is a long rigid bone connecting the shoulder to the sternum, physically limiting shoulder width. In cats, the clavicle is a vestigial bone that does not articulate with the sternum — it floats freely within the musculature. This allows the shoulders to compress laterally and independently, enabling the cat to pass through any opening larger than its own head.

The feline spine complements this capacity. With 30 vertebrae compared to 26 in humans, and highly elastic intervertebral discs, a cat's spine allows exceptional curvature and rotation. The ligaments connecting the vertebrae are looser than in most mammals, providing flexibility that allows the animal to twist its body at angles impossible for other vertebrates of similar size.

Another factor is the righting reflex, popularly known as "cats always land on their feet." The feline vestibular system, located in the inner ear, is extraordinarily sensitive to spatial orientation. In free fall, the cat initiates a coordinated rotation that places its paws downward in fractions of a second, even from very low heights. In the rubble of a collapsing building, where structure fails in cascades, this ability to quickly reorient the body may be the difference between being crushed and finding a protective position.

Finally, the feline metabolism under extreme scarcity is remarkably resilient. Cats under forced rest and without water intake can survive for significantly longer periods than humans under the same conditions, partly due to highly efficient renal mechanisms for urine concentration and the capacity for mild metabolic torpor under severe stress.

Enkaz and Ali Cakas: what happened next

Ali Cakas formally adopted Enkaz. The two now live in Mardin, southern Turkey. The firefighter created a joint Instagram profile that accumulated more than 50,000 followers. In interviews, Cakas described the cat as "gentle and extremely intelligent" and said Enkaz never lost the habit of climbing onto his lap or shoulder whenever he is home.

The Mardin Fire Department informally adopted Enkaz as its mascot. The story was covered by international agencies including Reuters and AP, and was cited in scientific discussions about the role of animals as indicators of seismic risk.

The name the firefighters chose turned out to be ironic: Enkaz means rubble, what is left after destruction. But the cat became a symbol of the opposite — of what persists, of what survives, of what destruction could not erase.

Biology, not luck

Popular narrative frames stories like Enkaz's as miracles or as proof of some mystical connection between cats and hidden forces of nature. Science offers a more interesting explanation: cats are the product of ten thousand years of coevolution with human environments and millions of years of evolutionary pressure in habitats where fitting into narrow spaces and detecting predators before seeing them was a matter of life and death.

Enkaz did not have exceptional luck. It had exceptional biology, shaped by an evolutionary history far longer than any earthquake.
