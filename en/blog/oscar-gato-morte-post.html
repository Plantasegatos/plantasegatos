Oscar, the Cat Who Sensed Death

He was not a famous cat. He had no special scientific name, belonged to no exotic breed, performed no tricks. He was a medium-hair black and white cat, rescued as a kitten in 2005 and brought to live at the Steere House Nursing and Rehabilitation Center in Providence, Rhode Island — a palliative care home for patients with advanced dementia and terminal illness.

Within months, the medical staff began noticing something unsettling: Oscar appeared in the right rooms. He would lie beside specific patients. A few hours later, that patient would die.

Not once. Not ten times. More than one hundred times, with a consistency that left doctors and nurses without explanation.

The Case Reaches Science

In 2007, geriatrician Dr. David M. Dosa published the case in the New England Journal of Medicine — one of the most respected and rigorous medical journals in the world. Oscar had moved from hallway gossip to the pages of formal science.

The article described Oscar's behavior in detail: he would walk the wards, sniff rooms, and choose a patient. When this happened, the staff had learned to notify families. Oscar had become, involuntarily, an instrument of palliative care — allowing families to arrive in time to say goodbye.

In 2010, Dosa expanded his observations in the book Making the Rounds with Oscar: The Extraordinary Gift of an Ordinary Cat, published by Hyperion, detailing years of living alongside the cat and exploring the impact of animal behavior in end-of-life contexts.

Theory 1 — The Scent of Death

The most widely accepted scientific explanation involves chemistry. When cells collapse and organs begin to fail, a process called autolysis occurs — progressive cellular death. During this process, cells release volatile organic compounds, including ketones and other specific biochemical substances.

The human nose cannot detect these compounds at low concentrations. The feline sense of smell, however, is estimated to be 14 times more sensitive than a human's, with approximately 200 million olfactory receptors. Oscar likely associated this specific odor with the state of stillness and end of life, developing a conditioned behavior over time.

This theory is supported by the fact that Oscar ignored seriously ill patients who would recover — and chose with precision those in irreversible decline.

Theory 2 — The Physics of Comfort

A more pragmatic hypothesis suggests Oscar was simply seeking warmth and quiet. Terminal patients often remain motionless for extended periods, and hospital environments tend to be cold. For a cat, a still patient represents a stable, silent, uninterrupted heat source — ideal conditions for rest.

Under this reading, Oscar was not "predicting" anything — he was being a cat. The coincidence with death would be a natural consequence: the patients he chose were precisely the most immobile ones, meaning the closest to the end.

Theory 3 — Observational Learning

Oscar lived immersed in that environment from kittenhood. Years of living alongside a palliative care team may have taught him to read subtle signals that escape conscious human perception: the change in nursing routine, the different silence of certain rooms, the shift in environmental smells, the more frequent presence of family members.

In this theory, Oscar was not detecting death directly — he had learned to recognize the context of death. A sophisticated associative learning built over years of observation.

What Oscar Really Gave

Regardless of the biological explanation, Oscar's impact was real and documented. He offered presence. To families, he signaled it was time to come. To patients, he offered warmth and companionship in the moments when hallways were empty and rooms were silent.

Steere House formally recognized this. A plaque was installed in his honor reading: "To Oscar, for his compassionate dedication to the care of patients." A cat with institutional recognition — which, in itself, says something about the depth of the bond between felines and human beings in their most vulnerable moments.

Oscar died in 2022, at approximately 17 years of age. The question he left behind still has no complete answer: how do they know?
